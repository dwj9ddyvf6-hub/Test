## Cluster A: Cross-sectional factors, run on our population

### 1. Residual momentum (momentum on idiosyncratic returns)

Mechanism: rank stocks by the momentum of their residual returns after stripping market and factor exposure, rather than by raw price momentum. Blitz, Huij and Martens showed residual momentum has roughly half the volatility of raw momentum with comparable return, because it does not load on the factor swings that cause momentum crashes. The signal is the Sharpe-like ratio of cumulative residual return to residual volatility over the formation window. It is the textbook refinement of the most famous factor, and this estate has never run it in any form: the fleet's momentum sleeves rank raw price.
Why it might differ here: the Focus complex is one correlated thematic cluster, so raw momentum ranks mostly re-rank the cluster's beta against itself. Residual momentum strips exactly that, and what is left is name-specific drift, which is the only part an options book expressing single-name views can actually harvest. On a 30-name single-theme population the raw and residual rankings should diverge far more than in the published broad samples, and the divergence itself is measurable before any trade.
Cheapest first test: formation-only study on the estate's Alpaca daily bar cache for the Focus 30 plus the desk universe file: compute residuals against SPY and SMH from bars already held, print raw-vs-residual rank correlation and the forward 21-session spread of the residual ranking on history we already store. No new data, no chains.
Source: Blitz, Huij, Martens, "Residual Momentum," Journal of Empirical Finance, 2011.

### 2. 52-week high proximity (the George-Hwang anchor)

Mechanism: nearness to the 52-week high predicts future returns positively and subsumes much of raw momentum's power; investors anchor on the high and underreact to good news as price approaches it, so breakouts through the anchor drift instead of exhausting. The classic sort is simply price divided by trailing 52-week high.
Why it might differ here: our names are anchor-saturated. The Focus complex lives near its highs for months at a time and the desk's breakout families already fire on strength, but nobody has measured whether proximity itself, as a continuous cross-sectional variable on our names, carries the drift the paper finds, or whether our population's anchoring is inverted by retail profit-taking at round-number highs. The book also expresses breakouts as debit spreads whose payoff is convex in exactly the drift this variable predicts.
Cheapest first test: daily proximity series for the Focus 30 from the existing bar cache, joined to the Ghost Book and signal-journal fire records already on disk: grade forward 10 and 21-session returns by proximity quintile, and separately grade our own breakout fires by the proximity at fire time. One join over files that exist.
Source: George, Hwang, "The 52-Week High and Momentum Investing," Journal of Finance, 2004.

### 3. Cross-sectional value in its plainest form (earnings yield and book-to-market)

Mechanism: the original Fama-French sort. Cheap stocks on book-to-market and earnings yield outperform glamour stocks over multi-year horizons, compensation for distress risk or systematic over-extrapolation of growth. It is the most replicated cross-sectional result in finance and the estate has never run a value sort on anything.
Why it might differ here: our population is the glamour end of the market by construction. A value sort inside the Focus 30 does not find railroads and banks; it finds the relatively forgotten name inside a hot complex (the laggard chip, the out-of-favor infrastructure name), which is a different animal from the published deep-value tail: relative value inside a momentum complex, where the cheap name carries the complex's beta with a re-rating option. Whether that animal drifts up or is a value trap with a theme is an empirical question our own bars and filings can answer.
Cheapest first test: pull book equity and earnings for the Focus 30 and the wider desk universe from SEC EDGAR companyfacts (free, and the estate already works EDGAR in other lanes), form earnings-yield ranks, and grade forward returns from the existing bar cache. Data cost is one API pull per name.
Source: Fama, French, "The Cross-Section of Expected Stock Returns," Journal of Finance, 1992.

### 4. Quality: gross profitability with a Piotroski screen

Mechanism: Novy-Marx showed gross profits to assets predicts returns about as powerfully as book-to-market, and the profitable end is the growth end, not the distressed end. Piotroski's F-score, nine binary accounting checks, separates winners from losers inside any cheap or profitable cohort. The estate screens on price, volume, and vol; it has never screened on the income statement.
Why it might differ here: several Focus names are pre-profit or thin-margin by design (the space, storage, and energy-transition names), so a quality sort on this population is not a mild tilt, it is a near-binary split of the basket into funded compounders and story names. If quality carries its published premium even inside a speculative complex, the split should show it fast; if the complex's returns are pure narrative beta, quality will sort nothing, which is itself a finding about what we own.
Cheapest first test: EDGAR companyfacts pull for gross profit, assets, and the nine Piotroski inputs for the Focus 30; rank, and grade forward 63-session returns off the bar cache. Same pull infrastructure as concept 3, shared cost.
Source: Novy-Marx, "The Other Side of Value: The Gross Profitability Premium," Journal of Financial Economics, 2013; Piotroski, "Value Investing: The Use of Historical Financial Statement Information," Journal of Accounting Research, 2000.

### 5. The accruals anomaly

Mechanism: Sloan's result: the accrual component of earnings is less persistent than the cash-flow component, investors fixate on headline earnings, and high-accrual firms underperform low-accrual firms for a year after the filing. It is a pure accounting-information inefficiency, orthogonal to every price-based signal this estate runs.
Why it might differ here: our complex reports some of the street's most aggressive accrual profiles (stock compensation, capitalized costs, contract assets in the AI infrastructure buildout). The published anomaly was measured on ordinary industrials; on names where accruals are the story, the spread between cash earners and accrual earners may be wider, and it resolves on filing dates, which our earnings archive already timestamps. It also gives the book a short-side cross-sectional signal that is not a price pattern, a species the fleet almost entirely lacks.
Cheapest first test: EDGAR companyfacts for operating accruals (income minus operating cash flow, scaled by assets) across the desk universe, one pull per name per year, graded against forward returns from the bar cache around filing dates already in the terminal's earnings records.
Source: Sloan, "Do Stock Prices Fully Reflect Information in Accruals and Cash Flows About Future Earnings?" The Accounting Review, 1996.

### 6. Net stock issuance

Mechanism: firms that issue shares underperform, firms that repurchase outperform, over the following year; managers time issuance to overvaluation. Pontiff and Woodgate's composite issuance measure is one of the strongest accounting anomalies by t-statistic. It is distinct from Lane 1 #33, which tests buyback blackout windows and executed repurchase pace from 10-Q tables; this is the classic cross-sectional issuance sort, including the issuance side Lane 1 does not touch.
Why it might differ here: the Focus complex funds itself with equity. ATM programs, converts, and secondary offerings are routine in exactly our names, and each announcement is a dated, public, machine-readable event. In the published sample issuance is a slow annual sort; here it is a frequent event stream on a small population, which makes it testable at event horizon instead of annual horizon, against our own archive.
Cheapest first test: EDGAR filings pull for share-count change (shares outstanding across 10-Qs, same companyfacts pull as concepts 3 to 5) plus offering prospectus dates from EDGAR full-text for the Focus 30; grade post-issuance drift on the bar cache.
Source: Pontiff, Woodgate, "Share Issuance and Cross-Sectional Returns," Journal of Finance, 2008.

### 7. Low volatility and betting against beta, inside the basket

Mechanism: low-beta, low-volatility stocks earn higher risk-adjusted returns than high-beta stocks, flatly contradicting the CAPM; Frazzini and Pedersen's betting-against-beta factor monetizes it by levering the low-beta side and de-levering the high-beta side. The estate trades volatility constantly and has never once sorted on it cross-sectionally. Adjacent machinery exists (vol-targeted sleeve variants size positions by vol), but sizing by vol is not the anomaly; the anomaly is that the low-vol names themselves outperform per unit of risk.
Why it might differ here: inside a high-beta complex, the low-vol quintile is still high-beta by market standards, so the test is whether the effect is relative (works within any population) or absolute (needs genuinely sleepy names). Our session structure adds a second cut: Lane 3's decomposition found the estate's returns concentrate overnight and in the open drive, and low-vol names may carry a different segment profile than the complex's rockets, which changes which expression (shares, debit spreads, credit spreads) harvests them.
Cheapest first test: trailing beta and realized vol for the Focus 30 and desk universe from the bar cache, quintile forward returns and Sharpe by quintile, split by session segment using the 1-minute cache Lane 3 already built. All data on disk.
Source: Frazzini, Pedersen, "Betting Against Beta," Journal of Financial Economics, 2014.

### 8. The idiosyncratic volatility anomaly

Mechanism: Ang, Hodrick, Xing and Zhang: stocks with high idiosyncratic volatility earn abnormally LOW subsequent returns, about 1 percent a month between extreme quintiles in the US sample, one of the largest and most embarrassing anomalies for standard theory. The estate has never computed IVOL for anything.
Why it might differ here: our entire hunting ground is the high-IVOL tail of the market. If the anomaly holds inside the tail itself, then within our population the relatively-lower-IVOL names should systematically beat the wildest names per unit of time, which would reorder sleeve universes and card selection estate-wide. If it inverts here (because our high-IVOL names are high for event reasons, earnings and contracts, rather than lottery reasons), that inversion is a property of our population nobody has documented.
Cheapest first test: residual vol from a one-factor model on the bar cache for the full desk universe, quintile sorts on forward returns, with earnings-window days flagged from the earnings archive to separate event IVOL from ambient IVOL. Files all exist.
Source: Ang, Hodrick, Xing, Zhang, "The Cross-Section of Volatility and Expected Returns," Journal of Finance, 2006.

### 9. The MAX lottery effect

Mechanism: Bali, Cakici and Whitelaw: stocks with the highest single-day return in the prior month (MAX) underperform subsequently; investors overpay for lottery-like payoffs. It is related to IVOL but distinct: it prices the salient extreme day, not ambient variance.
Why it might differ here: Focus names print extreme days constantly, and the desk's own records contain the behavioral mirror: our sleeves chase those days. MAX gives a dated, per-name lottery stamp that can be joined directly to our fire records to ask whether the fleet systematically buys lottery stamps at their worst moment. That is a question about us, answerable only on our data, and no other concept in any lane asks it in this exact form.
Cheapest first test: MAX computed from the bar cache for the desk universe; two joins: forward returns by MAX quintile, and the Ghost Book / journal fire rate by the fire-day's MAX rank. Both joins run on files already in the estate.
Source: Bali, Cakici, Whitelaw, "Maxing Out: Stocks as Lotteries and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns," Journal of Financial Economics, 2011.

### 10. Amihud illiquidity premium across the wide universe

Mechanism: expected returns rise with illiquidity, measured cheaply by Amihud's ratio of absolute return to dollar volume. The premium is compensation for trading costs and inventory risk. The estate applies liquidity only as an exclusion gate (and the owner overruled thinness exclusions in September); it has never measured illiquidity as a return variable.
Why it might differ here: the wide desk universe (339 sleeves' underlyings plus the md2 catalog names) reaches far down the liquidity curve where the published premium lives, while the Focus 30 sits at the liquid extreme. A single Amihud sort across both populations tests whether the premium is monotone across the whole range we actually trade, and it prices the September ruling: if thin names carry a real premium, the ruling harvested something; if they carry only costs, the sort shows that too.
Cheapest first test: Amihud ratios from daily bars and volume already in the Alpaca cache for every name with 200+ bars, forward-return grading by decile, with quoted-spread estimates from the terminal chain snapshots where they exist to separate premium from cost. No new feeds.
Source: Amihud, "Illiquidity and Stock Returns: Cross-Section and Time-Series Effects," Journal of Financial Markets, 2002.

## Cluster B: Time-series and textbook technical forms

### 11. Short-term reversal, the plain one-week and one-month forms

Mechanism: Jegadeesh's reversal: last week's and last month's losers outperform winners over the next week and month, compensation for liquidity provision and overreaction correction. It is the oldest time-series anomaly in the textbook and the fleet, which is built overwhelmingly from momentum and breakout parts, has never run its mirror image as a standalone cross-sectional program (the RSI dip recipes D3 and E3 are index-level, condition-stacked variants, not the plain cross-sectional sort).
Why it might differ here: in a retail-crowded complex, weekly reversals are plausibly stronger (overreaction is the local fuel) but also more dangerous, because our losers are often losers for fundamental event reasons. The estate's earnings archive lets the test split reversal candidates into event-driven and unexplained, a cut the published samples rarely make and the one that decides whether reversal here is harvestable drift or a falling-knife census.
Cheapest first test: weekly formation sorts on the bar cache for the desk universe, forward 5-session returns, split by whether the formation week contained an earnings print per the earnings archive (priced_moves.json and realised_moves.json, 206 rows). All on disk.
Source: Jegadeesh, "Evidence of Predictable Behavior of Security Returns," Journal of Finance, 1990; Lehmann, "Fads, Martingales, and Market Efficiency," Quarterly Journal of Economics, 1990.

### 12. Volatility-managed momentum (the crash overlay)

Mechanism: Barroso and Santa-Clara, and Moreira and Muir's generalization: scaling momentum exposure inversely to its recent realized volatility roughly doubles its Sharpe and largely removes the crash tail, because momentum volatility spikes before its crashes. The desk's momentum sleeves run unmanaged; the overlay is a one-line sizing rule on top of a signal we already produce, and it has never been tested on our sleeves' own return streams.
Why it might differ here: our momentum exposure is concentrated in one complex, so its crash signature is the complex's crash signature, sharper and faster than the diversified published factor. The overlay's benefit should be correspondingly larger or the timing correspondingly worse; either way the estate's own sleeve journals contain the exact return streams to replay it on, which no outside sample can replicate.
Cheapest first test: pure replay. Take the desk momentum and ts_momentum_decile sleeves' journaled daily returns from the desk tree, apply the inverse-vol scaling rule in a spreadsheet-grade script, compare Sharpe and worst 21 sessions against the unmanaged stream. Zero new data.
Source: Barroso, Santa-Clara, "Momentum Has Its Moments," Journal of Financial Economics, 2015; Moreira, Muir, "Volatility-Managed Portfolios," Journal of Finance, 2017.

### 13. Dual momentum across the index ETFs (absolute plus relative)

Mechanism: Antonacci's combination: hold the relatively strongest of SPY, QQQ, IWM only when its absolute trend (price above its long moving average, or trailing return above cash) is positive, else sit in cash or the defensive asset. It is the most widely followed systematic timing design in the practitioner canon. Adjacent machinery exists (ts_momentum_decile ranks single names), but the index-level dual rule as a book-level allocation and expression program has never been run here.
Why it might differ here: the book's problem is not stock selection, it is complex-level drawdown: when the AI complex breaks, everything we own breaks together. An index dual-momentum overlay expressed in the book's native instruments (index verticals and collars on the leading ETF, cash otherwise) is a hedge against our actual concentration, and its whipsaw cost in a V-shaped complex tape is the open question our own session data can price.
Cheapest first test: rules replay on SPY, QQQ, IWM daily bars already in the cache back to 2018 (the same history the overnight study used), grading the overlay against buy-and-hold and against the book's actual weekly returns in the ledger. Bars and ledger both on disk.
Source: Antonacci, "Dual Momentum Investing," McGraw-Hill, 2014; Faber, "A Quantitative Approach to Tactical Asset Allocation," Journal of Wealth Management, 2007.

### 14. The plain dual moving-average trend filter on single names

Mechanism: the golden-cross family in its most literal textbook form: own (or express long) only while the 50-day average sits above the 200-day, stand aside or express short-premium otherwise. It is the first strategy every textbook teaches and, precisely because every sleeve here uses fancier triggers, the unadorned form has never been isolated on our names. D2 tests an SMA50 slope gate on breakout fires; this is the separate classic question of the 50/200 state as a holding filter on the names themselves.
Why it might differ here: Focus names spend long stretches in violent uptrends punctuated by 30 percent air pockets that do not break the 200-day. The filter's published value comes from avoiding secular declines; here its value would come from a different place, state-dependent expression choice (debit structures above the filter, credit structures or nothing below it), which is a book-specific use the literature never tests because the literature holds shares.
Cheapest first test: state series for the Focus 30 from the bar cache, joined to the book's own card and fill history in the ledger: grade realized card outcomes by the name's filter state at signing. The join is the test; the data is our journal.
Source: Brock, Lakonishok, LeBaron, "Simple Technical Trading Rules and the Stochastic Properties of Stock Returns," Journal of Finance, 1992.

### 15. Individual-stock return seasonality (the Heston-Sadka effect)

Mechanism: individual stocks show significant returns in the same calendar month across years, persisting up to five years out, a seasonal pattern in single names distinct from the January effect and from industry seasonality. It is in every anomaly survey and nobody trades it, which is exactly the profile this lane exists for.
Why it might differ here: our names have violent idiosyncratic calendars (product cycles, annual conferences, contract seasons, the November AI-complex rhythm this estate lives by). A same-month effect measured on a 30-name thematic basket over the 2018 to 2026 window our cache holds could be substantially a real business-calendar effect rather than a statistical ghost, and it would express naturally as a month-aware tilt on card timing rather than a standalone trade.
Cheapest first test: month-of-year return matrix for every Focus name from the bar cache, same-month forward grading with the earnings archive flagging print months so the seasonal signal is not just earnings seasonality in disguise. One script over existing files.
Source: Heston, Sadka, "Seasonality in the Cross-Section of Stock Returns," Journal of Financial Economics, 2008.

### 16. Opening range breakout in its plain form, on index underlyings

Mechanism: the textbook ORB: define the first 30 minutes' range on SPY and QQQ, trade the first close beyond it in the direction of the break, invalidation at the range midpoint, hold to the close. Practitioner canon for decades, formalized in recent years by Zarattini and Aziz with strong reported results on QQQ. The book's intraday work is F21's vol-release credit spreads, a different animal; the plain directional ORB has never been registered here. (Desk-roster overlap must be confirmed at intake; the 339 roster is thick with intraday sleeves and this concept yields to any exact desk duplicate.)
Why it might differ here: our expression is not shares or futures but defined-risk verticals priced off the live TastyTrade chain, and the ORB's edge, if real, concentrates in the first hour's repricing, which is also where our chain snapshots are densest. The estate also owns the session-segment decomposition from Lane 3 (open drive positive, midday negative), which predicts in advance where an ORB hold-to-close bleeds and where a hold-to-midday variant would not, a refinement the published share-form studies do not make.
Cheapest first test: replay on the 1-minute bar cache Lane 3 already assembled (21 symbols, 14 sessions is too thin; extend the same pull to SPY and QQQ over the full cached window), grading breakouts by segment with the estate's own segment boundaries. Data pull is the only cost.
Source: Zarattini, Aziz, "Can Day Trading Really Be Profitable?" SSRN working paper, 2023 (ORB on QQQ); Crabel, "Day Trading with Short Term Price Patterns and Opening Range Breakout," 1990.

## Cluster C: Calendar and event regularities in standard form

### 17. Turn-of-the-month effect

Mechanism: index returns concentrate in the window from the last trading day of the month through the first three of the next, attributed to pension and salary flows, window dressing settlement, and liquidity cycles. Documented since Ariel and Lakonishok-Smidt, persistent across decades and countries. Lane 1 #26 carries pre-holiday and January effects; the monthly turn is a separate, stronger regularity and appears nowhere in the estate's registers.
Why it might differ here: our book's rhythm is monthly in a way the index literature's is not: options cards, expiry cycles, and the sleeve fleet's monthly rebalance families (the aster_etf monthly sleeves) all turn over at month boundaries, so the estate is itself a small flow at the turn. Testing the effect on SPY and QQQ and simultaneously on the Focus basket tells us whether the turn premium lives in the broad tape, in our complex, or in neither, and the expression (short-dated index verticals over a four-day window) is the cheapest defined-risk form the book owns.
Cheapest first test: pure bar study: SPY, QQQ, and equal-weight Focus basket returns inside vs outside the turn window over the full 2018 to 2026 cache, with the overnight/intraday split applied, since our one proven edge lives in the overnight segment and the turn effect may too. No new data.
Source: Lakonishok, Smidt, "Are Seasonal Anomalies Real? A Ninety-Year Perspective," Review of Financial Studies, 1988; McConnell, Xu, "Equity Returns at the Turn of the Month," Financial Analysts Journal, 2008.

### 18. Pre-FOMC announcement drift

Mechanism: Lucca and Moench: the S&P 500 earns a large share of its annual excess return in the 24 hours before scheduled FOMC announcements, beginning after the morning of the prior day, with no commensurate volatility. It is one of the most robust event regularities ever published, by Federal Reserve staff, and it is not in any estate register (Lane 1 #25 is prediction-market divergence on macro prints, a different mechanism).
Why it might differ here: the drift is an index-level equity phenomenon; the book would express it in index options, where the pre-FOMC window also carries a known implied-vol ramp into the print and crush after it. Whether the equity drift survives translation into a defined-risk options expression, after paying the ramp, is a question about our expression layer, not about the drift, and it is exactly the kind of translation test this book exists to run. The Focus basket's beta to the drift is a second free measurement.
Cheapest first test: FOMC dates are public and few (eight a year, about 70 in the cache window): grade SPY and QQQ returns in the pre-announcement window from the bar cache, split overnight vs intraday, then shadow-price the standard vertical expression off the terminal's chain snapshots for the meetings inside snapshot coverage. Bars first, chains second, both in the estate.
Source: Lucca, Moench, "The Pre-FOMC Announcement Drift," Journal of Finance, 2015.

### 19. The Halloween indicator (Sell in May) as a regime tilt

Mechanism: Bouman and Jacobsen: returns in November to April dominate May to October across 37 of 39 countries studied, one of the most persistent calendar effects in the literature. Lane 1 #26 covers January and pre-holiday effects; the six-month seasonal split is distinct and unregistered here.
Why it might differ here: nobody proposes this book sit out six months; the testable form is a tilt: does the Focus complex, whose business calendar is heavily second-half (product launches, holiday capex, the November earnings gauntlet), show the standard split, its inverse, or none? The answer prices a real recurring decision the book already makes implicitly, how aggressively to run the summer card slate, and our eight years of cached bars cover eight full seasonal cycles on the exact names.
Cheapest first test: seasonal split of Focus basket and SPY returns over the cache window, by segment (overnight vs intraday), one script. If the split is absent in both segments, the concept retires itself at zero cost, which is the point of testing classics cheaply.
Source: Bouman, Jacobsen, "The Halloween Indicator, 'Sell in May and Go Away': Another Puzzle," American Economic Review, 2002.

### 20. Monthly options expiration week effect

Mechanism: index returns in expiration week have historically exceeded off-week returns (documented across the Stivers-Sun and earlier op-ex literatures), plausibly from dealer hedging flows and pin-related buying into the monthly settlement. The terminal watches expiry pins as a dealer tool, but the week-level return regularity as a tradable calendar program has never been tested here.
Why it might differ here: the modern expiration calendar is not the literature's: weekly and daily expirations have dissolved the single monthly opex into a continuous surface, so the classic effect may have migrated (into Fridays, into 0DTE settlement days) rather than died. Our chain snapshot archive is precisely the dataset that can see where it went, because it records the surface day by day instead of assuming the 1990s calendar.
Cheapest first test: bar study first: SPY and QQQ returns by expiration-week vs off-week and by weekday-with-expiry vs without, over the cache window; then the chain snapshot archive grades whether any surviving pocket coincides with measurable pin or gamma structure. Bars and snapshots both held.
Source: Stivers, Sun, "Stock Market Uncertainty and the Relation Between Stock and Bond Returns" and related op-ex week literature; Ni, Pearson, Poteshman, "Stock Price Clustering on Option Expiration Dates," Journal of Financial Economics, 2005 (the pinning half of the mechanism).

## Cluster D: Pairs and statistical arbitrage basics

### 21. Cointegration pairs inside the Focus complex

Mechanism: Gatev, Goetzmann and Rouwenhorst pairs trading: find stocks whose prices are cointegrated, trade the spread when it diverges beyond two standard deviations, exit on convergence. The foundational stat-arb paper, run on broad universes. The estate has never run a pairs program of any kind; the fleet is entirely directional.
Why it might differ here: the Focus complex is a set of natural economic pairs the broad-universe literature never isolates: NVDA against AMD, AVGO against MRVL, WDC against STX-class storage exposure, the two contract manufacturers, the AI power names against each other. These pairs share factor exposure so tightly that their spreads may be far more stationary than random large-cap pairs, and when they do diverge it is usually a dated, name-specific event (a contract win, a guide-down) whose nature the book's gestalt reads are built to judge. A pairs sleeve here is market-neutral inside the complex, the one posture the entire estate lacks.
Cheapest first test: Engle-Granger and Johansen screens over the Focus 30's cached daily bars, spread half-life table, then a rules replay of the classic two-sigma form on the surviving pairs. One data pull, all ours.
Source: Gatev, Goetzmann, Rouwenhorst, "Pairs Trading: Performance of a Relative-Value Arbitrage Rule," Review of Financial Studies, 2006.

### 22. PCA residual statistical arbitrage (the Avellaneda-Lee form)

Mechanism: extract statistical factors by PCA from a stock universe's returns, model each name's residual as an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, and trade residual excursions scored by their s-score, market and factor neutral by construction. It is the canonical industrial stat-arb design, published in full.
Why it might differ here: run on the 339-name desk universe, the first statistical factors will be our complex's own anatomy (the AI beta factor, the crypto factor, the rate-sensitive factor), and the residuals are precisely the name-specific moves our gestalt process reads by eye. The s-score gives the desk's near-miss and watch machinery a neutral signal it has never had: a ranked list of names statistically stretched against their own complex, refreshed daily, which is also a candidate generator for the book's cards even if the full neutral program is never sized.
Cheapest first test: PCA on the cached daily returns of every universe name with 200+ bars, residual s-scores computed for the most recent session, and a backward replay over the cache window grading next-5-session residual reversion by s-score bucket. Compute only, on data held.
Source: Avellaneda, Lee, "Statistical Arbitrage in the US Equities Market," Quantitative Finance, 2010.

### 23. ETF-versus-components dislocation (SMH and SOXX against their baskets)

Mechanism: index arbitrage in its simplest relative-value form: when an ETF trades rich or cheap to the intraday value of its components, or when the ETF's move diverges from the capitalization-weighted move of its top holdings, the gap mean-reverts as authorized participants arbitrage it. The estate trades SMH and SOXX as directional vehicles and has never measured them against their own baskets.
Why it might differ here: SMH and SOXX are Focus names 1 and 2 in the study set, and their top holdings are the rest of the study set, so the estate holds every leg of this trade in its own data already. Semiconductor ETFs also carry a retail and momentum-chasing flow that can push the wrapper away from the basket for hours; whether that dislocation is harvestable at our horizons, or is pure noise inside the spread, is measurable from the 1-minute cache Lane 3 built, extended by the same pull.
Cheapest first test: reconstruct SMH's top-10 basket from Focus names in the 1-minute cache, compute wrapper-minus-basket divergence series, grade reversion by divergence size. The names and the cache are ours; the weights are public and static enough at this horizon.
Source: Engle, Sarkar, "Premiums-Discounts and Exchange Traded Funds," Journal of Derivatives, 2006; Ben-David, Franzoni, Moussawi, "Do ETFs Increase Volatility?" Journal of Finance, 2018 (the flow mechanism).

## Cluster E: Information regularities in standard form

### 24. Analyst revision momentum

Mechanism: Chan, Jegadeesh and Lakonishok: revisions to analyst earnings estimates, and especially the breadth of revisions (upgrades minus downgrades across covering analysts), predict returns for months, separate from price momentum and from the earnings surprise itself. It is a first-rank textbook anomaly. The estate's earnings machinery is built entirely on implied-versus-realised moves; the revision stream has never been wired as a signal here.
Why it might differ here: Focus names carry extreme estimate dispersion and violent revision cycles tied to the AI capex narrative; revision breadth in this complex is a direct read on whether the fundamental story is broadening or narrowing, information our price-based gestalt cannot see. A revision signal would also be the book's first fundamentally-sourced timing input for earnings expressions, upstream of the implied-move work the queue already runs.
Cheapest first test: staged honestly: first a data-availability probe, whether the terminal's earnings records or any estate file carries estimate or revision fields (the archive's 206 priced/realised rows are the first place to look); if absent, the test is a four-week forward collection on the Focus 30 from a free revision source, grading post-revision drift on the bar cache as the sample accrues. The concept's first deliverable is the probe result, not a backtest.
Source: Chan, Jegadeesh, Lakonishok, "Momentum Strategies," Journal of Finance, 1996 (revision momentum sections); Stickel, "Predicting Individual Analyst Earnings Forecasts," Journal of Accounting Research, 1990.

### 25. Open-market insider buying

Mechanism: Lakonishok and Lee: stocks with heavy net open-market buying by corporate insiders outperform, and the signal strengthens for small, value, and contrarian names; insider selling is weak (it is mostly diversification), buying is the informative leg because it is voluntary and concentrated. The estate's record shows an insider feed attempt that was refused at the source; the classic signal itself has never been tested on our population.
Why it might differ here: insider buying in high-multiple growth names is rare and therefore loud: a Focus-complex officer buying open-market at these valuations is a stronger statement than the same act at a sleepy industrial, and the complex's officers more often sell. The asymmetry is the test. Form 4 filings are free, timestamped, and machine-readable, so the signal can be built at disclosure-date honesty (filed date, not transaction date) from the same EDGAR infrastructure other lanes already use.
Cheapest first test: EDGAR Form 4 pull for the Focus 30 and desk universe over the cache window, open-market purchase codes only, graded at filed date against forward returns from the bar cache. One new parser over a free feed.
Source: Lakonishok, Lee, "Are Insider Trades Informative?" Review of Financial Studies, 2001.

### 26. Dividend initiation and omission drift

Mechanism: Michaely, Thaler and Womack: dividend initiations are followed by positive drift and omissions by severe negative drift, far beyond the announcement-day move; the market underreacts to what payout changes signal about permanent cash flows. The estate has never traded a payout event.
Why it might differ here: payout events in the Focus complex are rare and getting less rare in an interesting direction: the mature end of the complex (the big chip and infrastructure names) is initiating and raising payouts as capex matures, while the speculative end omits nothing because it never paid. An initiation inside our basket is a regime statement about a name we hold other views on, and the drift window (weeks) matches the book's card horizons and its vertical expressions naturally.
Cheapest first test: event census first: every initiation, omission, and special dividend among the Focus 30 and desk universe across the cache window, from public dividend histories joined to the bar cache; if the census is too thin to grade, that count is the finding and the concept waits for events rather than borrowing significance from outside samples.
Source: Michaely, Thaler, Womack, "Price Reactions to Dividend Initiations and Omissions," Journal of Finance, 1995.

### 27. Buyback announcement drift (the event form)

Mechanism: Ikenberry, Lakonishok and Vermaelen: firms announcing open-market repurchases earn abnormal returns of about 12 percent over the following years, concentrated in value names; the announcement signals undervaluation. This is the announcement-event classic. Lane 1 #33 carries the plumbing form (blackout windows and executed pace from 10-Q repurchase tables); the announcement drift itself, and the estate's reaction discipline around it, is untested here.
Why it might differ here: our names announce buybacks constantly and the announcements are frequently defensive, timed against drawdowns and dilution from stock compensation, which the classic sample's were not. Whether announcement drift exists in the complex, or whether the modern complex announcement is fully priced noise (or worse, a negative tell when it substitutes for capex credibility), is decidable from our own event history, and the answer feeds the gestalt reads that currently treat buyback headlines as unexamined color.
Cheapest first test: announcement census for the desk universe from EDGAR 8-K filings (the estate already parses EDGAR in other lanes), announcement-date returns and 21/63-session drift from the bar cache, split by whether the name was in drawdown at announcement, which our bars know.
Source: Ikenberry, Lakonishok, Vermaelen, "Market Underreaction to Open Market Share Repurchases," Journal of Financial Economics, 1995.

### 28. Published short interest, the plain monthly level signal

Mechanism: the textbook form, distinct from Lane 1 #3's lending-fee and utilization spikes: high short interest as a share of float predicts underperformance (Asquith, Pathak, Ritter), and the signal is in the slow published level, not in borrow-market microstructure. FINRA publishes the full file free twice a month. The estate has never ingested it.
Why it might differ here: short interest in Focus names is structurally high and heavily hedged (pairs, converts, market-maker inventory), so the raw level may carry less signal here than anywhere, which is itself worth knowing before any squeeze-adjacent idea borrows credibility from short interest folklore. The discriminating cut our data allows: names where high short interest coincides with our sleeves' long fires (crowded disagreement) versus names where it stands alone.
Cheapest first test: ingest the free FINRA short interest file for the desk universe, two snapshots a month across whatever history FINRA serves, grade forward returns by short-interest quintile from the bar cache, with the sleeve-fire join as the second table. Data cost is zero; the parser is the work.
Source: Asquith, Pathak, Ritter, "Short Interest, Institutional Ownership, and Stock Returns," Journal of Financial Economics, 2005.

## Cluster F: Standard options structures as programs

### 29. Single-name covered calls on Focus names (the BXM logic, brought to our holdings)

Mechanism: the CBOE BuyWrite logic: systematically selling one-month, slightly out-of-the-money calls against held stock converts part of the equity risk premium into premium income, lowers volatility, and underperforms only in sharp rallies. It is the most studied options program in existence, at index level. The book holds Focus shares running to their invalidations and has never run a systematic overwrite on them; overwriting appears only ad hoc, if at all.
Why it might differ here: the index version sells a diversified, low-IV underlying. Ours would sell the highest-IV names in the market, where the premium is fat precisely because the upside tails are real (our names gap 15 percent on prints). Whether overwrite income on this population compensates the capped tails is not answerable from BXM's record; it is answerable from our chain snapshots and the ledger's actual holding history, replaying the overwrite against the positions the book really held, on the days it really held them.
Cheapest first test: replay on the ledger: for every share position the book held at least 21 sessions, price the standard overwrite (30-day, 5 percent OTM call) off the terminal chain snapshots where coverage exists and off TastyTrade chain history otherwise, and compare total return against the unoverwritten holding. Positions and chains are estate assets; no new program is built to ask the question.
Source: Callan Associates / CBOE BXM methodology studies; Whaley, "Understanding the BXM Buy-Write Benchmark," and the CBOE S&P 500 BuyWrite Index (BXM) factsheet record.

### 30. Systematic cash-secured put writing on names we would own (the PUT logic, single-name)

Mechanism: the CBOE PUT index logic: sell one-month at-the-money cash-secured puts, hold to expiry, repeat; historically index-level put writing earned equity-like returns at two-thirds the volatility. Adjacent premium families crowd the registers (F1's index put spreads, G2's 1DTE puts, the book's put-write pricing sheet and G22's decile logging on it), but the classic single-name program, sell the put on a name the book has already graded as ownable at the strike, take assignment as an entry method, has never been registered as a program. Its unit is not premium income; it is discounted acquisition of graded names.
Why it might differ here: index put writing sells diversified crash risk. Single-name put writing on our graded watchlist sells name-specific gap risk on names whose gap distribution the estate measures better than anyone (the earnings archive's realised-move record per name). The wheel's second half, assignment into a name at an effective discount followed by the concept-29 overwrite, closes a full acquisition-and-income cycle that fits the book's probe-and-grade discipline in a way index premium never did.
Cheapest first test: shadow-price the standard cycle on the book's current graded watchlist names off live TastyTrade chains for four weeks (the pricing sheet infrastructure from Lane 5's existing work carries it), grading assignment-adjusted entry cost against simply buying at market on the same days from the bar cache. Pricing only; no fills.
Source: CBOE S&P 500 PutWrite Index (PUT) methodology and performance record; Bondarenko, "Variance Risk Premium" survey sections on put-write returns, 2014.

### 31. The poor man's covered call (LEAPS diagonal) as a standing expression

Mechanism: buy a deep in-the-money long-dated call (the LEAPS leg, delta 0.75 to 0.85, a year or more out) as a capital-light stock substitute, and sell short-dated out-of-the-money calls against it on a monthly cycle. The diagonal's economics are the covered call's, with the basis risk that the long leg is an option: its theta, vega, and strike geometry differ from stock, and the structure lives or dies on managing the long leg's decay against the short leg's income. Sleeve-wave wires use diagonals as one-off expressions on donchian fires; the standing program form, one diagonal per graded long thesis with a written roll discipline, is unregistered.
Why it might differ here: the book's binding constraint is capital efficiency under the Charter's stress-loss fractions, and the diagonal is the classic answer: defined maximum loss equal to the debit, no share capital consumed, and the short leg's income is richest exactly on our high-IV names. The catch the literature understates is assignment and pin risk on the short leg around our names' event gaps, which our own earnings calendar coverage can schedule around, a refinement only an estate with our archive would build in from the start.
Cheapest first test: construct-and-grade on paper: take the book's three longest-held current long theses from the ledger, build the standard diagonal on each off live TastyTrade chains, and shadow-mark all legs daily for one monthly cycle in the expression shadow ledger, grading against the share holding's actual marks. The shadow ledger exists; the chains are live; the theses are ours.
Source: McMillan, "Options as a Strategic Investment," diagonal spread chapters; CBOE and Options Industry Council educational record on LEAPS diagonals (the practitioner canon form).

### 32. Term-structure calendar spreads on the index underlyings (the plain form)

Mechanism: buy the back month, sell the front month at the same strike, harvesting the implied-volatility term differential: front-month implieds overreact to near events and decay faster, so the calendar earns when the term structure normalizes and loses when the front event explodes through the strike. Queue entry 4 mentions calendars only as a long-vol purchase inside its underpriced earnings quintile; the standing term-structure program on SPX and QQQ, entered on differential extremes rather than events, has never been registered.
Why it might differ here: the estate owns an unusual asset for this trade: the terminal's chain snapshot archive, a day-by-day record of the SPX, QQQ, and IWM surfaces, which means the front/back differential's own distribution, seasonality (it breathes with the earnings calendar and FOMC cycle), and normalization speed are measurable from our records rather than assumed. The book's other premium programs are short the front; a calendar book is long the back, and the correlation between those books inside our own portfolio is a diversification fact we can compute, not conjecture.
Cheapest first test: differential study on the snapshot archive alone: build the 30/60-day ATM implied differential series for SPY and QQQ from stored snapshots, mark the standard calendar's theoretical P&L across the series, and report the differential's percentile-conditional forward behavior. No live pricing, no fills; the archive is the test.
Source: McMillan, "Options as a Strategic Investment," calendar spread chapters; Ni, "Trading Volatility Spreads" practitioner literature on term-structure differentials.

### 33. Broken-wing butterfly as the book's standard neutral structure

Mechanism: a butterfly with one wing widened, typically structured for zero or credit cost: long one call, short two calls at the body, long one further call with the far wing skipped wider, so the position carries a directional lean with a defined loss plateau and no naked side. It is the standard defined-risk alternative to the iron condor in the practitioner canon. The book's neutral premium work runs through credit spreads (F21) and the earnings iron fly (F5); the broken-wing form as a standing weekly index program is unregistered, and the desk's iron_condor sleeve is desk-side share-book machinery, not this book's structure.
Why it might differ here: the structure's known weakness is the gap through the body, and our session research says gaps are where this market's returns live (the overnight segment carries the drift). A broken-wing program on QQQ priced at the close and held through the overnight segment is therefore a direct bet against our own best-documented finding, which makes it a genuinely informative test: if the structure still earns after paying the overnight drift on our tape, the premium is real and large; if the drift eats it, we have priced our own edge from the other side, which the book should know either way.
Cheapest first test: shadow pricing only, four weekly cycles: construct the standard broken-wing on QQQ at Friday's close off live TastyTrade chains at executable sides under the standing pricing basis, mark to the following Friday, and print the overnight segment's contribution separately using the bar cache. The pricing basis and ledger already exist from F1's wiring.
Source: McMillan, "Options as a Strategic Investment," butterfly chapters; Tastytrade practitioner research record on broken-wing butterflies (the modern popularizer of the form).

### 34. Scheduled macro-event long volatility (CPI and FOMC straddles in plain form)

Mechanism: buy the at-the-money straddle the session before a scheduled macro release, exit after the release: the classic event-vol trade, earning when the realised release-day move exceeds the implied move the market charged. The book's event machinery is entirely earnings-facing (queue entry 4, F5, the earnings archive); scheduled macro prints, eight FOMC and twelve CPI a year, have never been priced as an event book here, and queue entry 5's VVIX gate is a variance-timing overlay, not the event trade itself.
Why it might differ here: the estate's earnings work produced a per-event implied-versus-realised archive discipline that transfers directly: macro implied moves are extractable from the chain snapshots for the meetings inside coverage, and the realised side is in the bar cache, so the estate can build the macro twin of its earnings archive and ask the only question that matters, whether the surface overcharges or undercharges for scheduled macro risk on our underlyings, SPX and QQQ rather than single names. The pre-FOMC drift (concept 18) is the equity-side cousin; this is the options-side measurement, and the two together price the same window from both sides.
Cheapest first test: archive construction: for every FOMC and CPI date inside chain snapshot coverage, extract the prior session's implied move from the snapshots, join the realised move from the bar cache, print the ratio table. If the table shows systematic undercharge, the trade form follows; if overcharge, the short side is the candidate and it joins the premium families' evidence. Snapshots and bars are both held.
Source: The event-vol practitioner canon (Euan Sinclair, "Volatility Trading," Wiley, 2008, event chapters); Kurov, Sancetta, Strasser, Wolfe on scheduled-announcement implied premiums.

### 35. The textbook protective put program (cost-budgeted, rules-based)

Mechanism: the oldest options program of all: hold the equity exposure, buy a fixed strip of out-of-the-money index puts on a fixed calendar (quarterly, 5 percent OTM, budgeted at a stated percent of net asset value per year), and let everything else run. The book buys convexity opportunistically (Ledger vs. Surface) and at clustered invalidations (queue entry 18), and the desk carries the qqq_put_ladder overlay; the plain, always-on, cost-budgeted program has never been run or priced as a program in this book, so the estate has no honest number for what permanent insurance costs or pays on its own exposure profile.
Why it might differ here: the book's exposure is not the index; it is a concentrated thematic basket whose crash beta to SPY is well above one and whose worst days are name events, not market events. Index puts may therefore be the wrong insurance for our actual loss distribution, and the only way to know is to replay the program against the book's real daily returns in the ledger, including the episodes where the complex fell alone. That replay also prices the alternative (Focus-basket puts) on the same episodes, turning a textbook program into a measurement of our own basis risk.
Cheapest first test: pure replay: apply the standard program's cost (priced off chain snapshots where the window allows, off a stated IV assumption where it does not, with the assumption named) to the ledger's daily book returns since inception, and report the drag and the episode-by-episode payoff against the book's five worst drawdown windows. Ledger and snapshots are on disk.
Source: The protective-put canon: Figlewski, Chidambaran, Kaplan on portfolio insurance performance; CBOE S&P 500 5% Put Protection Index (PPUT) methodology and record.
