### How to read the evidence labels

Throughout: **[documented]** means the practice appears in the trader's own book, letters, or repeated on-record interviews. **[reported]** means credible secondary reporting by Schwager, Zuckerman (*The Man Who Solved the Market*), Sebastian Mallaby (*More Money Than God*), or comparable sources, not confirmed in the trader's own words. **[self-claimed]** means the trader's own unverified account, usually of early or personal-account returns. **[legend]** means a number or story that circulates widely but traces to no checkable source, or that grows in the retelling. The distinction matters because the formation value of a rule does not depend on the legend attached to it: Seykota's "cut losses" is worth learning even if the exact multiple on his early account cannot be audited.

## 1. The honest problem first: survivorship, selection, and lore

Any study of "the greatest traders" begins with a biased sample, and pretending otherwise would corrupt everything after it. Three biases need naming plainly, because each one changes how a lesson should be weighted.

**First, Schwager interviewed winners.** *Market Wizards* (1989) and its successors collect seventeen, then dozens, of exceptional performers. As even sympathetic reviewers note, Schwager "interviewed seventeen winners. He did not interview the thousands of traders with similar methods who blew up and disappeared. Every lesson in the book comes with an invisible asterisk: this worked for the people it worked for" [https://winchellhouse.com/2026/10/03/book-review-market-wizards-by-jack-d-schwager/]. The same criticism is made in academic-adjacent summaries: selection bias and survivor bias are inherent, and self-reported stories carry retrospective rationalisation [https://ww2.jacksonms.gov/libweb/xTVMhH/274025/market__wizards__by-jack__schwager.pdf]. This does not make the corpus useless. It makes it a source of *hypotheses about practice*, not proof of expectancy. A rule repeated independently by a trend follower, a floor options trader, a macro manager, and a stock picker who never met each other is stronger evidence than any one man's track record, because the convergence is unlikely to be an artefact of one lucky run. That convergence test is the organising principle of the closing table in this study.

**Second, the best records are the least checkable.** Medallion's figures come from Zuckerman's reporting and Renaissance's own fee history, not from a public audit a reader can inspect. Seykota's famous account growth and Marcus's $30,000 to $80 million are Schwager-era accounts from an era of personal and pooled commodity accounts with no modern reporting standard. Druckenmiller's "never a down year" at Duquesne is widely corroborated by investors and journalists and was repeated by Druckenmiller himself, but it is a manager-reported record for a fund that returned outside capital in 2010. Livermore's 1929 profits are reconstructed from newspapers and a semi-fictionalised biography. The correct response is neither credulity nor dismissal: it is to learn the *process*, which is described consistently and in detail, and to hold the *numbers* at the label they earned.

**Third, era is an edge that does not transfer.** Floor traders like Tony Saliba and Tom Baldwin traded inside spreads and information lags that no longer exist. Livermore's bucket-shop tape, Marcus's 1970s commodity trends, Robertson's long/short market of the 1980s, and Simons's early stat-arb all ran against slower, less arbitraged competition. Several of the greats say this themselves in later interviews: the pattern that paid them has been competed down, and what survives is not the pattern but the discipline around finding the next one. McLean and Pontiff's finding, documented in the local study, that published stock-return predictors decayed roughly 58 percent from in-sample to post-publication returns, is the academic version of the same warning. A formation study that teaches 1987 tactics as 2026 edges would be lying. One that teaches how each great *found, sized, defended, and retired* edges is teaching the transferable thing.

With that stated once, clearly, it does not need repeating under every name. The sections below flag the specific myths where they attach to specific men.

## 2. Jesse Livermore: the original text, and the original warning

**Sources:** *Reminiscences of a Stock Operator* (1923, Edwin Lefèvre, from interviews with Livermore) and Livermore's own *How to Trade in Stocks* (1940). Full setup detail is in the local study, section 1.1; what follows is the operating core and the part of his story most often skipped.

**Edge as he describes it.** The tape: price and volume as the record of what money is actually doing, read at "pivotal points" where the line of least resistance reveals itself. He did not predict; he waited for precedent to favour a play and then tested it with a first tranche. "What beat me was not having brains enough to stick to my own game, that is, to play the market only when I was satisfied that precedents favored my play." And the most quoted sentence in all of trading literature: "It never was my thinking that made the big money for me. It always was my sitting."

**Sizing and pressing.** Pyramiding in tranches, each addition only after the previous tranche showed a profit and the pivotal point held. The first tranche is explicitly a probe: if it shows a loss, the timing is wrong and he is out, cheaply. Pressing, for Livermore, is therefore never an act of conviction at entry; it is an act the market authorises afterwards, with its own money.

**Cutting.** A hard capital rule in his own book: never sustain a loss of more than 10 percent on a speculation, establish the stop before the trade, and treat "profits take care of themselves but losses never do" as arithmetic, not temperament. He also forbade averaging down in the strongest terms available to him, and forbade trading on tips, using his own cotton disaster, taken on another man's conviction, as the teaching case.

**Drawdowns and psychology.** Here Livermore is the cautionary tale inside his own textbook, and any honest study must say so. He made fortunes in 1907 and 1929 [reported] and went bankrupt repeatedly, including 1915 and finally 1934, and died in 1940. The man who wrote the loss rules broke them under tip influence, overtrading, and personal distress. The formation lesson is not hypocrisy; it is that *knowledge is not a control*. Every great after him who survived built structural controls, stops resting in the market, size limits enforced outside their own discretion in the moment, segregated capital, precisely because Livermore demonstrated what the unaided will does under pressure. He himself segregated family money in trust, in his own account because a trader can lose every cent he can lay his hands on.

**Process, diet, records.** Tape first, news as explanation after the fact. He kept his own records of prices and of his trades and studied his errors; the *Reminiscences* is structured as an error catalogue, each fortune-losing episode mined for the rule it violated. That habit, write the rule when calm, grade the breach rather than the loss, is the ancestor of every journaling practice in section 10 below.

**Repeated rules.** Wait for the pivotal point. Sit tight in a confirmed trend. Trade the leaders of the leading group. Never add to a loser. Never trade on tips. Do not trade every day.

## 3. Ed Seykota and Michael Marcus: the systematic root, and the lineage that produced Kovner

This cluster was the thinnest part of the local study relative to its importance, because almost everything in modern trend following and in Kovner's risk religion descends from it. The lineage is documented and unusually direct: Amos Hostetter at Commodities Corporation mentored Seykota; Seykota mentored Marcus; Marcus mentored Kovner [https://www.turtletrader.com/seykota-marcus-kovner/; https://macro-ops.com/lessons-from-a-trading-great-michael-marcus/].

### 3.1 Ed Seykota: cut losses, ride winners, keep bets small, follow the rules

**Edge as he describes it.** Reactive trend following, systematised. Seykota built one of the first computerised futures trading systems in the early 1970s, adapting Richard Donchian's moving-average ideas and testing them on the slow computers of the era. His own ordering of what matters: "In order of importance to me are: (1) the long term trend, (2) the current chart pattern, and (3) picking a good spot to buy or sell" [https://www.newtraderu.com/2022/05/08/ed-seykota-strategy/]. He is explicit that understanding the market is optional and often counterproductive for a trend trader: when an uptrend happens, the price is moving up; the system reacts, it does not explain. His summary of his own development work, that it is 90 percent data analysis and 10 percent model building, is a research allocation worth copying verbatim.

**His five rules, in his words as collected across his site and the Schwager interview:** (1) Cut losses. (2) Ride winners. (3) Keep bets small. (4) Follow the rules without question. (5) Know when to break the rules. His answer to what the elements of good trading are is the most famous compression in the canon: "(1) cutting losses, (2) cutting losses, and (3) cutting losses. If you can follow these three rules, you may have a chance" [https://synapsetrading.com/trading-tips-ed-seykota/]. And the warning that gives the rule its teeth: "If you can't take a small loss, sooner or later you will take the mother of all losses."

**Sizing.** Conservative to a degree that surprises people who associate trend following with leverage. From his own published guidance: "I intend to risk below 5 percent on a trade, allowing for poor executions"; "Speculate with less than 10 percent of your liquid net worth. Risk less than 1 percent of your speculative account on a trade. This tends to keep the fluctuations in the trading account small, relative to net worth. This is essential as large fluctuations can engage {emotions} and lead to feeling-justifying drama" [https://brameshtechanalysis.com/2015/03/18/ed-seykotas-trading-style-part-ii/]. Note the reasoning: the sizing rule is not primarily statistical. It is psychological engineering. Keep the venture below your threshold of financial importance so that drawdowns do not trigger the emotional "uncle point" that makes a trader abandon a working system at exactly the wrong moment. Pyramiding, in his formulation borrowed from the inscription he liked to quote, means adding smaller and smaller amounts on the way up.

**Cutting and stops.** Stops are set at entry, at the point where the chart "sours," and moved only to lock in profit as the trend continues. He allows himself discretionary profit-taking when a market gets wild, and is candid about why: it usually does not exit better than the trailing stop would, but it reduces portfolio volatility, "which helps calm my nerves. Losing a position is aggravating, whereas losing your nerve is devastating." That sentence deserves to be underlined in any formation curriculum: the protected asset is the nerve, not the position.

**Drawdowns and psychology: the Trading Tribe.** Seykota's most original contribution is not his system; it is his institutionalised treatment of feeling. He founded the Trading Tribe, a peer process in which traders bring a live emotional "hot seat" issue and work it through with the group, on the theory that feelings are information and that a trader who cannot feel a signal cleanly will distort it into drama. His later writing is full of deliberately plain systems language for inner states, and it can read as eccentric. The transferable core is dead serious and matches Kovner (close it if it disturbs your balance), Marcus (protect mental capital), and Druckenmiller (know when you are hot and when you are not): emotional state is a position-sizing input, monitored like any other. Longevity, in his repeated phrase, is the key to success, and longevity is mostly a money-management and self-management achievement: "The key to long-term survival and prosperity has a lot to do with the money management techniques incorporated into the technical system."

**Process and records.** End-of-day, systematic, deliberately inattentive to intraday movement and to news: one of his collected rules is to file the news in the trash. His record-keeping is the system itself: signals taken or not taken, and the equity curve as the verdict. The performance attached to his name, turning $5,000 into roughly $15 million over twelve years in Schwager's account, is **[self-claimed / reported]**: it comes from the *Market Wizards* interview era and is not an audited fund record. The rules, by contrast, are **[documented]** in his own extensive writing, and they are what a student should take.

### 3.2 Michael Marcus: three confirmations, mental capital, and the courage to hold

**Edge as he describes it.** Marcus, who began as a fundamental analyst and lost money, rebuilt himself under Seykota into a trader who required three independent confirmations before a trade counted as his best: "The best trades are the ones in which you have all three things going for you: fundamentals, technicals, and market tone. First, the fundamentals should suggest that there is an imbalance of supply and demand, which could result in a major move. Second, the chart must show that the market is moving in the direction that the fundamentals suggest. Third, when news comes out, the market should act in a way that reflects the right psychological tone" [https://macro-ops.com/lessons-from-a-trading-great-michael-marcus/]. That third leg, tone, is Livermore's tape test in modern dress: a market that rallies on bad news is telling you who is trapped. Marcus's summary, "I look for confirmation from the chart, the fundamentals, and the market action. I think you can trade anything in the world that way," is a complete cross-asset method in one sentence.

**Sizing and pressing, including the probe technique nobody else teaches as clearly.** Marcus describes taking, at a critical intraday chart point, a position far larger than he could afford to hold, twenty contracts instead of the three to five he could hold overnight, against an extremely close stop: "The market either took off and ran, or I was out." The risk in dollars stays small because the stop distance is tiny and honoured instantly; the size is large only in units. This is the same mathematics as Minervini's tight VCP pivot and Qullamaggie's stop-no-wider-than-one-ADR rule in the local study, arrived at independently in 1970s futures: *size is set by stop distance, so a tight, well-chosen invalidation is what buys you size, never conviction alone.* He also scaled exposure with volatility rather than trading fixed lots, an early form of the volatility targeting that AQR and modern trend programs later formalised [reported: https://pictureperfectportfolios.com/how-to-invest-like-michael-marcus-commodity-trading-wizard/].

**Cutting, and the doubt rule.** "If you become unsure about a position, and you don't know what to do, just get out. You can always come back in. When in doubt, get out and get a good night's sleep. I've done that lots of times and the next day everything was clear." Doubt, for Marcus, is not weakness to be overridden; it is a signal that the three confirmations are no longer simultaneously present, and exit is the default while it persists.

**Psychology: two kinds of capital.** His most durable teaching: trading consumes two capitals, financial and mental, and losing either knocks you out of business. A drawdown that leaves the account intact but the trader gun-shy, second-guessing a working system, has done real damage. Hence his insistence that each trader must trade their own style and accept its characteristic pains: "Every trader has strengths and weaknesses. Some are good holders of winners, but may hold their losers a little too long. Others may cut their winners a little short, but are quick to take their losses. As long as you stick to your own style, you get the good and bad in your own approach." That is Schwager's "method that fits the person" finding, stated by a practitioner a decade before Schwager distilled it, and it is also a direct warning against assembling a Frankenstein method from incompatible greats, a warning the disagreements table in section 12 takes seriously.

**Record and performance label.** The $30,000 to $80 million over roughly two decades figure is Schwager's account of Marcus's Commodities Corporation years **[reported]**. Commodities Corporation itself is well documented as an incubator, it seeded or trained Kovner, Paul Tudor Jones passed through its orbit, and its culture, "see the signal, like the signal, follow the signal," is quoted by Marcus as the firm's teaching. The mentorship chain Seykota to Marcus to Kovner is the single best-documented example in this study of trading skill being *transmitted*, which is itself a formation fact: these men did not learn from books alone. Each had a person.

## 4. The macro greats

Macro is where sizing stops being a risk footnote and becomes the strategy itself. The four men in this section, plus Bacon, disagree about almost every forecast and agree, almost word for word, about what to do when the forecast is wrong.

### 4.1 George Soros: reflexivity, asymmetry, and survival first

**Edge as he describes it.** Reflexivity, set out in *The Alchemy of Finance* (1987): participants act on biased perceptions; those actions move prices; prices change the fundamentals they were supposed to reflect; the loop runs self-reinforcing until it becomes self-defeating. A rising currency crushes an export sector and thereby justifies the fall that follows; a rising stock lets a company raise cheap capital and thereby become the stronger company its price already claimed it was. The tradable consequence: look for a flawed prevailing bias married to a real underlying trend, position for the feedback to run, and stay alert for the moment the loop overextends and the authorities' constraint binds.

**Sizing and pressing.** Soros is the source of the sentence Druckenmiller repeats in every interview: it is not whether you are right or wrong that matters, but how much you make when you are right and how much you lose when you are wrong. In practice at Quantum that meant modest or no position in ordinary situations and an enormous position when the structure was asymmetric. The 1992 sterling trade is the worked example both men retell consistently: Druckenmiller built a short of roughly $1.5 billion through the summer; Soros's contribution, when the break ripened in September, was to urge going far larger, to roughly $10 billion notional, because a pegged price defended at recessionary interest rates could realistically break only one way, the carry cost of being early was small, and the devaluation payoff was large. Britain left the ERM on 16 September 1992 and Quantum made roughly $1 billion on the trade [reported, consistent across both principals' accounts; local study section 4.1]. The lesson is about *structure*, not clairvoyance: asymmetry lives in the setup (a peg, a cap, a forced buyer or seller), and size follows the scenario table, not the forecaster's confidence in a date.

**Cutting and drawdowns.** "First, survive" is his formulation, and *The Alchemy of Finance* is unusually honest evidence for it, because it is a real-time diary, kept as positions ran in 1985 and 1986, in which he records being wrong, reducing, and reversing, rather than a memoir smoothed by hindsight. He also wrote about listening to his own back pain as a warning that a position had grown beyond his understanding of it. Popular retellings turn that into mysticism. Read in context it is the same somatic alarm Seykota, Kovner, and Marcus each describe in their own vocabulary: the body registers an oversized position before the conscious mind files the report, and the professional move is to investigate the alarm by cutting first and analysing second.

**Process and diet.** Soros read voraciously and argumentatively: his method requires a stated thesis about the prevailing bias, which means engaging economics, politics, and history as primary material, then watching price for the loop's stage. Quantum's record, roughly 30 percent a year from 1969 to 2000 in most summaries, with two years above 100 percent, is **[reported]** from fund histories and Mallaby; the fund also had flat and defensive years when, by his own account, no asymmetry was on offer, and he regarded sitting out as a position, not an absence of one.

**What is myth here.** Soros did not single-handedly "break the Bank of England" by force of will; the position worked because the British policy position was internally contradictory, and many funds were short. His own writing is clear-eyed about that, and about his failures elsewhere, including losses when a reflexive loop he had identified ran far longer than his capital or patience allowed. Timing a loop's turn is the weak point of his own method, by his own admission, which is why his sizing rule, lose little when wrong, does the heavy lifting.

### 4.2 Stanley Druckenmiller: sizing is 70 to 80 percent of the equation

Druckenmiller is the most useful living teacher in this study for one simple reason: in retirement he gives long, specific, unguarded interviews, Kirby, Sohn, university talks, the Norges Bank *In Good Company* conversation, the 2015 Lost Tree Club talk, and he tells the same story with the same numbers and the same confessed errors for thirty years. Consistency across decades of interviews is the strongest evidence standard available for a discretionary trader, and he meets it.

**Record.** Duquesne Capital, 1981 to 2010, roughly 30 percent a year compounded, no down year, and, in the frequently repeated count, only five losing quarters out of 120 [reported and self-stated consistently: https://youtubetotranscript.com/transcript?v=G-MlrpoMig0]. He was also lead portfolio manager of Quantum from 1988 to 2000. He closed Duquesne in 2010 not after a collapse but citing the emotional toll of his own standard, a fact that belongs in the psychology column as much as the performance column.

**Edge as he describes it.** Two inheritances, one from his Pittsburgh mentor and one from Soros. From Pittsburgh: do not invest in the present; ask what will make people think differently in eighteen to twenty-four months, and above all watch liquidity, central bank policy and credit creation, because liquidity moves markets more than earnings over the horizons he trades. Earnings and valuation are conditioned on the liquidity trend, never read alone. From Soros: sizing. "Sizing is probably 70 to 80 percent of the equation," and "it's not whether you're right or wrong that's important, but how much money you make when you're right and how much you lose when you're wrong" [local study section 4.2].

**Sizing and pressing, the pig doctrine, stated carefully.** His Lost Tree line is quoted everywhere and misread often: bulls make money, bears make money, pigs get slaughtered, "I'm here to tell you I was a pig. And I strongly believe the only way to make long-term returns in our business that are superior is by being a pig" [https://advisoranalyst.com/2015/04/22/how-to-trade-like-stan-druckenmiller-george-soros-and-jim-rogers.html]. Read the whole passage and the doctrine is conditional, not reckless: only once or twice a year do you see something that really excites you; the mistake 98 percent of managers make is feeling they have to be playing in a bunch of stuff; when you truly see it, concentrate, put your eggs in one basket, *and watch the basket very carefully*. Concentration is paired, in his other repeated teaching, with five buckets rather than one: being able to move across equities, bonds, currencies, commodities, and credit is what let him avoid forcing trades in a market offering no edge, and he credits asset-class mobility directly for the no-down-year record in the Norges Bank interview: the action in bonds and currencies tends to come in equity bear markets, so "you can put the equities in the drawer for a while" [https://ceointerviews.ai/interview/36278/]. Diversification across *opportunity sets*, concentration *within* the chosen bet: that pairing resolves the apparent contradiction and reappears in the disagreements table.

**Cutting and changing his mind.** "Once I'm out, I'm out." Interviewers remark on his lack of emotional residue around losses; he attributes it to humility and open-mindedness: "the only reason you can change your mind is if you're not arrogant about a position" [https://ceointerviews.ai/interview/36278/]. His demand for liquidity is part of the same rule: he favours instruments liquid enough that changing his mind is executable at size. An illiquid position converts a small analytical error into a large financial one because the exit itself moves the price.

**Drawdowns, streaks, and the confessed catastrophe.** "I believe in streaks. Sometimes you're seeing the ball, sometimes you're not, and one of my number one jobs is to know when I'm hot and when I'm not. When I'm hot, I need to turn the dial straight up. When you're cold, the last thing you should do is make big bets to get even, you need to turn yourself down." Then the counterexample, told by him, against himself, in detail, at Lost Tree in 2015 [https://gist.github.com/timhwang21/e6a2b24e064182dd9099ad00e4f4f9a6; https://acquirersmultiple.com/2018/07/stanley-druckenmiller-my-biggest-mistake-and-what-i-learned-from-it/]: in early 1999 he shorted internet stocks with $200 million and lost $600 million by mid-March, down about 15 percent on the year; he recovered to up about 35 percent by hiring young technology specialists and riding the boom; in January 2000 he sold all the tech, calling valuations insane at 104 times earnings; then, watching his two young hires make 3 percent a day while he sat out, he broke: "I just had to play. I couldn't help myself." He bought roughly $6 billion of technology stocks within, by his telling, an hour of the top, and lost heavily over the following six weeks. His own diagnosis is not analytical. It is emotional contagion and ego: the pain of watching others make money in a move he had correctly judged to be a bubble. Every formation curriculum should teach this episode beside Livermore's cotton trade, because they are the same episode, a correct thesis abandoned or reversed under social and emotional pressure, separated by seventy years and identical in structure. The rule he draws: if you recognise a trade was done for emotional reasons, exit it when you recognise that; do not dress it up as a thesis.

**Process and diet.** Liquidity first each morning, then earnings and valuation inside that frame, then price. He describes himself as relentlessly inquisitive and competitive, reading across asset classes, and, importantly for a modern practitioner, willing to hire and defer to people who understand a new regime better than he does, the "gunslingers" episode being both the success (1999 recovery) and the warning (2000 envy) of that same habit. His recent public admissions, that selling Nvidia too early was a mistake, stated plainly with no excuse-making, are the same record-keeping honesty applied in public: the error log is not private shame, it is the working document.

### 4.3 Paul Tudor Jones: play great defense

**Edge as he describes it.** Trading turns. Jones says he made his money at tops and bottoms and deliberately missed "the meat in the middle," because at a turn the stop can be tight and the potential move large, which manufactures the asymmetry he demands. His historical-analog work on 1929 before the 1987 crash, combined with intraday risk cutoffs as the crash unfolded, produced Tudor's roughly 62 percent return in October 1987 in most retellings and a triple-digit 1987 overall [reported; local study section 4.3]. The 1987 documentary *Trader*, filmed before the crash, is rare primary evidence: it shows the process, charts, analogs, and his agitation in real time, rather than a reconstruction.

**The 5 to 1 rule.** "I'm looking for 5:1. Five to one means I'm risking one dollar to make five. What five to one does is allow you to have a hit ratio of 20 percent. I can actually be a complete imbecile, wrong 80 percent of the time, and I'm still not going to lose" [https://traderlion.com/quotes/paul-tudor-jones-quotes/]. Note what the rule really is: a *selection* filter that makes accuracy optional. Most traders try to become more accurate; Jones restructured the payoff so accuracy stopped being the binding constraint.

**Cutting, daily.** "Every day I assume every position I have is wrong. I know where my stop risk points are going to be. I do that so I can define my maximum drawdown. Hopefully, I spend the rest of the day enjoying positions that are going in my direction. If they are going against me, then I have a game plan for getting out." And: "If I have positions going against me, I get out; if they are going for me, I keep them." "Losers average losers" is his compression of the averaging-down prohibition. He also refuses meaningful risk in front of key reports, calling that gambling rather than trading, and treats an uncomfortable position as self-solving: get out, because you can always get back in [https://www.tradingwithrayner.com/21-best-successful-trading-rules/; https://synapsetrading.com/trading-tips-paul-tudor-jones/].

**Drawdown protocol, the most explicit in the canon.** "I will keep cutting my position size down as I have losing trades. When I am trading poorly, I keep reducing my position size. That way, I will be trading my smallest position size when my trading is worst." The mirror rule, increase volume when trading well, decrease it when trading poorly, is stated as a pair, and the asymmetry is the point: most amateurs do exactly the reverse, sizing up to recover. His early biography supplies the origin: a roughly 60 to 70 percent drawdown in his cotton-trading youth, after which, in the retelling of his own lesson, he asked why risk everything on one trade and why not make life a pursuit of happiness rather than pain, and rebuilt himself as a defensive trader [reported: http://oxstones.com/lessons-from-paul-tudor-jones/]. Secondary summaries of his practice put a hard ceiling near a 10 percent monthly drawdown, "he never wants to lose 10 percent in a month," which should be labelled **[reported]**, but it is consistent with everything he says on record about pre-defined maximum drawdown. He also warns that his biggest hits came *after* great periods, when he started to think he knew something, the euphoria-side counterpart to Schwartz's "my biggest losses have always followed my largest profits" in the local study.

**Process and diet.** Charts and price response over narrative: how a market reacts to good or bad news is his health check, Livermore's test at macro scale. The 200-day moving average is his regime line across markets. He has said that ninety percent of any great trader is risk control, and, "the most important rule of trading is to play great defense, not great offense. Don't focus on making money; focus on protecting what you have."

### 4.4 Bruce Kovner: undertrade, undertrade, undertrade

Kovner's *Market Wizards* interview is the risk chapter of the canon, and the local study (section 4.4) has his central quotations in full. What this study adds is his place in the Seykota to Marcus lineage, and the two rules of his that are most often under-taught.

**Edge and method.** Global macro built the Marcus way: fundamentals supply the *why* (a supply-demand or policy imbalance that could produce a major move), technicals supply the *when* and, critically, the stop. He compared ignoring technicals as a fundamental trader to a doctor refusing to take a temperature. Before trading he rehearsed multiple imagined future histories of a market, plausible paths and his response to each, so that surprise found a prepared response rather than a frozen one. That pre-mortem exercise is the direct ancestor of the contingency rehearsal in the local study's playbook.

**Sizing.** "Risk management is the most important thing to be well understood. Undertrade, undertrade, undertrade is my second piece of advice. Whatever you think your position ought to be, cut it at least in half." And on novices: they trade three to five times too big, taking 5 to 10 percent risks when they should take 1 to 2 percent. His own entry rule makes the arithmetic mechanical: a predetermined stop exists before entry because "that is the only way I can sleep," position size is determined by the stop, and the stop is determined on a technical basis [http://www.turtletrader.com/trader-kovner/].

**Correlation as size, the rule that scales from a desk to a fund.** "If you have eight highly correlated positions, then you are really trading one position that is eight times as large." No risk system that counts positions instead of exposures survives contact with a crisis, when correlations converge anyway. Kovner also held a first rule that precedes all technique: do not get caught in a situation where you can lose a great deal of money for reasons you do not understand. And his emotional rule closes the loop with Seykota and Marcus: if his emotional balance was disturbed by a position, he closed it, because a position you cannot think clearly about is too big by definition.

**Record label.** Caxton Associates, founded 1983 after his Commodities Corporation years under Marcus, compounded in the high teens over decades with assets above $10 billion at peak [reported]. Kovner is notably uninterested in legend; Schwager's portrait is of a man who studied, prepared scenarios, and slept because his stops were set.

### 4.5 Michael Steinhardt: variant perception on a clock

**Edge as he describes it, in *No Bull* and in interviews.** Variant perception: hold a well-founded view that differs from consensus, and be able to state all four parts crisply, what consensus believes, what you believe instead, why you are right, and *over what timeframe it will be proved*. The fourth element is his distinctive addition. A variant perception with no catalyst date and no proof horizon is not a trade; it is a hope with research attached. If you cannot state the four parts, you own a consensus position while paying active risk for it, and his instruction is to cut it.

**Operating style.** Far more trading-oriented than the buy-and-hold value managers he is sometimes shelved with: trading around positions, taking profits and re-establishing, shorting aggressively, and running a morning culture in which analysts defended a variant view under genuinely hostile questioning. The daily process point is the transferable one: the thesis had to survive being argued against, out loud, by colleagues incentivised to find its hole, before it earned size. That is a journaling and review practice in social form.

**Drawdowns and the ending.** Steinhardt Partners compounded at roughly 24 percent a year net over 28 years, 1967 to 1995 [reported]. His difficult final years, including a major 1994 bond loss, and his decision to close the fund in 1995 and return capital rather than continue in a market and at an energy level that no longer fit his style, are part of his own teaching. Knowing when your style no longer fits the market in front of you, and stopping, is a risk rule Druckenmiller echoed in 2010 and Robertson enacted, less voluntarily, in 2000. The greats who did *not* stop, Livermore and Niederhoffer in the local study's blowup cases, supply the control group.

### 4.6 Julian Robertson: long the best, short the worst, and the timing lesson

**Method.** Fundamental long/short equity at global scale: own the fifty best companies you can find, strong management, durable competitive position, reasonable price, and short the fifty worst, broken models, bad management, prices resting on fading fundamentals. His test of the whole enterprise is disarmingly simple: "If the 50 best stocks you can find do not outperform the 50 worst, you should be in another business" [local study section 4.6].

**Record and close.** Tiger Management grew from $8.8 million in 1980 to roughly $21 billion at peak and compounded at 31.7 percent a year after fees over its life, against roughly 12.7 percent for the S and P 500, in Robertson's own March 2000 closing letter to partners [documented, the letter itself: https://aletteraday.substack.com/p/letter-78-julian-robertson-2000]. His stated summary of the whole method, in that letter's spirit and across his interviews: "Avoid big losses. That's the way to really make money over the years."

**The lesson his ending teaches better than his prime does.** From August 1998 Tiger was hurt by a yen position, then positioned early and heavily against technology stocks it judged to be priced on "mouse clicks over fundamentals." Investors withdrew roughly $7.7 billion, and Robertson closed in March 2000, weeks before the Nasdaq peak vindicated much of the view. Timing vindication is not a defence available to a fund whose capital has left, nor to a leveraged trader whose margin has called. The twin formation lessons: being right about value does not set the timing, so size and staying power are part of correctness; and the better response to a hostile regime is to *reduce* size while keeping the process, rather than either doubling down on the process or abandoning it. His other legacy is people: the Tiger Cubs, Coleman, Laffont, Ainslie, Halvorsen, Mandel and others, carried his morning-meeting culture of defended theses and conviction sizing inside a risk budget into a generation of funds. Like Commodities Corporation, Tiger is evidence that great trading is transmissible through apprenticeship and argument, not only through books.

### 4.7 Louis Bacon: top-down, rates-driven, and out in days if unconfirmed

Bacon is the most private man in this section, and honesty requires saying that his record here rests on investor letters summarised in profiles, Mallaby's *More Money Than God*, and rare interviews [reported throughout]. What is consistently described: a top-down, interest-rate-driven macro process, form the view on the policy and rate cycle in the United States and Europe, express it in the most liquid rates, currency, equity-index, and commodity instruments available, trade actively around the theme as short-term price action confirms or contradicts, and **close losing trades after only a few days if the market does not confirm**. Moore Capital compounded at roughly 19 percent a year from 1989 to 2012 in MoneyWeek's summary, at relatively low volatility for a macro fund, and that smoothness was the product: it was manufactured by cutting quickly, not by being right more often. A severe 1994 reversal and large redemptions are part of the record he rebuilt from. His shop-design lesson transfers directly to anyone running several playbooks at once: set each trader's, or each playbook's, risk profile first, and fit total assets around those budgets, rather than imposing a gross exposure from the top. Risk budgets per strategy, judged as a combination, is now the multi-manager industry standard described in section 6, and Bacon was running a version of it early.

## 5. The modern discretionary stock lineage, in one pass

The local study gives this family a full chapter each (sections 1.2 to 1.12), and this study does not repeat it. What a formation reader needs here is the *chain of transmission* and the handful of operating facts that the macro and quant sections keep referencing.

Livermore's tape and pivotal points pass to Nicolas Darvas, whose Box turned them into an end-of-day system a touring dancer could run by telegram: buy-stop above a settled consolidation box, sell-stop below it, pyramid as new higher boxes form, no deliberation at the moment of exit. William O'Neil industrialised Livermore's "study the leaders" instruction: CAN SLIM is the distilled anatomy of the biggest winners of every cycle back to the 1950s, bought at a pivot on volume 40 to 50 percent above average, never more than about 5 percent extended, and sold at 7 to 8 percent below cost with no exceptions and no averaging down. David Ryan, O'Neil's protege and a three-time U.S. Investing Championship winner, names the actual study drill: print annotated charts of past superperformers at their buy points and review them until recognition is instant, then scan each night for today's closest match. Dan Zanger stripped the method to price, volume, and pattern, "I use absolutely no indicators whatsoever," and scrolled roughly 400 charts a night, learning the personalities of the same watchlist names for years. Stan Weinstein supplied the regime vocabulary everyone after him uses: four stages around a 30-week moving average, longs only in Stage 2, cash or shorts in Stage 4, and the discipline of staging the market and the group before staging the stock.

Mark Minervini fused the lineage into SEPA, a pass/fail funnel, trend template, accelerating fundamentals, catalyst, precise pivot, risk defined first, and the Volatility Contraction Pattern, a sequence of shrinking pullbacks on drying volume whose tight final contraction is what makes a small stop, and therefore a large position for a given dollar risk, possible. Marty Schwartz contributed the ego rule: he lost money for years trying to be right as a fundamental analyst and made money once he followed price, and his warning that his biggest losses always followed his largest profits, answered by trading *smaller* after a big win and taking days off, is the retail-scale version of Jones's euphoria warning and Druckenmiller's 2000 confession. Kristjan Kullamaggie (Qullamaggie) translated the lineage for modern liquid momentum: breakouts after a 30 to 100 percent prior move, episodic pivots on genuine surprise gaps in neglected names, and parabolic shorts as the smallest and most dangerous setup, all sized from a stop never wider than one average daily range, risking roughly 0.25 to 1 percent per trade, selling a third to a half into 2 to 3R strength and trailing the rest on the 10 or 20-day average. Oliver Kell organised the same price action into a six-phase cycle around the 10 and 20-day exponential averages and was candid that his first six years made no money, which is a formation datum in itself: the apprenticeship is long, and surviving it financially and psychologically is a design requirement, not a footnote. Mark Ritchie II and the *Momentum Masters* roundtable (Minervini, Ryan, Zanger, Ritchie II) close the loop: asked the same questions, the four converge on leaders in leading groups, earnings and sales strength behind the move, precise pivots, pre-set small losses never argued with, concentration in best ideas, and nightly homework, disagreeing only in flavour.

Two cross-cutting facts about this lineage matter for the convergence table. First, the championship evidence shows an on/off switch: winning years in the U.S. Investing Championship are concentrated, high-turnover leadership trading in strong markets, while the 2022 winner in the million-dollar stock division made roughly 13.5 percent in a year the S and P fell about 19 percent, meaning that in a bear year *winning was mostly not losing* [local study section 1.12]. The playbook includes its own defensive mode; running the aggressive mode in a Stage 4 market is a misreading of the teachers. Second, every teacher in this lineage teaches the same study method, historical models, and the same record method, a journal graded in risk units, described in section 10.

## 6. The quants and the builders of firms

The discretionary greats manage a person. The men in this section managed, or built, a *system and then an organisation*, and their transferable lessons are mostly about what that change does to every rule above.

### 6.1 Edward Thorp: estimate the odds, bet a fraction, reconcile the claim

Thorp's arc, blackjack to warrants to Princeton Newport Partners, is in the local study (section 3.1). The operating core: find a game whose odds can be estimated, bet only when the odds favour you, and size as a fraction of the Kelly optimum, f* = (bp - q) / b, because full Kelly, even with exactly correct inputs, expects a 50 percent drawdown with roughly 50 percent probability at some point, and inputs are never exactly correct. Half-Kelly retains roughly three quarters of the growth at about half the volatility; the practitioners' rule drawn from Thorp, MacLean, and Ziemba is to shrink further when the edge estimate is noisy. His Princeton Newport shape is the teaching point: hundreds of hedged, independent small bets, positive in 227 of 230 months in his retelling [self-claimed in memoir, widely corroborated], a smooth curve manufactured by diversification across independent edges and by hedging away every exposure that was not the intended edge.

Two Thorp habits transfer further than Kelly. First, *reconcile a claim against an independent source*: asked in 1991 to review a Madoff portfolio, he checked the claimed options trades against actual market volume and concluded they could not have happened at the claimed size. Apply that first to your own backtests, then to any vendor, any guru, and any study, including this one. Second, his diagnosis of LTCM, whose partners he said thought Kelly made no sense, as "anti-Kelly," levering a small edge until a nickels-in-front-of-a-bulldozer payoff remained, names the failure mode of every blowup in this document: the edge may have been real; the sizing made survival impossible regardless.

### 6.2 Jim Simons and Renaissance: never override the model

**What is documented and what is lore.** Medallion's reported record, roughly 66 percent a year gross and 39 percent net of its extraordinary 5 percent management and 44 percent performance fees from 1988 to 2018, over $100 billion of trading profits, closed to outsiders in 1993 and employees-only from 2005, capped near $10 billion with profits distributed out, comes from Zuckerman's *The Man Who Solved the Market*, built on former-employee interviews [reported: https://www.readtrung.com/p/jim-simons-and-the-making-of-renaissance]. The famous win rate, "we're right 50.75 percent of the time," attributed to Robert Mercer in Zuckerman's telling, and Simons's own summary, "I have no opinion on any stocks. The computer has its opinions and we slavishly follow them," are the two numbers and the one sentence a student needs. The rest of Medallion's signal set is genuinely secret, and any source claiming to reveal it is selling lore.

**The operating lessons, which are organisational, not mathematical.** One: data before models. Simons treated pristine, survivorship-free, error-checked data as a founding obsession, gathering records others considered absurd in scope; a modern summary of the firm's research culture puts its working motto as "there's no data like more data," with humans debating fiercely in research and never intervening in production [https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/1424399014204637186]. Two: the no-override rule is an emotional firewall, and its test came in August 2007, when forced liquidation by other quant funds drove correlated losses through Renaissance's book; firms that intervened and switched models off crystallised the damage, while Renaissance, in the standard retelling, let the system run, adjusted from new data, and recovered [reported: https://manualdobruxo.com.br/jim-simons-and-the-man-who-solved-the-market-how-renaissance-technologies-actually-works-1ngd]. Three: the machine took a decade. Early Renaissance lost money, a Baum-era approach suffered a roughly 40 percent drawdown and a departure, and Medallion itself lost money in its second year; the consistent machine dates from the early 1990s, after Peter Brown and Robert Mercer arrived from IBM speech recognition in 1993 and rebuilt equity stat-arb as language modelling on price streams [https://fasterthannormal.co/people/jim-simons]. Four: capacity is part of the strategy. A short-horizon edge decays with size, so the fund capped itself and expelled outside capital; Renaissance's longer-horizon outside funds have had ordinary and sometimes difficult years, which is not a scandal but the same lesson from the other side: horizon and capacity *are* the product. Five: hire for scientific method, not finance pedigree, and pay from a common pool tied to the whole fund, so no one's bonus depends on defending their own signal. A solo practitioner cannot copy the trades and should not try; the copyable assets are clean data, written rules, no discretionary override of a tested rule in production, honest capacity limits on any edge, and retirement of signals that stop working.

### 6.3 David Shaw: relative value as engineering, and the funding lesson

Shaw, a Columbia computer scientist, founded D. E. Shaw in 1988 to apply serious computing to relative-value mispricing: model each stock as factor exposures plus a residual, trade stretched residuals that show signs of reverting, hedge the factor exposures, and hold thousands of names so that any single residual is noise and the portfolio is the signal. That design, equity statistical arbitrage in the Avellaneda and Lee tradition, is now industry infrastructure. Shaw's distinctive teaching episode is 1998: a large Bank of America financing, roughly $1.3 to 1.4 billion drawn in SEC-filing-based retellings, funded expanded fixed-income relative-value positions that required stable funding and orderly markets; Russia and LTCM removed both, and the capital pool lost heavily, one detailed retelling puts it at about minus 73 percent, a $372 million loss, before the firm recovered [reported; local study section 3.3]. The lesson he institutionalised, and the one a small trader should tattoo next to Kovner's correlation rule: a convergence trade is also a funding and liquidity trade. A spread that is right can still force your exit if leverage, margin, or illiquidity chooses the date. Size every spread for the funding stress, not for the model.

### 6.4 Ken Griffin and Citadel: risk as a separate power

Griffin began trading convertible bonds from a Harvard dorm room, famously with a satellite dish installed to get quotes, and built Citadel into a multi-strategy firm of more than $60 billion whose structure, not any single trade, is the study object [reported: https://www.daytrading.com/citadel-ken-griffin-strategies]. Three operating facts are documented in Citadel's own descriptions and consistent reporting. First, the *pod model*: many semi-autonomous teams, each with its own capital allocation, strategies spanning market-neutral equities, fixed income, commodities, currencies, and derivatives, systematic and discretionary side by side, so the firm is a portfolio of weakly correlated return streams rather than one large opinion. Second, risk is a *separate power centre*: the Portfolio Construction and Risk Group reports directly to Griffin as chief executive, independent of the trading desks, staffed by specialists per asset class whose job is to challenge traders' assumptions, with continuous stress testing of crashes, liquidity freezes, and correlation breakdowns, position limits that cap any single trade at a small percentage of firm capital, and automatic stop-loss protocols at team level [https://www.daytrading.com/citadel-ken-griffin-strategies; https://advicescout.com/ken-griffins-investment-strategy/]. Teams that breach drawdown limits lose capital allocation mechanically; capital flows toward teams earning it, which is Tudor Jones's "trade smallest when trading worst" implemented as corporate law rather than personal virtue. Third, technology and talent recycling are treated as the edge itself: Griffin has repeatedly described performance as the product of teamwork and constant improvement through setbacks, and the firm's history, near-collapse in 2008 with losses above 50 percent in its flagship funds, followed by a full recovery and new highs within a few years, is the institutional version of a drawdown protocol: cut, survive, re-earn allocation. The transferable lesson for a practitioner running several sleeves is exact: independent risk oversight, even if the "risk group" is a written rule and a weekly review you cannot negotiate with in the moment; hard capital limits per strategy; and reallocation by evidence, not by affection for a strategy.

### 6.5 Steve Cohen and SAC / Point72: intensity, catalysts, and layered oversight

Cohen is the hardest man in this study to document responsibly, and the study should say why. His early reputation, a Gruntal trading-desk phenomenon who read the tape and traded enormous volume, and SAC Capital's long record from 1992, are extensively *reported*; his personal rules are not published, and the internet is full of precise-sounding formulas attributed to him that no reliable source supports. A careful recent review makes the point explicitly: a September 2023 Point72 Europe disclosure documents a fundamental-research-intensive long/short approach with group-level oversight reviewing value at risk, stress tests, market beta, large positions, and illiquidity, with flags, guidelines, and hard limits escalating as thresholds are crossed, and it does *not* substantiate the circulating "5-2-1" position rule, fixed VIX leverage bands, or other tidy formulas attributed to Cohen [https://www.luxalgo.com/blog/steve-cohens-trading-rules-risk-reward-insights/]. SAC also pleaded guilty to insider-trading charges in 2013 and paid $1.8 billion in penalties, after which Cohen converted to the Point72 family office in 2014 and later reopened to outside capital; any formation study that presented Cohen as a pure process exemplar without that fact would be doing lore, not history.

What *is* documented and worth learning: the operating intensity and structure. Cohen built a trading floor culture around catalyst-driven fundamental long/short equity, deep analyst research feeding portfolio managers, high turnover, quick invalidation when a thesis's catalyst failed to move the stock, the tape reader's principle that a stock that cannot respond to its own good news is telling you something, and, at Point72 scale, hundreds of investing teams (the firm reports roughly $50 billion in assets and more than 200 investing teams in its 2026 public materials) under layered, centralised risk oversight of exactly the Citadel type [https://pictureperfectportfolios.com/how-to-invest-like-steve-cohen-point72-strategy-explained/]. The transferable items are the catalyst discipline, respect for price response over story, and the same structural lesson as Griffin: at scale, edge is an *organisation* that generates, sizes, oversees, and retires thousands of small theses, not a single gifted reader of tape. Cohen's tape gift was real and reported by everyone who sat near him; it is also the least transferable item in this entire study, and a student who tries to copy the gift instead of the structure has the lesson backwards.

### 6.6 The rest of the quant shelf, briefly

Cliff Asness and AQR contribute the factor discipline: value, momentum, quality, carry, and defensive exposures, each documented across asset classes (Asness, Moskowitz, and Pedersen's "Value and Momentum Everywhere"), combined rather than chosen between, volatility-targeted, and, Asness's temperament lesson, held through pre-described drawdowns with a written account of what normal pain looks like and what would actually change his mind. Rob Arnott contributes the rebalancing engine: any price-independent anchor contra-trades at every rebalance, trimming what ran and adding to what fell relative to fundamentals, and his warning that a factor whose recent success is mostly its own multiple expanding is performance-chasing in costume. Both are treated in full in the local study, sections 3.4 and 3.5. Together with McLean and Pontiff's decay prior, haircut every backtest, expect perhaps half live, they form the systematic conscience that a discretionary trader needs beside them: every edge has a half-life, a capacity, and a normal drawdown, and all three should be written down *before* the drawdown, not discovered during it.

## 7. The options masters, in one pass

Again the local study carries the full treatment (its section 2), so here is the operating residue. Sheldon Natenberg: an option is a volatility instrument first and a direction instrument second; edge exists only in the gap between implied volatility and a well-estimated forecast of realised volatility, and the adjustment plan is the trade, not an afterthought. Lawrence McMillan: structure fluency, for any view there are five ways to express it and the craft is choosing the shape whose Greeks match the view, plus put/call extremes as contrary sentiment that only counts when price confirms. Tony Saliba, the only options trader in the original *Market Wizards*: defined-risk spreads, a Greek portfolio known at all times, theoretical values homeworked before the open so cheap and rich are recognised instantly in a fast market, and a life organised as "tread water a little better than even, day after day, while positioned for the rare spectacular day," the 1987 crash being his, because protection was always on. Jeff Augen: calendars create measurable volatility distortions, so build the database, a name's earnings IV ramp, its last eight actual moves against implied, before choosing any structure. Nassim Taleb and Mark Spitznagel: own convexity, refuse concave payoffs, and run the barbell, most capital safe, a small budgeted slice, commonly discussed at roughly 3 percent, in deep out-of-the-money convexity that bleeds in calm years as an insurance premium decided in advance, so that a quarter like Q1 2020, when Universa's tail strategy reportedly returned over 4,000 percent, leaves a blended portfolio roughly intact while the index falls. The academic spine under all of it, the variance risk premium (implied has on average exceeded realised; Bakshi and Kapadia showed delta-hedged long index option portfolios lost money on average), cuts both ways and both sides are in this study's disagreements table: selling insurance has a real documented expectancy *and* a concave shape that has ended Niederhoffer twice and LTCM once, so the masters who sell it do so winged, diversified, and sized for the day volatility triples, while the masters who buy it budget the bleed as a cost. Neither camp sells naked single-name premium into a binary event, and that unanimity is the loudest signal in the options literature.

## 8. The Schwager canon, distilled once, properly

Jack Schwager's five interview books, *Market Wizards* (1989), *The New Market Wizards* (1992), *Stock Market Wizards* (2001), *Hedge Fund Market Wizards* (2012), and *Unknown Market Wizards* (2020), plus *The Little Book of Market Wizards*, constitute the largest body of primary practitioner testimony in existence, and Schwager himself has given the same distillation for thirty years. It is worth stating in his structure, because the convergence table in section 11 is essentially this list, independently re-derived.

**Method plus mental attitude equals success, and the method must fit the person.** Schwager's own summary is that his interviewees' methods are mutually incompatible, trend against mean reversion, systematic against discretionary, concentrated against diversified, while the attitude underneath is constant. Every wizard found or built a method matching their temperament and, crucially, did not abandon it in its drawdowns. **Risk control is the closest thing to a universal.** Predetermined stops, sizing by risk, no single trade able to hurt them badly; many wizards attribute success primarily to money management rather than entry skill, including men, like Kovner and Jones, whose entry skill was demonstrably elite. **Cut losses and let winners run** appears in every style, including styles that disagree about everything else. **Patience and selectivity**: trading is mostly waiting; overtrading is the universal amateur error; Gary Bielfeldt's poker analogy in the original book, wait for hands where the odds are skewed, is Druckenmiller's fat-pitch doctrine in another game. **Independence and responsibility**: the wizards do their own work, ignore tips and consensus noise, and, in Schwager's telling, none of them blame the market, a broker, or another trader for a loss. **Homework and review**: they work enormously and treat early blowups and long apprenticeships as the tuition that taught the risk rules; Schwager's subjects almost all describe a formative near-ruin, Jones in cotton, Schwartz in his fundamental years, Saliba in an early unhedged position, Kell across six flat years, that reorganised their practice around survival.

Schwager also deserves credit for a later act of methodological honesty: *Unknown Market Wizards* deliberately sought exceptional traders outside the fund world, solo and small-account traders found through FundSeeder performance data, partly as an answer to the objection that the original books only captured an era of floor access and fund scale. The lessons did not change when the subjects did. That stability across five books, four decades, and radically different market structures is the strongest available evidence that the convergent rules in section 11 are properties of trading itself, not of one era's plumbing, while the survivorship warning of section 1 remains in force for every individual story inside the books.

## 9. How they cut, press, and endure: the mechanics in one place

Sections 2 to 8 gave each man's version. This section lines the versions up, because a practitioner does not need sixteen opinions on cutting losses; they need the shared mechanism and the two or three places the versions genuinely differ.

**Cutting is always pre-decided and always denominated in risk, never in hope.** Livermore's 10 percent capital ceiling, O'Neil's 7 to 8 percent price rule, Kovner's technical stop set before entry ("the only way I can sleep"), Seykota's stop at the point the chart sours, Jones's daily stop risk points restated every morning, Bacon's exit after a few unconfirmed days, Minervini's pivot-low stop, and Renaissance's model exits are the same act wearing different clothes: the exit condition is written while calm, and the position's size is *derived from* the distance to that exit, so honouring the exit costs a known, survivable amount, conventionally 0.5 to 2 percent of capital. The greats who express the stop differently, Weinstein and systematic trend programs using weekly closes to avoid whipsaw, differ on *confirmation*, not on pre-commitment, and the disagreements table resolves how a practitioner chooses.

**Pressing is always authorised by profit or confirmation, never by conviction at entry and never by a loss.** Livermore adds only to tranches showing profit. Druckenmiller concentrates when a rare fat pitch appears *and* scales with his own hot streak. Jones increases volume when trading well. Schwartz, instructively, is the dissenter in degree: he traded smaller after his largest profits because euphoria, in his experience, was the risk. Minervini and Qullamaggie add on new bases and new pivots, which is pyramiding with fresh invalidation points rather than a bigger original bet. Seykota's pyramid adds smaller and smaller amounts. The shared shape: exposure grows into evidence. The anti-pattern, named identically by Jones ("losers average losers"), Livermore, O'Neil, Minervini, Druckenmiller, and Tharp, is exposure growing into *refutation*, averaging down, and it appears in every blowup account in this study, Livermore's own bankruptcies, Niederhoffer, LTCM's doubling into widening spreads, Robertson holding early shorts into a mania, as the mechanism of ruin. If this study could transmit only one behaviour, it would be this asymmetry: **add to what is working, exit what is not, and let size be a function of the market's verdict rather than of your need.**

**Drawdown handling is a ladder, not a mood.** Jones cuts size trade by trade as losses accumulate, so his smallest size coincides with his worst trading. Druckenmiller turns the dial down when cold and describes the job as knowing which state he is in. Tudor's reported monthly ceiling near 10 percent, Citadel's mechanical capital withdrawal from pods in drawdown, the local playbook's ladder (at minus 5 percent from peak, gross times 0.75; at minus 10, half and best setups only; at minus 15, stop and conduct a full journal review before restarting small) are one policy at four scales. Schwartz adds the symmetric half everyone forgets: shrink after *big wins* too, and take days off as a reward, because the loss that follows euphoria is a documented pattern in his record, in Jones's warning about thinking he knew something, and in Druckenmiller's March 2000 purchase. Steinhardt and Robertson add the terminal rung: if the regime or your own energy no longer fits your style, reducing to zero and returning capital is a legitimate, honourable execution of the ladder, not a defeat appended to it.

**The psychology is treated as risk management, not as inspiration.** Note how un-mystical the greats are about inner life once you collect them: Seykota budgets emotion into his position size (trade small enough that fluctuations do not "engage" drama) and built a peer process to metabolise feeling. Marcus counts mental capital beside financial capital and protects both. Kovner closes any position that disturbs his balance. Soros investigates his back. Jones, after a youth-destroying drawdown, reorganised his day around being "as happy and relaxed as I can be," with positions that go against him exited immediately so the day can be enjoyed. Druckenmiller names ego and envy as his costliest inputs and humility, the ability to change his mind, as his edge's precondition. None of them describe conquering emotion. All of them describe *engineering around it*: smaller size, pre-written exits, resting stops, days off, peer argument, segregated capital. A formation program should copy the engineering and skip the motivational reading entirely.

## 10. Daily process, information diet, and the record

**The day has the same skeleton everywhere.** Preparation happens away from the live market: Darvas by telegram after hours, Zanger's 400 charts at night, Minervini's nightly scan and contingency rehearsal, Saliba's theoretical values computed before the open, Kovner's scenario histories written in advance, Jones restating every position's stop before the day begins, Druckenmiller's liquidity frame set before any single name is considered. Execution is then deliberately narrow: alerts at pre-written pivots, cards written before entry, no new setups invented intraday. The local personal-quant plan (pre-open read at 08:00, execution window, midday read limited to cutting, partials, or standing down, pre-close pass, close review) is this skeleton with modern instruments attached, and it matches the masters point for point, including its rule that a genuinely new episodic pivot is the sole sanctioned same-day exception, because that setup is defined as a same-day trade with its own card. Review closes the day the same day, while the memory of *why* is still available, because every journaling teacher in the canon is explicit that a journal reconstructed at the weekend is fiction.

**Information diets are shockingly narrow, and narrow by design.** Seykota files the news in the trash. Zanger uses no indicators at all. Simons's firm ingests a terabyte-scale firehose and lets no human narrative near production, the opposite intake with the identical principle: the *decision* consumes a small, defined signal set, and everything else is research material or noise. Livermore read the tape and treated news as the explanation laggards tell each other. Marcus required news only as a tone test, does price confirm it, never as an entry reason. McMillan required price to confirm any indicator extreme before acting. The convergent rule: **define, in writing, what inputs are allowed to change a decision, and let everything else be entertainment you have chosen not to consume.** Breadth, options flow, dealer gamma positioning, and short volume, in the local operating style, sit exactly where McMillan put put/call ratios: regime and confirmation inputs that can change size and structure, never standalone entries. "Someone bought calls" is not a setup, in any master's language.

**The record is the edge's memory, and its unit is R.** Van Tharp's language, adopted across the local playbook, makes sixteen men's record habits commensurable: initial risk R defined before entry; every result recorded as an R-multiple; any loss beyond minus 1R classified as a *mistake* (moved stop, oversize, gap) and investigated as one, regardless of dollars; expectancy computed per setup over at least 30 trades before any conclusion and 100 to 200 for a clear one; execution graded separately from outcome, so a winning rule-break is logged as a bad trade and a losing rule-follow as a good one. Minervini, Ryan, and the championship lineage add the chart: one screenshot per trade, annotated at entry, so review compares the decision with what was actually visible at the time rather than with hindsight's chart. Simons and Thorp add the systematic version: reconcile every claim, a backtest, a sleeve, a month, against an independent source before believing it. Asness adds the forward version: pre-write each strategy's normal pain, depth and duration, so a drawdown is compared against a document rather than against hope. The monthly letter habit, Druckenmiller's public error admissions and Soros's real-time diary being the exemplars, states what you own, why, what would change your mind, and what you got wrong, in prose, on a schedule. A trader who keeps these records for a year owns something none of the greats could buy: a true sample of their own behaviour, which is the only dataset in which their personal edge, if any, can actually be detected.

## 12. Where the greats genuinely disagree, and how a practitioner resolves it

Disagreement between masters is a map of the dimensions on which a practitioner must make a written, personal choice, and then stop re-litigating it mid-drawdown. Each row states the poles honestly, names who holds them, and gives the resolution used in the local operating style, with the reasoning, so a reader who resolves differently can see what they are trading away.

| Dimension | Pole A | Pole B | Practitioner's resolution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concentration vs diversification | Druckenmiller, Soros, Robertson: concentrate in conviction, five to twelve names, press hard | Thorp, Simons, Shaw, Griffin: diversify across hundreds or thousands of independent small bets and pods | Both, at different layers: concentrate *within* a discretionary book (a handful of names you can defend out loud), diversify *across* playbooks and sleeves, whose return streams are the independent bets. The error is mixing layers, forty names in one book is neither. |
| Prediction vs reaction | Soros, Druckenmiller, Steinhardt: form a scenario or variant perception and position for it | Seykota, Simons, Thorp: no opinions, only estimated odds, systems, and reaction to price | Scenarios set *context and size*; price and invalidation set *entries and exits*. A macro view may raise or lower heat and choose instruments; it may never override a stop or a stage signal. Steinhardt's fourth element, a proof timeframe, disciplines the predictive side. |
| Fundamentals: essential or irrelevant? | O'Neil, Minervini, Robertson, Marcus (leg one): earnings, quality, and supply-demand imbalance are essential | Zanger, Seykota, Darvas (mature), Simons: price, volume, and pattern alone suffice | Fundamentals as a *filter*, price as the *trigger*, Marcus's three-leg test as the standard for best-grade trades. Either alone is half of the lineage's method, and the filter/trigger split is testable in your own journal within a year. |
| Following rules vs breaking them | Renaissance in production, Seykota rules 1 to 4: follow without question, never override | Seykota rule 5, Druckenmiller, Soros: know when to break them; discretion is the edge | Rules bind *in the moment*; they may be changed only in a scheduled review, in writing, with evidence. That is what "know when to break the rules" means at institutional scale (Citadel changes allocations between periods, never mid-panic by whim), and it preserves both poles' real content. |
| Stops: hard intraday vs confirmed close | Jones, Kovner, Minervini, Qullamaggie: invalidation honoured when hit | Weinstein, systematic trend: weekly-close confirmation to avoid whipsaw | One convention per setup, written on its card before entry. Fast leadership setups use the intraday or daily-close break; slow portfolio sleeves use weekly rules. Mixing conventions *inside* a live trade, invoking the weekly rule to excuse today's breach, is the actual error both camps condemn. |
| Selling premium vs owning convexity | Variance-risk-premium harvesters and the put-write evidence: sell rich insurance systematically | Taleb, Spitznagel, Saliba's protection rule: own convexity, refuse concave payoffs | A barbell, held simultaneously: sell premium only defined-risk, winged, diversified, and sized for a volatility-tripling day, *and* own a small, budgeted tail sleeve. Either half alone is an incomplete philosophy; Niederhoffer is what the first half looks like without the second, and an unbudgeted long-premium book bleeds out in calm years, which is the second half without the first's discipline. |
| Trend following vs mean reversion | Seykota, the Turtle tradition, time-series momentum evidence: ride sides, never pick tops and bottoms | Shaw-style stat-arb, Arnott's rebalancing, dispersion: trade the stretch back toward fair value | Different sleeves, different money, different clocks, and never the twain inside one position: a momentum trade is never "rescued" by a value argument, and a rebalancing core never chases. Asness and Arnott's own factor-timing dispute resolves the same way: hold both, rebalance on a calendar, do not time the rotation tactically. |
| Factor and signal timing | Arnott: valuations can warn that a factor is crowded and expensive | Asness: timing factors is weak and mostly disguised value exposure | Do not time factors for return. Do use extreme crowding or re-rating as a *risk* flag to trim a sleeve, which is a drawdown decision, not a return prediction, and is the only claim of Arnott's the evidence needs you to act on. |
| Discretion and feel vs pure system | Jones, Cohen, Druckenmiller: cultivated feel, the tape, the turn, is the edge | Simons, Seykota, Thorp: feel is bias; the model or the rule decides | Earn discretion. A tested rule runs by default; discretion may *reduce* size, tighten an exit, or veto a trade (all defensive acts) from day one, and may *initiate or enlarge* only after a year of journal evidence shows your discretionary overrides have positive expectancy in R. That single asymmetry captures most of both camps' value. |
| How much to risk when certain | Soros and Druckenmiller at the fat pitch: bet very large when the structure is asymmetric | Kovner always, Seykota always: undertrade, cut your intended size in half, under 1 to 5 percent regardless | Distinguish *portfolio share* from *risk share*. The fat-pitch masters concentrated portfolio share in liquid instruments against scenario tables with small, defined worst cases, the sterling trade's worst case was carry. A practitioner may concentrate share only when the worst case, honestly computed including gaps, stays inside the ordinary per-trade risk budget; if it does not, it is not a fat pitch, it is a large bet wearing a fat pitch's clothes. |
| When a great trade ends: targets vs trailing | Jones turns and partial exits at extremes; Qullamaggie and Minervini sell a third to a half into 2 to 3R strength | Livermore, Seykota, Weinstein: sit, trail, let the trend or the stage end it; targets cap the very tail that pays for everything | Partial into strength on a written schedule, trail the core to a trend or stage exit. The partial pays the trader and lowers heat (Schwartz's and Jones's psychology), the trailed core keeps the tail (Livermore's arithmetic). Pure targets forfeit the tail; pure sitting forfeits nothing except the trader's ability to endure the give-back, which is why the hybrid exists. |
| What a drawdown means | Druckenmiller and Jones: information about *you*, turn the dial down, you are cold | Asness and the systematic camp: a drawdown inside pre-written normal pain is information about *nothing*; changing course is the error | Both, sequenced: first compare the drawdown with the pre-written normal-pain document for that strategy. Inside normal pain and execution grades are clean, hold course at reduced size (the ladder still applies). Outside normal pain, or execution grades show drift, stop and review. The document, written in calm, arbitrates between the two philosophies so the argument never happens at the worst moment. |

## 13. What does not transfer, said plainly

Floor access, bucket-shop tapes, 1970s commodity trend persistence, pre-decimalisation spreads, and the information speed of every era before this one are gone, and any course selling them as edges is selling costume. Renaissance's and D. E. Shaw's execution, data, and cost structures cannot be replicated from a laptop, and neither can Citadel's or Point72's research organisations; what transfers from the firms is governance, not signals. Livermore's size relative to his markets, Soros's size as part of the reflexive loop itself, and Robertson's shareholder base are not available to a small trader, and the small trader's compensating advantages, no capacity constraint, no redemption risk, the ability to sit in cash for months and to trade illiquid-by-institutional-standards names, are real and are exactly the ones Qullamaggie and the championship lineage exploit. Finally, the *returns* do not transfer as expectations: championship percentages cluster in historic bull years, Medallion is capacity-capped by design, and Duquesne's record belongs to a man who also lost $600 million on one emotional episode inside it. The transferable inheritance is a way of operating: a small playbook studied to fluency, invalidation before entry, size from risk, exposure that grows into evidence and shrinks into refutation, a ladder for drawdowns, a narrow information diet, and a record kept in R that makes next year's trader smarter than this year's. Every great in this study, including the ones who broke their own rules and paid for it in public, teaches some part of that sentence, and the ones who lasted teach all of it.
