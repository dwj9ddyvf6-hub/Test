Surface: a theory and evidence review covering individual bias, collective dynamics, structural theories of instability, the empirical record of sentiment measurement, and the logic of forecasting crowd belief.

Second, which collective theories make distinct predictions, and where do they merely rename the same cycle? Third, which sentiment effects have survived replication, at what size, horizon, and decay, and which have not? Edges: this is not a trading system. It does not estimate a live signal, it does not backtest, and it does not claim that any effect named here is currently exploitable after costs. Psychology explains a large part of why prices can depart from a narrow fundamental benchmark. It explains far less, on its own, about timing. That asymmetry runs through the whole document and is the single most important thing to internalise before building anything.

## How to read the grades

Grades used in Section 3 and echoed elsewhere:

* **Robust**: found in multiple independent samples, countries, or data types, with a mechanism that predicts where the effect should be stronger or weaker, and with that moderation actually observed.
* **Moderate**: replicated, but size, sign, or horizon depends materially on sample, construction, or regime. Usable as a conditional input, dangerous as a standalone rule.
* **Fragile**: published and plausible, but sensitive to specification, period, or multiple testing corrections. Treat as a hypothesis, not a fact.
* **Debunked or failed in its strong form**: the headline claim did not survive direct replication, out of sample tests, or live deployment. A weaker cousin may still be true and is named separately where it exists.
* **Unverifiable as stated**: the claim is framed so loosely that no observation could refute it. Such claims are excluded from the testable propositions.

A note on honesty about citations: journal, book, and working paper citations below follow the standard scholarly record for this literature. Where a finding is best known through a replication or a review rather than the original, both are cited, because for an engine builder the replication is often the more decision relevant document.

## History, backfill, and provenance

The owner ruling of 2026-10-30 governs every historical number in this volume and in any engine built on it. The world did not start when the engine did, and neither did crowd psychology. Three rules follow.

First, inputs are backfilled, honestly. Where a published series exists back to 1929, 1963, 1978, or whenever it genuinely begins, the engine uses the full series, not a convenient recent window. Percentile bands, extreme zones, and regime history are computed on the backfilled distribution. A reading described as a 95th percentile event must be a 95th percentile event against the full honest history of that series, or the label must name the shorter window it actually used.

Second, provenance travels with the data. Each series carries its source, its retrieval date, its true start date, and whether its values are as first published or later revised. Several workhorse series are revised: University of Michigan consumer sentiment is revised after first release, the Baker and Wurgler index changes when its underlying inputs are revised, and Shiller valuation data are restated as earnings are restated. An engine that silently mixes vintage and revised values will manufacture fake precision at exactly the historical extremes it most wants to study. Where the distinction matters for a claim in Section 3, this study says which vintage the original authors used.

Third, reconstructions are labelled, and the ledger is sacred. A gauge computed today from historical inputs, using a formula invented today, is a RECONSTRUCTION. It describes what the formula would have said. It is not evidence that anyone, human or engine, forecast anything at the time. Reconstructions are legitimate for calibration and for backtests, and they must carry the label in every table, chart, and terminal display. The forecast and outcome ledger obeys the opposite rule: it is never backfilled, never reconstructed, and never repaired after the fact. A prediction exists only if it was written before its horizon began. Section 5 enforces this by labelling every proposition as either a live ledger proposition, graded only forward, or a history proposition, graded on backfilled inputs and labelled a backtest. Confusing the two is the single most serious integrity failure available to this program, worse than any wrong forecast.

A practical consequence deserves stating now, because it shapes Section 3. For the great historical extremes, some modern gauges simply did not exist. There is no AAII survey for 1929, because the survey begins in 1987. There is no VIX as published for 1987, because the index begins in 1990 with a different construction and its modern form dates from 2003; earlier volatility values are reconstructions from options or realised volatility. There is no put and call volume in the modern CBOE sense for 1929. The honest history of 1929 therefore runs through prices, volumes, margin and broker loan data, newspaper tone archives, and later scholarly reconstruction, and this study grades claims about 1929 accordingly: rich on narrative and leverage, thin on survey measurement. Pretending otherwise would be fabrication with footnotes.

# 1. The behavioral canon: mechanisms, not summaries

A bias label is not an explanation. For each tendency below, the structure is the same: the underlying mechanism, the positioning footprint it should leave if it is real, the market level consequence that follows only if enough capital shares the tendency at the same time, and the boundary condition that tells you when not to invoke it. That last step is where most popular treatments fail. A bias that explains everything explains nothing.

## 1.1 Prospect theory and loss aversion in positioning

**Source.** Kahneman and Tversky (1979), "Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk," *Econometrica* 47(2): 263-291. Formalised for asset pricing by Barberis, Huang, and Santos (2001), "Prospect Theory and Asset Prices," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 116(1): 1-53. The parameter most often quoted, a loss aversion coefficient near 2.25, comes from Tversky and Kahneman (1992), "Advances in Prospect Theory," *Journal of Risk and Uncertainty* 5: 297-323. That number is a laboratory median across gambles, not a constant of human nature. Meta-analytic work finds wide dispersion by elicitation method, stake size, and population. Treat 2 to 2.5 as a central tendency for small to moderate stakes in Western experimental samples, and treat any use of a single precise value in an engine as false precision.

**Mechanism.** Four components do the work, and they must not be collapsed into the slogan that losses hurt more than gains feel good.

1. *Reference dependence.* Outcomes are coded as gains and losses relative to a reference point, usually purchase price for a held position, a recent high for a portfolio, or a round number or prior close for an index. The reference point moves, and when it moves the same price path becomes a different psychological object. This is why anchoring, treated in Section 1.6, is not a separate curiosity: it supplies the reference point that prospect theory needs.
2. *Diminishing sensitivity.* The value function is concave over gains and convex over losses. The first 5 percent move matters more, psychologically, than the move from 40 to 45 percent. In the loss domain, convexity implies risk seeking: a holder deep in a loss prefers a gamble that might restore breakeven over a certain small loss, even at negative expected value.
3. *Loss aversion proper.* The value function is steeper for losses than for gains at the reference point. Realising a loss is not merely recording information. It closes a mental account at a painful value, in the language of Thaler (1985), "Mental Accounting and Consumer Choice," *Marketing Science* 4(3): 199-214.
4. *Probability weighting.* Small probabilities are overweighted and moderate to high probabilities are underweighted relative to linear weighting. This is the component that makes lottery like payoffs attractive and insurance like payoffs attractive at the same time, in the same person.

**Positioning footprint.** If this mechanism is operating, you should observe: reluctance to sell below reference price even when the thesis is broken; increased risk taking after losses, including averaging down and moving to higher volatility instruments to chase breakeven; a preference for realising gains early to lock in the concave, pleasant part of the gain domain; demand for skewed, lottery like payoffs (deep out of the money options, low priced speculative names) out of proportion to their expected return; and asymmetric reaction to news, where bad news for a held loser produces avoidance and delayed processing rather than immediate exit.

**Market consequence.** Aggregated, loss aversion plus narrow framing can generate the equity premium puzzle in the form proposed by Benartzi and Thaler (1995), "Myopic Loss Aversion and the Equity Premium Puzzle," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 110(1): 73-92: investors who evaluate a volatile portfolio frequently experience the pain of interim losses often enough to demand a high premium to hold it. At the single name level, the same machinery underlies the disposition effect (Section 1.3) and contributes to momentum, because delayed realisation of losses slows the incorporation of bad news into prices. Barberis and Huang (2008), "Stocks as Lotteries," *American Economic Review* 98(5): 2066-2100, show how probability weighting alone can price positively skewed stocks low in expected return terms: investors bid them up for the small chance of a large gain.

**Boundary.** Prospect theory is a theory of choice among described or experienced gambles. It does not, by itself, predict that a market must fall or rise on a given day. It predicts conditional behaviour given a reference point and a framing. An engine that claims "loss aversion is high today" without specifying whose reference point, formed when, has said nothing testable. The measurable version is always relative: distance from purchase price distributions, from 52 week highs, from round numbers, from recent peaks in leveraged positioning. It is also contested how far laboratory loss aversion generalises to experienced traders with real stakes. List (2003), "Does Market Experience Eliminate Market Anomalies?" *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 118(1): 41-71, found that market experience substantially reduced some anomalies in field settings, while other work finds loss aversion persisting among professionals in modified form. Grade the mechanism **robust as a description of individual choice**, **moderate as a direct market timing input**.

## 1.2 Overconfidence and trading frequency

**Sources.** Barber and Odean (2000), "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth," *Journal of Finance* 55(2): 773-806. Barber and Odean (2001), "Boys Will Be Boys: Gender, Overconfidence, and Common Stock Investment," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 116(1): 261-292. Odean (1998), "Volume, Volatility, Price Impact, and Profitability of Overconfident Market Makers," in working paper form and related published work; Odean (1999), "Do Investors Trade Too Much?" *American Economic Review* 89(5): 1279-1298. Theory: Daniel, Hirshleifer, and Subrahmanyam (1998), "Investor Psychology and Security Market Under and Overreactions," *Journal of Finance* 53(6): 1839-1885.

**Mechanism.** Overconfidence in markets is not one thing. The literature separates at least three forms, and an engine should keep them separate because they leave different traces.

1. *Overestimation:* believing your expected return or skill is higher than it is.
2. *Overplacement:* believing you rank higher relative to others than you do.
3. *Overprecision:* believing your information is more precise than it is, expressed as confidence intervals that are too narrow.

Overprecision is the form with the cleanest trading implication. A trader who thinks his signal has low variance will trade larger, trade more often, diversify less, and update less on contrary evidence, because contrary evidence looks like noise relative to a supposedly precise prior. Self attribution then protects the error: wins are credited to skill, losses to bad luck, so confidence can rise after a winning streak even when the streak is statistically unremarkable. Daniel, Hirshleifer, and Subrahmanyam (1998) build an asset pricing model on exactly this asymmetry, generating short run momentum from continued overconfidence after confirming news and long run reversal when the overreaction corrects.

**Evidence and size.** In Barber and Odean (2000), 66,465 households at a large discount broker, 1991 to 1996, the most actively trading quintile earned about 11.4 percent per year net against a market return of about 17.9 percent over the same period, a gap of roughly 6.5 percentage points per year. The gross returns of high and low turnover households were much closer: the damage was done largely by trading costs and by poor trade timing, in that the stocks bought tended to underperform the stocks sold. Odean (1999) found that, on average, the stocks individual investors bought underperformed the stocks they sold over the following year, by a margin on the order of 3 percentage points before costs in the specifications most often cited, which is inconsistent with the trades carrying positive private information on average. In Barber and Odean (2001), men traded about 45 percent more than women in the same brokerage sample and reduced their net returns by about 0.94 percentage points per year more than women did, with the gap larger among single investors. The gender variable is a proxy instrument for overconfidence in that design, not a claim that gender itself causes returns. Later work in other countries and in newer retail samples, including Taiwanese day trading records studied by Barber, Lee, Liu, and Odean (2009), "Just How Much Do Individual Investors Lose by Trading?" *Review of Financial Studies* 22(2): 609-632, finds the same direction: aggregate individual investor trading losses are economically large, on the order of 2 percent of GDP in that market and period by their accounting, driven by losses to institutions plus commissions and taxes.

**Positioning footprint.** Rising turnover without a corresponding rise in information events; narrowing dispersion of analyst or retail forecasts after a run of confirming price action; larger average position size and higher leverage after winning periods; increased options activity concentrated in short dated contracts, which offer the highest leverage per unit of capital for expressing a precise view.

**Boundary and modern complication.** Turnover is not a pure overconfidence meter. Turnover also rises with genuine news, with lower transaction costs, with gamification and app design, and with market volatility itself. The zero commission era after 2019 in the United States mechanically raised retail turnover without requiring any change in human confidence. Any engine that reads raw turnover as confidence without normalising for cost regime, volatility, and news intensity will manufacture false extremes. The robust claim is narrower and still powerful: conditional on trading, more trading by non professional investors predicts worse net outcomes for those investors on average, and aggregate retail intensity tends to rise late in moves rather than at their start. Grade: **robust** for the cost of excessive trading, **moderate** for turnover as a real time sentiment gauge.

## 1.3 The disposition effect

**Sources.** Shefrin and Statman (1985), "The Disposition to Sell Winners Too Early and Ride Losers Too Long," *Journal of Finance* 40(3): 777-790, who coined the term. Odean (1998), "Are Investors Reluctant to Realize Their Losses?" *Journal of Finance* 53(5): 1775-1798, the canonical brokerage test. Grinblatt and Keloharju (2001), "What Makes Investors Trade?" *Journal of Finance* 56(2): 589-616, Finnish registry data. Kaustia (2010), "Prospect Theory and the Disposition Effect," *Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis* 45(3): 791-812, on functional form.

**Mechanism.** The disposition effect is prospect theory plus mental accounting applied position by position. Selling a winner closes the account in the gain domain, delivering a realised pleasure that the concave value function makes attractive now rather than later. Holding a loser keeps the account open and preserves the convex, gamble to breakeven option, while selling would force the steep loss part of the value function to be experienced as realised. Regret aversion adds force: a realised loss admits error, an unrealised loss can still be narrated as temporary. Pride seeking adds force on the other side: a realised gain can be reported, to oneself or others, as proof of skill.

**Evidence and size.** Odean (1998), 10,000 accounts, 1987 to 1993: investors realised gains at a rate roughly 50 percent higher than they realised losses. In his terms, the proportion of gains realised was about 0.148 against a proportion of losses realised of about 0.098. The effect held across most of the year and weakened in December, when tax loss selling gives an offsetting rational motive, which is itself a useful falsification style check: a purely mechanical artefact would not respect the tax calendar. The winners sold went on to outperform the losers retained by about 3.4 percentage points over the following year in that sample, so the behaviour was costly, not a disguised informed strategy. The effect has since been found in many countries, in housing (Genesove and Mayer, 2001, "Loss Aversion and Seller Behavior: Evidence from the Housing Market," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 116(4): 1233-1260, where sellers facing nominal losses set higher asking prices and waited longer), in mutual fund managers in attenuated form, and in laboratory markets. Kaustia (2010) shows the propensity to sell rises with gain size but in a way that is not fully captured by the simplest prospect theory value function, a reminder that the label covers a family of related behaviours rather than one exact equation.

**Market consequence.** Disposition driven selling creates a supply overhang pattern with price implications. Grinblatt and Han (2005), "Prospect Theory, Mental Accounting, and Momentum," *Journal of Financial Economics* 78(2): 311-339, model how aggregate unrealised capital gains in a stock predict its future returns: when the shareholder base sits on losses, disposition holders withhold supply and bad news is incorporated slowly, contributing to momentum; when the base sits on large gains, eager realisation caps advances. Empirically, the capital gains overhang variable has shown predictive content in United States data in that literature, though its incremental value over standard momentum in recent decades is contested.

For crashes, the disposition effect has a second, darker implication. As long as losses are moderate, holders ride them. Once losses cross a pain or margin threshold, behaviour flips from riding to capitulation (Section 1.9). The same population can therefore produce both abnormally low selling volume on the way down and abnormally high selling volume at the bottom. Any model that assumes a constant propensity to sell will miss both regimes.

**Boundary.** Not all reluctance to sell a loser is bias. Tax, information, rebalancing, and transaction cost explanations were tested and largely rejected as full explanations in Odean (1998), but in any individual case they can dominate. At the aggregate level the effect is **robust**. As a day to day timing tool for an index it is **weak**, because the distribution of unrealised gains across holders is slow moving and hard to observe without holdings data.

## 1.4 Herding and information cascades

**Sources.** Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch (1992), "A Theory of Fads, Fashion, Custom, and Cultural Change as Informational Cascades," *Journal of Political Economy* 100(5): 992-1026. Banerjee (1992), "A Simple Model of Herd Behavior," *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 107(3): 797-817. Scharfstein and Stein (1990), "Herd Behavior and Investment," *American Economic Review* 80(3): 465-479, on reputational herding. Lakonishok, Shleifer, and Vishny (1992), "The Impact of Institutional Trading on Stock Prices," *Journal of Financial Economics* 32(1): 23-43, an early and largely negative test for strong institutional herding in equities. Chiang and Zheng (2010), "An Empirical Analysis of Herd Behaviour in Global Stock Markets," *Journal of Banking and Finance* 34(8): 1911-1921, using return dispersion methods.

**Mechanism.** Two distinct mechanisms share the word herding, and confusing them produces bad measurement.

*Informational cascades.* Agents decide sequentially and observe predecessors actions but not their private signals. After a few early actions agree, a later agent may rationally follow the crowd even against his own signal, because the observed actions aggregate more information than he holds alone. Once a cascade starts, private information stops entering the public pool, so the cascade rests on a thin base of early signals and is therefore fragile: a small piece of public news can reverse it. The key insight is that cascades do not require irrationality. Rational agents, coarse action spaces (buy or not, in or out), and sequential observation are enough.

*Reputational and career herding.* A fund manager who fails conventionally risks less career damage than one who fails unconventionally, in the spirit of Keynes remark on conventional failure, developed formally by Scharfstein and Stein (1990). This produces clustering in holdings and trades among professionals even when their private views differ. It is reinforced by benchmark relative compensation: the cost of underperforming alone exceeds the benefit of outperforming alone.

A third, simpler mechanism is payoff externality: in markets with momentum, liquidity, or network adoption, following the crowd is directly profitable for a while regardless of information. Trend following by systematic funds is not a psychological error at all, but its footprint, correlated buying of what has risen, is observationally similar to herding in price and flow data. An engine that labels all correlated buying as herd psychology will misread systematic flows.

**Evidence.** Direct measurement is index and method dependent. Lakonishok, Shleifer, and Vishny (1992) found only modest herding among pension fund managers in individual stocks by their trade based measure. Return dispersion approaches, following Christie and Huang (1995) and Chang, Cheng, and Khorana (2000), find dispersion compressing in extreme markets in many countries, consistent with herding in stress, but the method is contested: dispersion can compress for fundamental reasons when a single macro factor dominates returns, which is exactly what happens in crises. That identification problem has never been fully solved and should be stated whenever a dispersion based herding number is displayed. In laboratory and prediction market settings, cascades are easy to produce and easy to break, matching the fragility prediction. In social media era retail markets, coordination can be explicit rather than inferential, as in forum organised buying episodes. Explicit coordination is not a cascade in the Bikhchandani sense, because participants communicate signals, or at least slogans, directly. It has different dynamics: faster formation, a visible narrative, and dependence on platform moderation and broker restrictions as circuit breakers.

**Positioning footprint.** Return dispersion compression relative to what factor volatility would predict; correlated retail order imbalance across many small names with a shared theme; fund flow synchronisation; forecast dispersion falling while uncertainty, properly measured, is not falling.

**Boundary.** Herding is **robust as a mechanism**, **fragile as a single number**. The engine implication is to measure its footprints separately (dispersion, flow correlation, positioning correlation) and to report disagreement among them, rather than publishing one herding score that hides the identification problem.

## 1.5 Attention-driven buying

**Sources.** Barber and Odean (2008), "All That Glitters: The Effect of Attention and News on the Buying Behavior of Individual and Institutional Investors," *Review of Financial Studies* 21(2): 785-818. Da, Engelberg, and Gao (2011), "In Search of Attention," *Journal of Finance* 66(5): 1461-1499, introducing Google search volume as an attention measure. Peng and Xiong (2006), "Investor Attention, Overconfidence and Category Learning," *Journal of Financial Economics* 80(3): 563-602.

**Mechanism.** Buying and selling are asymmetric tasks. A seller can only sell what he holds, a small set. A buyer choosing from thousands of stocks faces a search problem, and attention solves it by shrinking the choice set to whatever has caught his eye: extreme moves, high volume, news headlines, trending lists, leaderboard tables. Barber and Odean (2008) formalise this as attention based buying: net buying by individuals concentrates in attention grabbing stocks, not because investors have evaluated them and concluded they are best, but because those are the stocks that entered the consideration set at all. Institutions, with systematic screening tools, show the pattern far less. This asymmetry predicts temporary price pressure in attention grabbing names followed by reversal, with the effect strongest where the buying clientele is retail and the stock is hard to short.

Da, Engelberg, and Gao (2011) gave the field a measurable proxy. Abnormal Google search volume for a ticker predicted higher prices over the following two weeks and partial reversal over the following year in their 2004 to 2008 sample, with stronger effects for smaller, retail held names, and search volume rose before and around initial public offerings in ways consistent with retail attention driving first day returns. Later work extended attention measurement to news counts, app platform trending lists, and social media mention volume. The Robinhood era provided a stark field test: Barber, Huang, Odean, and Schwarz (2022), "Attention Induced Trading and Returns: Evidence from Robinhood Users," *Journal of Finance* 77(6): 3141-3190, using the platform popularity data while it was published, found that stocks experiencing extreme herding by Robinhood users, essentially attention spikes, earned negative average returns over the following month, on the order of 5 percent in the most extreme events in their reporting, with the pattern consistent with attention driven, uninformed buying pressure that subsequently reversed.

**Positioning footprint.** Search and mention volume are interesting to an engine because they are among the few psychology variables observable before or alongside the trade rather than after it. The signature sequence, where the theory is working, is: attention spike, retail order imbalance to buy, short horizon price pressure, elevated volume, then decay and partial reversal over days to weeks. Where attention rises but order imbalance does not follow, the spike is probably informational or institutional. Where order imbalance rises without attention, positioning or systematic flow is a better explanation.

**Boundary.** Attention is not sentiment. Attention measures salience, not direction. A stock can dominate attention because holders fear collapse. Direction requires a second variable: tone, order imbalance, or options skew. Much failed sentiment engineering comes from treating a volume of mentions variable as if it were a bullishness variable. Grade: attention predicting retail buying and short term pressure is **robust**; attention alone predicting direction beyond a few weeks is **not supported**.

## 1.6 Anchoring

**Sources.** Tversky and Kahneman (1974), "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases," *Science* 185(4157): 1124-1131. In markets: George and Hwang (2004), "The 52-Week High and Momentum Investing," *Journal of Finance* 59(5): 2145-2176. Hirshleifer (2001), "Investor Psychology and Asset Pricing," *Journal of Finance* 56(4): 1533-1597, review.

**Mechanism.** Judgements under uncertainty start from a salient number and adjust insufficiently. In markets the salient numbers are supplied for free and in abundance: purchase price, 52 week high and low, round numbers, all time high, a famous prior peak, an analyst target, an offering price, the price at which a position was last added. Anchoring matters for prices, not only for individuals, when the anchor enters the reservation prices of many holders at once. Near a 52 week high, anchored sellers who treat the high as a ceiling supply stock and slow further advance in some accounts, while in the George and Hwang account the nearness to the high instead proxies for good news that investors underreact to, so nearness predicts continuation. Both stories cannot be casually invoked for the same observation. The empirical fact is that nearness to the 52 week high predicts future returns in the direction of continuation in United States data and has been used as a momentum signal that, in the original sample, outperformed standard price momentum measures. The mechanism remains debated, which is precisely why the fact and the story should be graded separately: fact **moderate to robust** in equity cross sections, story **contested**.

Anchoring also operates on the downside through purchase price, which is the reference point machinery of Section 1.1, and through nominal price levels in housing and in low priced stocks, where a return to a former nominal level is treated as meaningful although splits and inflation make nominal levels economically arbitrary. Round number clustering in orders and in index commentary is a visible residue: limit orders and stops cluster at round prices, which can create real, if modest, support and resistance effects through order book mechanics rather than through any mystical property of the number.

**Engine implication.** Anchors are among the most measurable psychological objects in a market, because the candidate anchors are public prices. Distance to 52 week high, to all time high, to volume weighted average cost proxies, and to round levels can be computed from free price data with no inference about inner states. That measurability is a reason to prefer anchor based variables over mood based variables in an honest engine, even though the anchor story is less colourful than the mood story.

## 1.7 Recency, extrapolation, and the narrative fallacy

**Sources.** Greenwood and Shleifer (2014), "Expectations of Returns and Expected Returns," *Review of Financial Studies* 27(3): 714-746, on survey expectations. De Bondt and Thaler (1985), "Does the Stock Market Overreact?" *Journal of Finance* 40(3): 793-805, on long horizon reversal. Taleb (2007), *The Black Swan*, popularised the term narrative fallacy; the academic antecedents include Tversky and Kahneman on representativeness (1974, above) and Shiller narrative work treated in Section 2.4. Bordalo, Gennaioli, and Shleifer (2018), "Diagnostic Expectations and Credit Cycles," *Journal of Finance* 73(1): 199-227, formalise representativeness driven extrapolation.

**Mechanism.** Recency weighting and representativeness combine into return extrapolation: investors form expected returns that rise after realised returns have risen. Greenwood and Shleifer (2014) compare six survey measures of expected returns (Gallup, Shiller, AAII and others, in various forms) against the expectations implied by standard asset pricing models, and find survey expectations are strongly positively correlated with recent past returns and with price levels, and negatively predict subsequent realised returns at the aggregate level. That is, the crowd expects most after prices are already high and subsequently earns least. This is one of the most consequential and best documented facts in the whole sentiment literature, and it inverts the naive interpretation of bullish surveys: high stated expected returns are, at the aggregate level and at horizons of a year or so, a contrary indicator in that dataset, because they encode extrapolation rather than information.

The narrative fallacy is the storytelling layer over extrapolation. Humans compress a price path into a causal story with heroes, villains, and a moral, then trade the story. Stories have properties that data do not: they require a protagonist, they prefer intention over accident, they resist base rates, and once formed they filter new evidence. A narrative also solves a coordination problem (Section 1.4): it gives strangers the same reason to buy the same thing at the same time, in portable form. That is why narratives are not decoration on top of markets. They are transmission infrastructure, developed at length in Section 2.4.

Extrapolation has a horizon structure that any engine must respect. At short horizons of months, prices themselves tend to continue (momentum, Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993, "Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers," *Journal of Finance* 48(1): 65-91), while at multi year horizons extreme past performance tends to reverse (De Bondt and Thaler, 1985). Survey expectations extrapolate at both horizons, which is why they can negatively predict at the annual horizon while price momentum positively predicts at the monthly horizon. Sentiment as momentum input versus sentiment as contrary indicator is not a contradiction to be resolved once. It is a horizon statement, developed in Section 4.4.

**Boundary.** Extrapolation in surveys is **robust**. The leap from "surveys extrapolate" to "therefore fade every bullish reading tomorrow" is not supported: the negative predictability in Greenwood and Shleifer is at aggregate, roughly annual horizons, is modest in explanatory power, and coexists with positive short run continuation in prices. Timing precision is exactly what this literature does not provide.

## 1.8 FOMO and envy dynamics

**Sources.** The academic treatment is thinner here than the popular treatment, and this study says so plainly. Adjacent rigorous work includes: Banerjee (1992) and Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer, and Welch (1992) on cascades (Section 1.4); Hong, Kubik, and Stein (2004), "Social Interaction and Stock Market Participation," *Journal of Finance* 59(1): 137-163, showing that social investors are more likely to participate in the market, controlling for observables; Kaustia and Knupfer (2012), "Peer Performance and Stock Market Entry," *Journal of Financial Economics* 104(2): 321-338, using Finnish data to show that positive returns experienced by peers increase an individual entry probability; and the relative wealth and keeping up literature following Frank (1985), *Choosing the Right Pond*. For crypto and meme episodes, the evidence base is largely case study and platform data rather than settled peer reviewed consensus, and is graded accordingly.

**Mechanism.** FOMO, fear of missing out, is usefully decomposed rather than treated as a primitive emotion. It has three separable parts. First, *regret over counterfactual wealth*: the pain is not poverty but watching a salient peer group become rich through an action that was available to you. This is envy in the technical sense of pain at another relative advantage, and it scales with peer salience and similarity, not with absolute returns. A neighbour doubling money in a speculative asset moves behaviour more than a distant fund earning more. Second, *social proof under uncertainty*: when valuation is genuinely hard, the observed buying of others is (fallibly) treated as information, which connects FOMO to cascades. Third, *anticipatory regret asymmetry*: the regret of missing a continued rise is imagined as larger and more blameworthy, socially, than the regret of joining and losing alongside everyone, which connects FOMO to reputational herding. Losing with the crowd is narratable as bad luck. Missing out alone is narratable as a personal failure.

This decomposition predicts where FOMO should bite hardest: assets with no anchor valuation (so social proof dominates), visible scoreboards (leaderboards, screenshots of gains, public portfolios), low friction entry (an app, fractional shares, 24 hour markets), and a story that makes early participants look clever rather than lucky. It predicts weak FOMO for dull, hard to discuss, institutionally held assets regardless of their returns. That cross sectional prediction is testable and is carried into Section 5.

**Dynamics.** FOMO buying is late cycle by construction, because the peer gains that trigger it must first accumulate and become visible. It is also reflexive: FOMO buying raises prices, which enlarges peer gains, which recruits the next cohort. The process ends not when valuation reaches a particular level but when the supply of new entrants willing and able to pay higher prices is exhausted, or when a sharp drawdown converts envy into its opposite, relief at not participating, which can flip the same social machinery into accelerated exit. The speed of the flip is a recurring theme: social transmission that built the position quickly can liquidate it quickly, because the holding rationale was never private information that would be updated slowly.

**Grade.** Mechanism **plausible and partially supported** (peer effects on participation are robust in registry data; the full FOMO loop in speculative episodes is **moderate**, resting substantially on case evidence). An engine should measure its footprints (new account or new holder growth, small trade share, search growth, cohort entry after peer gains) rather than claiming to measure the feeling.

## 1.9 Panic and capitulation mechanics

**Sources.** Kindleberger (1978), *Manias, Panics, and Crashes*, treated in Section 2. Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2009), "Market Liquidity and Funding Liquidity," *Review of Financial Studies* 22(6): 2201-2238, on liquidity spirals. Coval and Stafford (2007), "Asset Fire Sales (and Purchases) in Equity Markets," *Journal of Financial Economics* 86(2): 479-512, on forced selling by funds facing outflows. Khandani and Lo (2007, circulated; published version 2011), "What Happened to the Quants in August 2007?" *Journal of Financial Markets* 14(1): 1-46. Shleifer and Vishny (2011), "Fire Sales in Finance and Macroeconomics," *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 25(1): 29-48.

**Mechanism.** Panic is not simply a large amount of fear. It is a change in the structure of selling, from discretionary to forced, and from price sensitive to price insensitive. The sequence in the modern, leverage based account runs as follows. An initial price decline, from any cause, tightens constraints: margin calls, value at risk limits, risk parity de leveraging rules, fund redemptions, collateral haircuts. Constrained holders must sell regardless of their view of value, and they sell what is liquid, not only what fell. Their selling lowers prices further and raises measured volatility, which tightens the same constraints again. Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2009) name the two reinforcing loops: the loss spiral (losses reduce net worth faster than positions can be cut when leverage is present) and the margin spiral (higher volatility raises margins, forcing more selling). Liquidity withdraws at the same moment because market makers face the same volatility and funding constraints, so price impact per unit of selling rises precisely when selling volume rises. That convexity, impact rising with urgency, is the mechanical heart of a crash, and it requires no irrationality at the moment of the crash itself. The irrationality, if any, was installed earlier, in the leverage and crowding that made the system fragile.

Capitulation is the terminal phase as seen in flow data: the last discretionary holders, who rode losses under the disposition effect, cross their pain threshold and sell into the forced flow, producing climactic volume. The folk claim that climactic volume marks the bottom contains a kernel of mechanism: once forced and capitulating sellers are exhausted, the marginal seller disappears, and prices can stabilise or rebound sharply even on mediocre news, because the supply schedule has changed. The kernel is often overstated into a timing rule. Volume climaxes also occur mid decline, forced selling can persist for weeks when redemptions are staggered, and in 2008 style episodes new forced sellers were manufactured by the decline itself as further institutions hit constraints. Capitulation is easier to recognise in retrospect than at the close on the day.

**Positioning footprint.** Distinguishing forced from discretionary selling is the whole game. Proxies, none sufficient alone: selling concentrated in liquid, widely held names rather than in the names with the worst news (the fire sale signature of Coval and Stafford, 2007); commonality in selling across funds with shared holdings (the quant unwind signature of August 2007); spreads and market depth deteriorating faster than volume alone would explain; correlations spiking toward one as factor selling dominates name specific information; leveraged and inverse fund flows, margin debt contraction, and futures basis dislocations where observable.

**Boundary.** Panic mechanics are **robust** as an account of amplification. They are **not** a bottom prediction device. An engine built on this study should use panic variables to describe fragility and to widen uncertainty bands, and should be explicitly forbidden from converting a capitulation reading into a confident buy call. That restraint is written into the propositions in Section 5, several of which are designed to be killed if capitulation signals turn out to have no forward content at all in live accrual.

# 2. Structural theories: how individual psychology becomes a system

Section 1 explains people. Section 2 explains systems. The difference matters because the system level theories make predictions that no sum of individual biases makes on its own: about sequence, about fragility building in quiet times, about beliefs about beliefs, and about prices changing the fundamentals they are supposed to reflect.

## 2.1 Keynes: the beauty contest and higher-order beliefs

**Source.** Keynes (1936), *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*, Chapter 12, "The State of Long-Term Expectation." Modern formalisation: Morris and Shin (2002), "Social Value of Public Information," *American Economic Review* 92(5): 1521-1534. Allen, Morris, and Shin (2006), "Beauty Contests and Iterated Expectations in Asset Markets," *Review of Financial Studies* 19(3): 719-752.

**The idea, precisely.** Keynes newspaper beauty contest is often quoted and rarely stated correctly. Contestants must pick not the faces they find prettiest, nor even the faces they think others will find prettiest on average, but the faces that others will think others will think, and so on. Keynes claim about markets is that professional investment, under career and client pressure, operates at the third degree and beyond: anticipating what average opinion expects average opinion to be. At that point the object of analysis has quietly changed. The question is no longer what an asset is worth. It is what the market will pay, which depends on what the market expects the market to pay.

**Why it is structural, not anecdotal.** Higher-order belief dynamics have three testable consequences. First, *public signals are overweighted relative to their information content*, because a public signal moves not only your belief but your belief about everyone else belief, and everyone knows this. Morris and Shin (2002) show formally that agents in coordination settings place excess weight on public information, and that more public information can, in some conditions, reduce welfare by crowding out private information. For an engine, this predicts that widely broadcast sentiment readings can move prices partly because they are widely broadcast, a reflexivity of measurement itself (compare Section 2.2). Any sentiment gauge published on a terminal page is, in a small way, a public signal entering the game it measures. Honest design acknowledges this rather than pretending to be a passive thermometer.

Second, *prices can be sticky in the wrong place and jumpy at thresholds.* Allen, Morris, and Shin (2006) show that when traders have finite horizons and know that later traders will price partly off average expectations, current prices underweight long run fundamental information and overweight short run average opinion, generating momentum like persistence and delayed, then abrupt, adjustment.

Third, *the relevant forecast target changes.* If Keynes is right in degree, then forecasting fundamentals is insufficient and forecasting average opinion is insufficient: the tradeable object is the gap between price, average opinion, and higher-order opinion. A crowd that is bullish is not, by itself, a reason to expect a fall or a rise. A crowd that is bullish and believes the crowd will become more bullish, holding leveraged positions that force buying on strength, is a different object entirely. Section 4 builds the forecasting framework on exactly this distinction.

**Boundary.** The beauty contest is a model of a component of price formation, strongest for liquid, widely held, hard to value assets over short to medium horizons, and weakest where a cash flow anchor and a deep arbitrage community bind prices tightly. It does not license the lazy conclusion that fundamentals never matter. Earnings, rates, and cash flows dominate at long horizons in the variance decomposition literature, while higher-order dynamics dominate the path. An engine lives on the path, so it must care about the contest, but it must never report the contest as if it were the destination.

## 2.2 Soros: reflexivity

**Source.** Soros (1987), *The Alchemy of Finance*; Soros (2008), *The New Paradigm for Financial Markets*. Academic relatives with tighter formalisation: the feedback trading and bubble models in De Long, Shleifer, Summers, and Waldmann (1990b, Section 2.6), the leverage cycle of Geanakoplos (2010), "The Leverage Cycle," *NBER Macroeconomics Annual* 24: 1-65, and the credit and asset price feedback in Borio and White work at the Bank for International Settlements through the 2000s.

**The idea, precisely.** Reflexivity asserts a two way causation that standard equilibrium modelling assumes away. Participants biased perceptions affect the situation they are perceiving (the cognitive function runs from world to mind, imperfectly), and their actions, driven by those perceptions, change the situation itself (the manipulative or participating function runs from mind to world). In financial markets the second channel is concrete and mechanical, not mystical: rising prices improve collateral values, reported earnings of financial firms, credit ratings inputs, index weights, fund inflows, and executive compensation benchmarks, each of which supports further buying or lending. Falling prices reverse every one of those channels. A boom can therefore validate its own premise for a long time, which is why Soros boom bust sequence begins with a trend not yet recognised, proceeds through self reinforcement that survives early tests, reaches a climax where the bias and the trend are both maximal and most vulnerable, and ends in reversal that is also self reinforcing.

**What is testable and what is not.** In its philosophical form, reflexivity is **unverifiable as stated**: any outcome can be redescribed as reflexive after the fact. This study refuses to carry it in that form. In its mechanical form, it reduces to a set of specific feedback channels, each independently measurable and each with a sign and a lag: price to collateral to credit to demand to price; price to flows to mandated buying to price (index inclusion and benchmark effects); price to volatility to risk model limits to forced selling to price (Section 1.9). Those channels are real, several are formalised better by others than by Soros himself, and the engine propositions in Section 5 test channels, not the philosophy. Credit where due: Soros practitioner account insisted on the two way channel decades before the leverage cycle literature made it respectable, and his insistence that near equilibrium and far from equilibrium regimes differ in kind is a useful design principle. In quiet regimes, sentiment variables wiggle around an anchor. In far from equilibrium regimes, the anchor itself is being moved by positioning, and mean reversion assumptions embedded in indicators fail in the direction of continuation, until they fail catastrophically in the direction of reversal.

**Engine implication.** Do not build a reflexivity index. Build channel monitors: leverage growth, collateral sensitive lending proxies, flow to price sensitivity, and the correlation between price changes and subsequent fundamental revisions (for example, earnings revisions following price moves in financials and in story stocks, where the causal arrow plausibly runs both ways). Where several channels point the same way, label the regime reflexive and widen bands. Where they do not, do not use the word.

## 2.3 Minsky: the financial instability hypothesis

**Source.** Minsky (1986), *Stabilizing an Unstable Economy*; Minsky (1992), "The Financial Instability Hypothesis," Levy Economics Institute Working Paper No. 74. Popular structural history: Kindleberger (1978), *Manias, Panics, and Crashes: A History of Financial Crises* (later editions with Aliber). Empirical companion: Schularick and Taylor (2012), "Credit Booms Gone Bust," *American Economic Review* 102(2): 1029-1061. Jorda, Schularick, and Taylor (2015), "Leveraged Bubbles," *Journal of Monetary Economics* 76: S1-S20.

**The idea, precisely.** Stability is destabilising. Minsky taxonomy classifies borrowers by cash flow coverage. *Hedge finance* units cover interest and principal from income. *Speculative finance* units cover interest but must roll over principal. *Ponzi finance* units cover neither and depend on asset appreciation or new borrowing to service old debt. The hypothesis is about endogenous migration: a period of stability and rising asset prices validates risk taking, compresses risk premia and lending standards, refinances hedge units on more aggressive terms, and shifts the population toward speculative and Ponzi structures, not because anyone decides to destabilise the system, but because each individual step is rewarded. Fragility therefore accumulates most rapidly when measured risk is lowest. The Minsky moment is the point at which some Ponzi units can no longer obtain new finance, asset sales begin, prices fall, and the migration runs in reverse at forced speed, through the panic mechanics of Section 1.9.

Kindleberger five stages (displacement, boom, euphoria, profit taking, panic) are best read as the narrative calendar laid over Minsky balance sheet migration: an external displacement (a new technology, a financial liberalisation, a policy change) gives the story a plausible beginning, credit expansion funds the boom, euphoria is the phase where the Ponzi share and the extrapolative expectations of Section 1.7 peak together, profit taking by the better informed begins while prices are still rising on late entrant demand, and panic is the forced phase.

**Evidence.** The strongest support is not anecdotal crisis history but the long run credit data. Schularick and Taylor (2012), using 140 years of data for 14 advanced economies, find that credit growth is a powerful predictor of financial crises, substantially outperforming money growth, and Jorda, Schularick, and Taylor (2015) find that bubbles funded by credit, especially mortgage credit, are associated with deeper recessions and slower recoveries than equity bubbles without leverage. That distinction is a genuine, decision relevant refinement of the folk claim that bubbles are bad: the damage variable is leverage attached to the bubble, not overpricing alone. The dot com collapse destroyed large paper wealth with a mild recession; the housing collapse, smaller in some valuation dimensions, produced a systemic crisis because the overpricing sat on bank and household balance sheets at high leverage.

**Engine implication.** Minsky operates at the slowest frequency in this study, years, and mostly with credit and leverage data, much of it published with lags by central banks and the Bank for International Settlements for free. Its role in an engine whose other components move daily is not timing. It is a fragility prior: the same daily sentiment extreme means something different when credit has grown rapidly for years and risk premia are compressed than when the system is freshly deleveraged. Propositions that ignore the prior will average together regimes that should never have been averaged.

## 2.4 Shiller: narrative economics and social mood

**Source.** Shiller (2017), "Narrative Economics," *American Economic Review* 107(4): 967-1004, Cowles Foundation lecture; Shiller (2019), *Narrative Economics* (book). Shiller (1984), "Stock Prices and Social Dynamics," *Brookings Papers on Economic Activity* 1984(2): 457-510, an early statement that social movement drives prices beyond dividends. Related measurement: Shiller investor confidence and crash confidence indices, Yale School of Management, published as survey series.

**The idea, precisely.** Narratives are contagious stories that change how people interpret events and what actions feel appropriate: spend or save, buy or shun an asset class, trust or run a bank. Shiller epidemiological framing is deliberate. A narrative has a contagion rate (how readily it is retold, driven by emotional salience, celebrity attachment, moral content, and visual or human interest hooks) and a recovery rate (how quickly it is forgotten or displaced). When contagion exceeds recovery, the narrative goes epidemic, and its economic effects scale with its prevalence, not with its truth. Classic examples in his work include the Laffer curve story, the gold standard morality tales of earlier eras, the housing always rises story of the 2000s, and the recurring Bitcoin narratives, where the story of a new era and of outsider empowerment is inseparable from the asset demand.

Two points in Shiller deserve emphasis because popular summaries flatten them. First, narratives are often *constellations*: clusters of mutually reinforcing stories that share a moral (for example: technology is changing everything, traditional valuation is obsolete, and the cautious are being left behind). A single story rarely moves a market. A constellation lowers the social cost of acting and raises the social cost of abstaining, which is the FOMO machinery of Section 1.8 operating at population scale. Second, narrative timing runs in both directions. Stories cause price moves, and price moves cause stories, because a large move is itself news that demands a story, and journalists and participants supply one within hours. Any engine that regresses price on narrative tone without modelling this simultaneity will overstate the predictive content of tone. Tone measured after the move is partly a transcript of the move.

**Social mood, and a necessary separation.** The term social mood also names the socionomics program associated with Prechter and the Elliott Wave tradition, which claims that endogenous mood drives both markets and social trends, and that mood is measurable in market prices themselves, making the theory substantially self referential. That strong program is graded **unverifiable as stated to fragile**: its central variable is inferred from the phenomenon it is meant to explain, and controlled tests are scarce in the peer reviewed record. Shiller narrative economics is a different, weaker, and defensible claim: specific, identifiable stories spread measurably and affect specific decisions. The engine should adopt the weak claim, measure named narratives rather than mood in the abstract, and have nothing to do with wave counting. Conflating the two, in either direction, is an error: dismissing narrative measurement because socionomics overclaims, or borrowing socionomics confidence because Shiller is respectable.

**Measurement reality.** Narrative prevalence can be proxied with free text sources: news archives, search volume for narrative phrases rather than tickers, book and media n gram style counts over long windows, and the co occurrence structure of terms. Velocity and saturation, developed in Section 4.2, are the dynamic variables. The hard problems are classification (deciding which texts carry which narrative without reading the answer off subsequent prices), survivorship of sources (platforms change access), and the simultaneity problem above. A serious engine pre registers its narrative definitions and freezes them before grading, or its narrative labels will drift toward whatever worked.

## 2.5 Sentiment as a priced factor: Baker and Wurgler

**Source.** Baker and Wurgler (2006), "Investor Sentiment and the Cross-Section of Stock Returns," *Journal of Finance* 61(4): 1645-1680. Baker and Wurgler (2007), "Investor Sentiment in the Stock Market," *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 21(2): 129-151. Baker, Wurgler, and Yuan (2012), "Global, Local, and Contagious Investor Sentiment," *Journal of Financial Economics* 104(2): 272-287. The sentiment index data are published by Jeffrey Wurgler at NYU Stern and are free, a fact of practical importance for a no paid data engine.

**The idea, precisely.** Sentiment, in this program, is a propensity to speculate: optimism or pessimism unjustified by fundamentals, in the 2007 definition, that expresses itself most where valuation is most subjective and arbitrage is most limited. The empirical index is the first principal component of six proxies, each first orthogonalised to macroeconomic conditions so that the index aims at the sentiment residue rather than the business cycle itself: the closed end fund discount, NYSE share turnover, the number of initial public offerings, the average first day return on initial public offerings, the equity share in new issues, and the dividend premium (the valuation difference between dividend payers and non payers).

The central result is cross sectional and conditional, and it is routinely misquoted as a market timing result. When sentiment at the start of a period is low, subsequent returns are relatively high for exactly the stocks the theory points to: small, young, high volatility, unprofitable, non dividend paying, extreme growth, and distressed stocks. When sentiment is high, those same categories earn relatively low subsequent returns. Large, old, profitable, dividend paying stocks are comparatively insulated in both states. In other words, sentiment behaves like a factor with differential loadings: a sentiment beta. A broad index can look only mildly affected while the speculative tail of the cross section is being priced and repriced violently. Baker and Wurgler (2007) are explicit that aggregate timing evidence is weaker and more debatable than the cross sectional pattern, and an honest engine inherits that ordering of confidence.

**Replication status.** A recent replication and extension (Leong, Li, Li, Peng, and Xu, 2025, "Re-examining Investor Sentiment and Stock Returns: A Replication and Extension of Baker and Wurgler (2006)," *Economic Inquiry*, DOI 10.1111/ecin.13290) reports that the cross sectional effect is confirmed in the original 1963 to 2002 sample and in a new 2002 to 2023 United States sample, while also finding that predictive strength can shift or invert over time in some specifications and that the expected pattern disappears in their Chinese market test where the preconditions (valuation dispersion and arbitrage difficulty structure) differ. That combination, core pattern survives, magnitude unstable, portability conditional, is exactly what a **moderate to robust** grade means, and it is the appropriate grade here. Huang, Jiang, Tu, and Zhou (2015), "Investor Sentiment Aligned," *Review of Financial Studies* 28(3): 791-837, construct an aligned index from the same family of proxies using partial least squares and report stronger aggregate predictive performance than the original index, with monthly in sample and out of sample R squared values around 1.7 percent and 1.2 percent in their reporting, against near zero values for the original index at the aggregate level. Note what those numbers honestly say: even the improved aggregate predictor explains roughly one percent of monthly return variance. That is economically interesting and practically humbling, and both halves of that sentence must survive into any engine display.

## 2.6 Limits to arbitrage and sentiment beta

**Source.** Shleifer and Vishny (1997), "The Limits of Arbitrage," *Journal of Finance* 52(1): 35-55. Barberis and Thaler (2003), "A Survey of Behavioral Finance," in *Handbook of the Economics of Finance*. Pontiff (2006), "Costly Arbitrage and the Myth of Idiosyncratic Risk," *Journal of Accounting and Economics* 42(1-2): 35-52.

**The idea, precisely.** If arbitrage were free, unlimited, and riskless, sentiment could not move prices for long, and most of this study would be a psychology text with no market application. Arbitrage in reality has three frictions, each sufficient on its own. *Fundamental risk:* the rational trader who shorts an overpriced asset may be right about value and still lose if the mispricing widens, because his hedge is imperfect and the asset can become more overpriced on news he cannot forecast. *Noise trader risk:* Section 2.7. *Agency and funding risk:* most arbitrage capital is other people money, managed under evaluation windows shorter than the life of a mispricing. Shleifer and Vishny (1997) show that when arbitrageurs lose money in the short run precisely because mispricing widened, their investors withdraw funds, forcing liquidation at the worst moment. Performance based arbitrage is therefore least supplied exactly when expected returns to arbitrage are highest. This is the institutional version of the panic mechanics in Section 1.9 and it explains a fact that pure bias stories cannot: mispricing can be widely recognised, publicly discussed, and still persist and grow.

Sentiment beta follows directly. Stocks differ in how much sentiment can move them because they differ in arbitrage cost: idiosyncratic volatility (which makes arbitrage risky, Pontiff, 2006), short sale constraints and borrow cost, low float, low institutional ownership, absence of earnings anchors, and story intensity. The Baker and Wurgler cross section (Section 2.5) is the empirical shadow of this theory: the categories that load on sentiment are a checklist of hard to arbitrage, hard to value characteristics. For engine design this yields a discipline: sentiment readings should never be applied uniformly across a watchlist. The same market level sentiment extreme implies a large expected effect in a small, unprofitable, high volatility, high retail share name and a small effect in a mega cap payer. Applying one sentiment dial to every name equally is a theory error, not a simplification.

## 2.7 Noise trader risk: De Long, Shleifer, Summers, and Waldmann

**Source.** De Long, Shleifer, Summers, and Waldmann (1990a), "Noise Trader Risk in Financial Markets," *Journal of Political Economy* 98(4): 703-738. De Long, Shleifer, Summers, and Waldmann (1990b), "Positive Feedback Investment Strategies and Destabilizing Rational Speculation," *Journal of Finance* 45(2): 375-395. Black (1986), "Noise," *Journal of Finance* 41(3): 528-543.

**The idea, precisely.** Noise traders, in the technical sense, trade on beliefs or impulses not warranted by fundamental information, and, crucially, their sentiment is unpredictable. Unpredictability is what converts their presence from a profit opportunity for others into a source of risk. A rational arbitrageur betting against noise traders bears the risk that sentiment becomes more extreme before it corrects, moving prices further from value during his holding period. Because he is risk averse or funding constrained (Section 2.6), he limits his position, so prices can deviate from fundamentals in equilibrium, and assets exposed to sentiment risk can earn a premium for bearing it. Noise traders themselves can, in some regions of the model, earn higher expected returns than rational traders despite being wrong on average in their beliefs, because they inadvertently bear more of the sentiment risk they create and hold overpriced assets whose risk commands compensation. That counterintuitive result killed the naive Friedman argument that irrational traders must lose money and disappear, and with it the comfortable assumption that markets self clean quickly.

The companion paper (1990b) adds the destabilising rational speculator: if positive feedback traders buy after rises, a rational speculator who receives good news does not merely buy to the fundamental value. He buys more, anticipating that his own buying and the news will trigger feedback buying tomorrow, intending to sell into it. Rationality, under feedback demand, amplifies rather than corrects. This is the cleanest formal answer to the question of why smart money does not always lean against bubbles: for a speculator with a horizon shorter than the bubble life, riding is the return maximising strategy, and the collective result of individually rational riding is a larger bubble and a worse eventual break. Keynes beauty contest (Section 2.1) is the informal ancestor of this result.

**Engine implication.** Noise trader risk reframes what a sentiment extreme forecasts. An extreme does not forecast an immediate reversal. It forecasts elevated variance of the future path and a distribution with fatter tails in both directions in the near term (continuation as feedback and riding operate) and elevated reversal risk at longer horizons (as sentiment mean reverts and funding for the deviation runs out). Indicators built on sentiment should therefore be expected to predict the shape of the distribution better than its mean at short horizons. Grading an engine only on directional hit rate would discard the part of the theory that is most likely true.

## 2.8 Sentiment cycles and regime asymmetry: does fear move fast and greed move slow?

**The claim under test.** Market folklore says fear is fast and greed is slow: tops are processes, bottoms are events; stocks take the stairs up and the elevator down. The reverse claim also circulates: greed in the form of FOMO spikes can be extremely fast in the social media era, while fear in the form of grinding distrust after a crisis can persist for years (the wall of worry, post 2008 equity risk aversion lasting long after prices recovered). Both cannot be universal laws. What does the evidence actually support?

**What is robust.** *Return asymmetry at short horizons is real.* Large down moves in equity indices are, on average, sharper and more volatility generating than large up moves of similar size. The leverage effect and volatility feedback literature, from Black (1976) and Christie (1982) through Bekaert and Wu (2000), "Asymmetric Volatility and Risk in Equity Markets," *Review of Financial Studies* 13(1): 1-42, documents that negative returns raise subsequent volatility more than positive returns of equal magnitude, and volatility indices respond asymmetrically. At the index level, drawdowns of a given depth have historically been reached faster than recoveries of the same depth, partly for an arithmetic reason (volatility rises in declines, mechanically steepening them) and partly through the forced selling convexity of Section 1.9. *Loss aversion and attention asymmetry support faster fear transmission at the level of the individual:* losses are more salient, more urgently acted on once thresholds are crossed, and bad news receives more media amplification.

**What is also true, in the other direction.** *Greed formation in the data is usually slow, but its terminal phase is fast.* The Baker and Wurgler proxies that mark speculative excess (issuance, first day initial public offering returns, turnover) typically build over quarters, not days, consistent with Minsky migration (Section 2.3). Post crisis fear, meanwhile, can be the slowest variable in the system: equity fund outflows and depressed valuations persisted for years after 2008, and Shiller crash confidence recovered gradually. And in specific modern episodes, greed has been the fast variable: coordinated retail episodes in January 2021 and various crypto runs produced vertical moves in days, driven by the explicit coordination and scoreboard mechanics of Section 1.8, which did not exist at scale in the samples on which the stairs and elevator proverb was formed.

**Verdict.** The asymmetry is **conditional, not universal**, and the conditioning variables are identifiable. Fear is fast when leverage and forced selling are present, because its transmission is mechanical. Fear is slow when it takes the form of distrust and abstention, because abstention has no forcing mechanism and decays by generational and experiential turnover. Greed is slow when it must be funded by credit formation and issuance pipelines. Greed is fast when it is transmitted by social coordination into liquid, low friction instruments. An engine that hard codes fear fast, greed slow as a prior will be right in leveraged index declines and wrong in meme episodes and in post crisis recoveries. The correct design is to measure the transmission channel present in the current episode and let the speed follow from the channel. This conditional verdict is carried into the propositions, where asymmetry is stated as a channel dependent claim that live data can kill.

# 3. The empirical record, honestly graded

Theory tells you where to look. This section tells you what was actually found, how big it was, how long it lasted, and what happened when other people tried to find it again. The ordering inside each subsection is deliberate: original claim, best replication or extension, current grade. Where the famous version of a result and the defensible version differ, both are stated, and the grade attaches to the defensible version.

## 3.0 A discipline note: why so many published effects shrink

Three background facts should be held in mind through everything that follows.

First, multiple testing is endemic in empirical finance. Harvey, Liu, and Zhu (2016), "and the Cross Section of Expected Returns," *Review of Financial Studies* 29(1): 5-68, catalogue more than 300 published factors and argue that conventional significance thresholds are far too lax for a field that has mined the same returns database for decades; a new factor, on their accounting, needs a t statistic above roughly 3 to be credible. Sentiment studies are not exempt. A sentiment variable tried at five horizons, in three subsamples, with two constructions, and reported at its best cell is not one test. It is thirty.

Second, broad anomaly replication is sobering even outside sentiment. Hou, Xue, and Zhang (2020), "Replicating Anomalies," *Review of Financial Studies* 33(5): 2019-2133, attempt to replicate 452 published anomalies and report that a large majority fail under uniform, stricter methods, with microcap weighting and multiple testing accounting for much of the failure. McLean and Pontiff (2016), "Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?" *Journal of Finance* 71(1): 5-32, find that published predictors decay out of sample and decay further after publication, by roughly a quarter to a half on average across the anomalies they study, consistent with a mixture of statistical mining and arbitrage of real effects. An engine builder should therefore apply a standing haircut to any published sentiment effect size: assume the live effect, if real, is materially smaller than the paper effect, and design position relevance accordingly. In this program the engine sizes nothing, which makes the haircut an honesty device rather than a risk device, but the grading standard is the same.

Third, sentiment has a specific confound that generic anomalies do not: almost every sentiment proxy is correlated with past returns. Bullish surveys follow rising prices (Section 1.7). Put buying follows falling prices. Fund flows follow fund returns. A sentiment variable that merely repackages momentum or reversal will appear to predict returns until momentum and reversal are controlled, and will then often collapse. The studies graded highest below are the ones that survive that control, or that predict something other than the return that generated them: a cross sectional spread, a volume pattern, a volatility pattern, or a reversal after an initial pressure move in the opposite direction.

## 3.1 Survey sentiment: AAII, Investors Intelligence, Shiller, and the extrapolation result

**What the surveys are.** The American Association of Individual Investors (AAII) has polled its members weekly since 1987 on whether they are bullish, bearish, or neutral on the stock market over the next six months. Investors Intelligence has classified investment newsletter writers as bullish, bearish, or correction minded since 1963. The University of Michigan Surveys of Consumers (Section 3.6) measure household economic sentiment monthly, with a history to the 1950s in quarterly and then monthly form. Yale School of Management under Shiller publishes institutional and individual investor confidence indices and a crash confidence measure, in survey form from the late 1980s and 1990s onward depending on the series. All are free or freely summarised. All are as published at the time, which is a genuine virtue: a survey reading is a vintage object by construction, immune to the revision problem, provided the archive preserves first release values.

**The core finding.** Stated beliefs extrapolate the recent past and negatively predict aggregate returns at horizons around a year. Greenwood and Shleifer (2014), covered in Section 1.7, is the anchor study: across six survey series, expected returns rise after realised returns rise, and high survey expected returns are followed by low realised returns. The economic size is meaningful for a slow variable but the explanatory power is small: survey expectations explain only a few percent of the variance of subsequent annual returns in the specifications reported. Nobody honest turns this into a monthly timing model.

**The practitioner contrary indicator claim, graded.** The claim that extreme AAII bearishness marks bottoms and extreme bullishness marks tops is the most widely repeated sentiment claim in retail market culture. The careful academic position is narrower. Brown and Cliff (2004), "Investor Sentiment and the Near Term Stock Market," *Journal of Empirical Finance* 11(1): 1-27, and Brown and Cliff (2005), "Investor Sentiment and Asset Valuation," *Journal of Business* 78(2): 405-440, find sentiment levels are strongly related to contemporaneous and past returns, that survey sentiment has some predictive content for long horizon returns in the contrary direction, and that near term predictive power is weak once past returns are accounted for. Fisher and Statman (2000), "Investor Sentiment and Stock Returns," *Financial Analysts Journal* 56(2): 16-23, find the AAII and Investors Intelligence aggregates negatively related to subsequent S and P 500 returns in their samples, with the newsletter writer aggregate the stronger of the two, but with the usual small sample of true extremes problem: the number of independent extreme episodes in any survey history is in the low dozens at most, so confidence intervals around any extreme zone rule are wide, and rules fitted to famous extremes (October 2008, March 2009, December 2018) are at high risk of being descriptions of those episodes rather than laws.

**Grades.** Survey extrapolation of past returns: **robust**. Survey expected returns negatively predicting aggregate returns at roughly annual horizons: **moderate**, real in multiple series, small in variance explained, unstable in exact horizon. Weekly AAII extremes as a standalone short term timing signal: **fragile**. The engine implication is to treat surveys as slow state variables with percentile bands from their full history (AAII from 1987, Investors Intelligence from 1963), never as daily triggers, and to require any extreme zone claim to report how many independent episodes define the zone.

## 3.2 The Baker and Wurgler index as an empirical object

Section 2.5 presented the theory. Empirically, three facts matter.

First, the cross sectional pattern is the durable part. In Baker and Wurgler (2006), months sorted by the sentiment index show the predicted spread: after high sentiment months, speculative stocks (small, young, volatile, unprofitable, non paying, extreme growth or distressed) underperform their bond like counterparts over the following months, and after low sentiment months they outperform. The 2025 replication by Leong and coauthors, cited in Section 2.5, confirms the pattern in sample and in a fresh 2002 to 2023 United States sample, finds instability in some subperiods, and finds failure in China. Grade: **moderate to robust** in the United States, **conditional** internationally, with the condition being a market structure in which speculative stocks are hard to value and hard to arbitrage.

Second, the aggregate timing part is weak in the original construction and modest even when improved. The original index has near zero out of sample predictive R squared for the aggregate market in the Huang, Jiang, Tu, and Zhou (2015) comparison, and their aligned index improves this to roughly 1.2 percent monthly out of sample, as reported in Section 2.5. One percent of monthly variance is the honest order of magnitude for the best aggregate sentiment timing in the peer reviewed record. Any vendor or internal claim of dramatically higher aggregate timing power from sentiment alone should be presumed to contain lookahead, revision leakage, or mining until proven otherwise.

Third, the index is a revised, reconstructed object. Its inputs are revised, its principal component weights are estimated on the sample, and the version downloadable today is not the version an investor could have computed in real time in 1985. Values for early decades are therefore RECONSTRUCTIONS in the sense of the provenance rules, excellent for calibration, illegitimate as evidence of real time foresight. Provenance: source, Jeffrey Wurgler NYU Stern page, free; inputs from CRSP, Compustat, NYSE, and Ritter IPO data in the original research; series begins 1965 in the commonly distributed file. Grade as a calibration backbone: **strong**. Grade as proof that sentiment was tradable in real time in its early sample: **not established**.

## 3.3 Put and call extremes and options volume

**The claim.** High equity put to call volume ratios express fear and, at extremes, contrary bullish information; very low ratios express complacency and contrary bearish information. The CBOE publishes equity, index, and total put and call volume ratios daily, with usable history from the 1990s in modern form and methodology changes along the way, including the growth of weekly and then daily expiries after 2022, which changed the meaning of raw volume ratios by flooding them with short dated speculation and hedging.

**What the peer reviewed record actually supports.** The strongest options result is not about the aggregate ratio. Pan and Poteshman (2006), "The Information in Option Volume for Future Stock Prices," *Review of Financial Studies* 19(3): 871-908, use signed, buyer initiated option volume at the individual stock level and find that stocks with high put to call ratios constructed from buyer initiated volume underperform over the following week, with a long short spread on the order of 40 basis points per week in their headline specification, and with the effect concentrated in volume the authors classify as information motivated rather than liquidity motivated. Note the sign and the level: at the single name level, heavy put buying predicts further declines in that name in the short run, a momentum or information pattern, not a contrary bounce. The aggregate contrary story and the single name information story point in opposite directions at short horizons, and an engine that merges them into one put and call dial will be wrong in one of its two uses at all times.

For the aggregate index ratio, the evidence base is practitioner research, exchange studies, and newsletter backtests rather than a settled peer reviewed consensus. The pattern those sources report is consistent in shape: extreme high equity put and call readings cluster around short term lows and are followed by above average returns over the next one to four weeks on average, with enormous dispersion, frequent mid decline extremes that fail, and performance that degrades when thresholds fitted on earlier decades are applied unchanged after market structure breaks, notably after 2020 when retail call buying permanently raised call volume and after the daily expiry expansion. Academic treatments of related aggregates, such as Simon and Wiggins (2001) on S and P futures and options sentiment proxies, find predictive content in some options derived fear measures for subsequent returns and volatility, again with regime sensitivity. Bandopadhyaya and Jones (2008) find the equity put and call ratio carries some information for very short horizon index moves in their sample, in the contrary direction at extremes, with small magnitudes.

**The confounds, stated plainly.** Put volume mixes speculation, hedging of long portfolios, and structured product and dealer flows whose sign for sentiment is ambiguous or reversed. Index put buying by an institution hedging a large book is not a forecast of a fall; it is insurance purchased precisely because the buyer intends to stay long. Call volume in the daily expiry era mixes lottery speculation with market maker hedging flows. The ratio is also mechanically moved by the day just finished, because put writers and call writers adjust with price, so contemporaneous correlation with returns is guaranteed and proves nothing about prediction.

**Grades.** Signed single name option volume predicting short horizon returns in the information direction: **moderate**. Aggregate put and call extremes as a contrary indicator over one to four weeks: **fragile to moderate**, usable only as a percentile extreme against its own recent regime, never against a fixed threshold carried across the 2020 and 2022 structure breaks, and only as one confirming input among several. Any fixed rule of the form ratio above 1.0 means buy: **debunked as a universal rule** by the structural change in the series itself. Provenance: source, CBOE daily statistics, free; equity ratio usable in modern form from the mid 1990s; pre 2003 and pre 2020 segments are not comparable without regime flags, and any long chart that does not flag the breaks is misleading.


## 3.4 Fund flows and performance chasing

**The claim.** Retail investors chase past performance, allocating flows to funds and categories after they have risen, and this chasing is costly to the chasers and informative, in reverse, about the future returns of what they buy.

**Evidence.** The return chasing fact itself is among the most replicated in all of empirical finance. Sirri and Tufano (1998), "Costly Search and Mutual Fund Flows," *Journal of Finance* 53(5): 1589-1622, document the convex flow to performance relation: top performing funds receive disproportionate inflows, poor performers suffer muted outflows, an asymmetry that itself reflects loss aversion and attention in fund choice. Chevalier and Ellison (1997) establish the same sensitivity in an earlier sample. At the investor outcome level, Friesen and Sapp (2007), "Mutual Fund Flows and Investor Returns," *Journal of Banking and Finance* 31(9): 2796-2816, estimate that timing decisions cost equity fund investors on the order of 1.5 percent per year relative to buy and hold in their sample, and the long running DALBAR quantitative analyses, which are industry rather than peer reviewed and whose methods are contested, report larger gaps in the same direction. The direction is not in serious dispute. The exact annual cost number is sample and method dependent and should never be quoted as a constant.

The predictive version is Frazzini and Lamont (2008), "Dumb Money: Mutual Fund Flows and the Cross Section of Stock Returns," *Journal of Financial Economics* 88(2): 299-322, introduced in Section 1 terms in this study as the flow based sentiment test. Using 1980 to 2003 data, stocks heavily held by funds experiencing inflows subsequently underperform stocks held by funds experiencing outflows, with the headline three year value weighted spread between the extreme flow quintiles reported at about minus 0.85 percent per month in the original paper, an economically huge number that should immediately attract the haircut discipline of Section 3.0. An independent automated replication of the paper obtains a closely matching in sample spread (about minus 0.67 percent per month, same sign and significance), which supports the in sample result as a real feature of that dataset, and then finds the effect essentially vanishes out of sample in 2004 to 2010 (about minus 0.04 percent per month, statistically indistinguishable from zero). That pair of findings is the most instructive in this subsection: a result can be genuinely present in its discovery sample, genuinely replicable by code, and still fail to persist into the next regime, because flows, fund structure, and the value effect it overlaps all changed. Grade the dumb money cross sectional effect: **moderate in sample, fragile as a forward expectation at its published size**. Grade performance chasing as behaviour: **robust**.

Two refinements keep flows useful to an honest engine despite the haircut. First, flow extremes at the category level (sector funds, thematic funds, crypto products in the modern era, leveraged and inverse products) remain a clean measure of where extrapolative demand is concentrated, even if the return spread that follows is smaller and slower than the 2008 paper implies. The measurement claim survives even where the prediction claim shrinks. Second, flow driven price pressure in the underlying holdings is real and mechanical while it lasts (Coval and Stafford, 2007, Section 1.9; Lou, 2012, on flow induced trading), so flows predict volume, comovement, and short horizon pressure in the direction of the flow, and predict reversal only at longer horizons and conditional on the flow being sentiment rather than information. An engine that asks flows one question, will prices reverse, gets a fragile answer. An engine that asks flows three questions, where is demand concentrated, how much forced trading does it imply, and has the concentration reached historical percentile extremes, gets durable value.

**Provenance.** Investment Company Institute flow statistics are free in summary form with history to the 1980s for many categories and to 2007 for exchange traded fund detail in modern form; Morningstar category flows are the research standard but are paid, and are therefore excluded from engine propositions under the no paid data rule. FINRA margin statistics, published monthly and free, with history in modern form from the late 1990s, belong in this family as a leverage based flow proxy and are used in Section 3.9.

## 3.5 Issuance, initial public offerings, and the supply side of sentiment

Firms are better sentiment traders than households. That asymmetry is one of the most robust findings in the field.

**Long run underperformance of issuance.** Ritter (1991), "The Long Run Performance of Initial Public Offerings," *Journal of Finance* 46(1): 3-27, finds that United States IPOs from 1975 to 1984 underperform matched firms by economically large margins over three years, on the order of 29 percentage points of cumulative underperformance in the headline comparison, concentrated in the heaviest issuance years. Loughran and Ritter (1995), "The New Issues Puzzle," *Journal of Finance* 50(1): 23-51, extend the result to seasoned equity offerings. The result has been debated on measurement grounds (benchmark choice, weighting, and the small growth firm confound matter, and value weighted and later sample estimates are smaller), and the effect is weakest exactly where the Section 3.0 haircut predicts: in large firms and in value weighted portfolios. The direction, however, has survived four decades of scrutiny and international samples: heavy issuance into enthusiastic demand is followed by poor long run returns for the issued paper. Grade: **robust in direction, moderate in size**.

**Issuance volume as a sentiment gauge.** Baker and Wurgler (2000), "The Equity Share in New Issues and Aggregate Stock Returns," *Journal of Finance* 55(5): 2219-2257, show that the equity share in total new issues (equity divided by equity plus debt issuance) negatively predicts aggregate market returns at annual horizons in data back to 1928, a genuinely long sample by sentiment standards. First day IPO returns, a Baker and Wurgler index input, averaged roughly 7 percent in the 1980s, roughly 15 percent in the early 1990s in commonly reported Ritter tabulations, and roughly 65 percent in 1999 to 2000 at the bubble peak, before falling back; the exact averages move with sample definition, but the order of magnitude contrast between normal and bubble issuance windows is not sensitive to definition. Provenance: Jay Ritter IPO data, University of Florida, free, the standard source, updated and occasionally revised, so vintage matters for exact replication. Grade for equity share and IPO heat as slow contrary state variables at annual horizons: **moderate**. Grade as timing tools for quarters: **fragile**. The supply side adjusts slowly, pipelines take months, and hot windows can stay hot far longer than a contrary trader can stay solvent, which is the limits to arbitrage lesson of Section 2.6 applied to the calendar.

## 3.6 Consumer sentiment

**The series.** The Michigan Index of Consumer Sentiment has roots in surveys from the late 1940s, becomes quarterly in the 1950s and monthly from 1978, and is among the longest free sentiment series available, published by Surveys of Consumers, University of Michigan, with a revision process after preliminary release. The Conference Board Consumer Confidence Index begins in 1967, monthly from 1977. Both are household economic sentiment, not stock market sentiment, and the distinction is load bearing.

**What it predicts.** For consumption and the macroeconomy, consumer sentiment carries genuine but modest incremental information. Carroll, Fuhrer, and Wilcox (1994), "Does Consumer Sentiment Forecast Household Spending?" *American Economic Review* 84(5): 1397-1408, find sentiment helps forecast consumption growth beyond standard variables; Howrey (2001) finds the Michigan index helps predict recession probability and growth. Barsky and Sims (2012), "Information, Animal Spirits, and the Meaning of Innovations in Consumer Confidence," *American Economic Review* 102(4): 1343-1377, decompose confidence innovations and conclude that a large share reflects news about future fundamentals rather than pure animal spirits, a result that should caution anyone against treating the whole index as noise trader mood. Much of what consumer sentiment knows is real information about jobs, incomes, and inflation, arriving through households before it arrives in official statistics.

For stock returns, Lemmon and Portniaguina (2006), "Consumer Confidence and Asset Prices: Some Empirical Evidence," *Review of Financial Studies* 19(4): 1499-1529, is the key study: after decomposing consumer confidence into a macroeconomic component and a residual sentiment component, both components negatively predict subsequent returns for small stocks and stocks with low institutional ownership, the hard to arbitrage segment, consistent with the Baker and Wurgler cross sectional logic. Qiu and Welch (2006), in working paper and related published forms, find changes in the Michigan index robustly related to small stock excess returns in their specifications. The effects are cross sectional and slow, not aggregate and fast. Aggregate market timing from consumer sentiment levels is weak and sign unstable across samples, because a high level can mean strong household fundamentals (good for earnings) and extrapolative optimism (bad for subsequent returns) at once, and the two channels fight.

**Grades.** Consumer sentiment as macro information: **moderate to robust**. Consumer sentiment residual as a cross sectional sentiment proxy for small and retail held stocks: **moderate**. Consumer sentiment as an aggregate stock timing signal: **fragile**. Engine rule that follows: use the Michigan series, backfilled to its honest monthly start in 1978 with quarterly history before that clearly flagged, as a slow regime variable and as an input to the cross sectional sentiment state, with preliminary and final values kept as separate vintages. Never present it as a market mood thermometer without the Barsky and Sims caveat attached.

## 3.7 Media tone

**The core studies.** Tetlock (2007), "Giving Content to Investor Sentiment: The Role of Media in the Stock Market," *Journal of Finance* 62(3): 1139-1168, constructs a pessimism factor from the Wall Street Journal Abreast of the Market column, 1984 to 1999, and finds that high pessimism predicts downward pressure on the Dow the following day, followed by reversion toward fundamentals over the following days, and that extreme pessimism in either direction predicts elevated volume. The return magnitudes are small, on the order of a few basis points per day for a one standard deviation pessimism move in the reported specifications, which is the correct order of magnitude to expect from daily tone and a useful antidote to vendor exaggeration. Tetlock, Saar Tsechansky, and Macskassy (2008), "More Than Words: Quantifying Language to Measure Firms Fundamentals," *Journal of Finance* 63(3): 1437-1467, move to the firm level: the fraction of negative words in firm specific news predicts low earnings and low returns, with the stock price underreaction lasting briefly. Loughran and McDonald (2011), "When Is a Liability Not a Liability? Textual Analysis, Dictionaries, and 10-Ks," *Journal of Finance* 66(1): 35-65, show that general purpose negative word lists badly misclassify financial text and supply a finance specific dictionary that has become the field standard, a measurement contribution without which most later tone work is not credible. Garcia (2013), "Sentiment During Recessions," *Journal of Finance* 68(3): 1267-1300, extends the newspaper pessimism approach to New York Times columns from 1905 to 2005 and finds the return predictability of tone is concentrated in recessions, with a one standard deviation pessimism shock associated with a move on the order of 12 basis points in the next day Dow return in recession periods in his headline estimate, and much weaker content in expansions. That recession conditionality is theoretically satisfying (sentiment binds when uncertainty and arbitrage capital scarcity bind, Sections 2.6 and 2.7) and it disciplines engine design: tone should be expected to matter most exactly when trading on it is most dangerous.

**Replication and limits.** The media tone family is one of the better behaved in sentiment research: the short horizon pressure and reversal shape replicates across outlets and countries in broad outline, and the firm level negative word result is robust. The limits are equally clear. Effects are small, decay within days at the market level, are partly mechanical transcripts of yesterday returns (Section 2.4 simultaneity), and are highly sensitive to dictionary and corpus choices before the Loughran and McDonald standardisation. Commercial news sentiment products built on this literature routinely claim far more than the papers support, typically by reporting in sample fits on revised news archives. Grade: **moderate to robust** for the existence and shape of the effect, **fragile** for any claim of economically large daily timing profits after costs, and **contested** on whether tone causes sentiment or merely records it, with the honest answer being both, in proportions that vary by episode.

**Provenance warning.** Historical newspaper archives are revised corpora in practice: digitisation choices, column selection, and surviving editions shape the sample. Garcia long sample is a scholarly reconstruction from archives and is labelled a RECONSTRUCTION for any gauge built on it today, however excellent the scholarship.

## 3.8 Social media mood: the famous study, the fund, and the aftermath

This subsection exists because the most famous sentiment result of the social media era is also the clearest cautionary tale in the volume, and an engine program that does not internalise it will repeat it with better graphics.

**The claim.** Bollen, Mao, and Zeng (2011), "Twitter Mood Predicts the Stock Market," *Journal of Computational Science* 2(1): 1-8, analyse about 9.8 million tweets from 2008, derive mood dimensions with a lexicon tool (including a Calm dimension), and report that Calm Granger causes Dow Jones Industrial Average changes at lags of several days and that a self organising fuzzy neural network including mood predicts the direction of daily Dow moves with 87.6 percent accuracy in their test window. The number travelled around the world. A London based fund, Derwent Capital Markets, launched a Twitter sentiment fund on the strength of the approach in 2011, raised reported tens of millions, and closed after about a month of trading in 2012, having reportedly made a small profit in its single month, a fact sometimes quoted as vindication and sometimes as failure; the decision relevant fact is that the strategy did not survive as a going concern and no audited multi year live record demonstrating anything like 87.6 percent directional accuracy ever appeared.

**The replication record.** Lachanski and Pav (2017), "Twitter Mood Predicts the Stock Market? A Critique," *Econ Journal Watch* 14(3) and related working paper forms, reconstruct alternative Calm series, including for November and December 2008 data the original authors collected but did not use in their Granger tests, and find that the reconstructed measures do not Granger cause the Dow in that held out window. They further document specification fragility: the result depends on lexicon choices, aggregation, lag selection from a garden of forking paths, and a very short 2008 sample that contains the financial crisis, the election, and Thanksgiving, episodes in which mood words plausibly respond to the same news that moves markets. Other independent attempts to reproduce the headline accuracy out of sample have likewise failed to recover it. The consensus position in the subsequent literature is that the strong claim, a stable, large, multi day directional forecasting edge for the aggregate index from general Twitter mood, is **debunked in its strong form**.

**What survives.** A weaker, firm level family survives and should not be thrown out with the headline. Bartov, Faurel, and Mohanram (2018), "Can Twitter Help Predict Firm Level Earnings and Stock Returns?" *The Accounting Review* 93(3): 25-57, find that the aggregate opinion in firm specific tweets before earnings announcements predicts earnings and announcement returns, consistent with Twitter aggregating dispersed firm specific information rather than generic mood. Sprenger, Tumasjan, Sandner, and Welpe (2014), "Tweets and Trades," *European Financial Management* 20(5): 926-957, find message volume, sentiment, and agreement related to returns, volume, and volatility at the stock level with appropriate small magnitudes. StockTwits based work by Cookson and Niessner (2020) documents that disagreement on social platforms is associated with trading activity and has asset pricing content at the firm level. The pattern across the survivors is identical to the pattern in media tone: firm specific, short horizon, small, and about information aggregation and attention, not about national mood moving the index days later. Platform access is also a provenance fact: social platform data access has tightened repeatedly since 2023, series break without notice, and any engine gauge built on a platform feed must carry a break flag and a death plan for the series, or its history will silently change meaning.

**Grades.** Aggregate social mood predicting the index at multi day horizons with high accuracy: **debunked in its strong form**. Firm specific social attention and tone carrying small short horizon information: **moderate**. Social volume as an attention measure in the Da, Engelberg, and Gao sense: **moderate to robust**, with the direction supplied by a separate variable (Section 1.5).

## 3.9 Other famous effects: a short honour and dishonour roll

*Sunshine and weather.* Hirshleifer and Shumway (2003), "Good Day Sunshine: Stock Returns and the Weather," *Journal of Finance* 58(3): 1009-1032, report that morning sunshine in the city of a country main exchange is associated with higher daily returns across 26 exchanges, 1982 to 1997, with annualised magnitudes that look large because daily basis points compound in the telling. Subsequent scrutiny has raised multiple testing, data handling, and out of sample decay concerns, and the effect, if present, is a few basis points at most and untradable after costs. Grade: **fragile**, retained in this study as a teaching example of how a plausible mood mechanism plus many cities plus many weather variables can produce a beautiful false positive. Weather will not appear in the propositions.

*Sports sentiment.* Edmans, Garcia, and Norli (2007), "Sports Sentiment and Stock Returns," *Journal of Finance* 62(4): 1967-1998, find that national team losses in major international soccer tournaments are followed by economically large negative next day returns in the losing country market, on the order of minus 38 basis points in their headline estimate, with no symmetric gain after wins, an asymmetry consistent with loss aversion. The study is well executed and the asymmetry is theoretically attractive; later work debates magnitude, modern sample persistence, and whether the effect survives as markets and tournament trading have changed. Grade: **moderate to fragile**, real as evidence that mood shocks can move prices, not usable as an engine input for a United States equity book beyond a qualitative reminder.

*The Super Bowl indicator and calendar folklore.* The claim that an NFC or original league winner predicts a rising year, and its cousins, are data mined coincidences with no mechanism and no survival value once their selection process is understood. Grade: **debunked as prediction**, useful only as the limiting case that any long history contains patterns that fit perfectly and mean nothing, which is why Section 5 requires kill conditions written in advance.

*Closed end fund discounts.* Lee, Shleifer, and Thaler (1991), "Investor Sentiment and the Closed End Fund Puzzle," *Journal of Finance* 46(1): 75-109, argue discounts move with small investor sentiment, since discounts widen and narrow together and funds issue when discounts are small. Later work complicates the pure sentiment reading with fees, liquidity, and tax explanations for parts of the average discount, while the comovement and issuance timing patterns remain hard to explain without a sentiment like common factor. Grade: **moderate** as a sentiment barometer (it survives as a Baker and Wurgler input), **contested** as a complete theory of the discount level.

*VIX and fear gauges.* The CBOE Volatility Index is an options implied volatility measure, not a sentiment survey, and conflating the two is a category error this engine must not commit. VIX is a price: it embeds a variance risk premium, so its level mixes expected volatility with the price of insurance (Bollerslev, Tauchen, and Zhou, 2009, on the variance risk premium and return predictability). Whaley (2000) popularised the fear gauge label. Empirically, VIX spikes coincide with capitulation episodes and high VIX levels are followed on average by above average equity returns over subsequent months in United States history, with the same small number of independent episodes problem as every extreme zone claim (the deep spikes are 1998, 2008 to 2009, 2011, 2015, 2018, 2020, and 2022 in modern data, a sample of about seven, not seventy). Grade: VIX as a measure of the price of insurance and of stress: **robust**. VIX extreme thresholds as precise bottom timing rules: **fragile**. Provenance: VIX published from 1993 on the old construction (now VXO) and from 2003 in the current model free form with history recalculated to 1990; anything before 1990 is a RECONSTRUCTION, and the 2003 methodology break must be flagged on long charts.

*Margin debt.* FINRA and NYSE margin statistics show margin debt rising into the 2000, 2007, and 2021 peaks and contracting sharply in the breaks, a Minsky consistent leverage footprint (Section 2.3). As a level it trends with market capitalisation and must be normalised (by market value or GDP) before any percentile language is honest. Normalised margin leverage at extremes is a fragility state variable, not a timing variable: it stayed extreme for long stretches before each peak. Grade: **moderate** as fragility, **fragile** as timing.


## 3.10 Sentiment at the great extremes: the long record

Extremes are where sentiment theories earn their keep or are exposed, because at extremes the mechanisms of Sections 1 and 2 operate at full strength and leave records in many independent series at once. Each episode below is told the same way: what the backfilled published record honestly contains, which mechanisms are directly visible in it, and what an engine calibrated on full history should learn. Numbers are given only where they are standard in the published record. Gauge values that no one computed at the time are labelled RECONSTRUCTIONS.

**1929.** The Dow Jones Industrial Average peaked on 3 September 1929 at 381.17 and fell 12.8 percent on 28 October and 11.7 percent on 29 October. The honest sentiment record is indirect: brokers loans (the margin lending of the era) had grown to roughly 8.5 billion dollars by October 1929 in Federal Reserve and contemporary reporting, investment trusts traded at large premiums to net asset value (a closed end fund premium, the mirror image of the Section 3.9 discount, and direct evidence of speculative demand for leveraged equity exposure), and newspaper tone archives show the new era narrative constellation in full epidemic form. White (1990), "The Stock Market Boom and Crash of 1929 Revisited," *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 4(2): 67-83, remains the balanced scholarly account: valuation was high on contemporary dividend based measures, the boom had real earnings growth beneath it early on, and the late stage was increasingly speculative and credit funded. Mechanisms visible: Minsky migration funded by broker loans, extrapolation, narrative epidemic, then forced liquidation as loans were called. Engine lesson: the leverage and premium series, not any mood survey, are the transportable gauges. Where a modern analogue of a premium or a leverage series exists, backfill it to whatever start it honestly has and let 1929 discipline its extreme zone through the analogous historical episodes that do have full data.

**1987.** On 19 October 1987 the Dow fell 22.6 percent in one session, still the largest one day percentage decline in its history. There was no commensurate fundamental news that day. The Brady Commission report (1988) and subsequent scholarship assign a central amplifying role to portfolio insurance, a rules based selling strategy that sold index futures mechanically as prices fell, in effect a pre committed positive feedback seller, interacting with illiquidity and with futures and cash market dislocation. Shiller (1987) survey evidence, collected from investors immediately after the crash and reported in his crash questionnaires, found that investors cited the decline itself, and anxiety about further decline, rather than any specific news item, as their reason for selling, which is higher order belief dynamics (Section 2.1) documented in real time. Mechanisms visible: mechanical feedback selling (the institutional ancestor of modern volatility targeting de leveraging), beauty contest collapse, liquidity evaporation. There is no VIX as published for 1987; implied volatility values quoted for the episode are RECONSTRUCTIONS. Engine lesson: the most dangerous seller is not the frightened human but the rule that must sell. Positioning convexity, developed in Section 4.1, exists as a concept because of 1987.

**2000.** The Nasdaq Composite peaked on 10 March 2000 at 5048.62. The backfilled record here is rich and multi series: Shiller cyclically adjusted price to earnings ratio for the broad United States market peaked at about 44 in December 1999, the highest in its history back to the nineteenth century (source: Shiller online data, free, revised as earnings revise, so the live 1999 vintage differs slightly from the current file and the current file value is the honest one to quote with that caveat); average first day IPO returns reached roughly 65 percent in 1999 to 2000 (Ritter data, Section 3.5); equity mutual fund inflows set records in early 2000, peaking almost exactly with prices, the performance chasing signature of Section 3.4 at market scale; margin debt peaked in March 2000 in FINRA and NYSE statistics. The subsequent decline took the Nasdaq down about 78 percent peak to trough by October 2002, while the broad economy experienced only a mild recession, the Jorda, Schularick, and Taylor (2015) point that unlevered equity bubbles destroy wealth but levered credit bubbles destroy systems. Mechanisms visible: every Section 1 bias at once, plus supply side response (a record IPO and seasoned issuance wave selling overvalued paper to the demand, Section 3.5). Engine lesson: when valuation, issuance, flows, and leverage extremes coincide, the joint extreme carries information that no single series extreme carries. Coincidence counting across independent families is a design principle carried into Section 5.

**2008.** The crisis is the Minsky episode of the modern record. Its sentiment history runs in two phases that a single dial would merge disastrously. In the long build (2003 to 2007), the operative sentiment was complacency: credit spreads compressed, covenant quality deteriorated, housing narrative prevalence was epidemic, and measured volatility was low while fragility accumulated, exactly the stability breeds instability migration of Section 2.3. In the break (September to November 2008 and again into March 2009), the operative mechanics were forced: the VIX closed at 80.86 on 20 November 2008, its highest close under the modern methodology until 2020; AAII bearishness reached the mid 50 percent range at its crisis extremes in readings of that era; equity fund outflows were persistent; and fire sale comovement (Section 1.9) dominated name specific news. The broad market bottomed on 9 March 2009, months after the worst sentiment readings, which is the standing refutation of extreme equals bottom timing rules: the extreme zone was entered in October 2008 and the market fell for five more months, with the S and P 500 losing roughly a further quarter of its value after its first VIX extreme. Mechanisms visible: the full sequence, complacency, migration, moment, spiral. Engine lesson: extremes mark regimes, not dates. Any proposition in Section 5 that converts an extreme into a dated turning call is written to be tested against this episode in backtest and to be graded on duration and path, not on a single day hit.

**2020.** The Covid crash compressed a full sentiment cycle into weeks. From its 19 February 2020 closing high, the S and P 500 fell about 34 percent to its 23 March 2020 close, the fastest decline of that depth from a peak in its history. The VIX closed at 82.69 on 16 March 2020, exceeding the 2008 closing record, a comparison that is valid because both values are under the modern methodology. Baker, Bloom, Davis, and Terry (2020), in their analysis of the unprecedented stock market reaction to Covid, document that no previous infectious disease outbreak, including the 1918 influenza, produced a remotely comparable United States market reaction, and attribute the difference to government restriction and service sector exposure news, a reminder that even the purest panic episode has a fundamental information core that sentiment amplifies rather than replaces. The recovery was equally fast, powered by policy response and by a retail participation surge that set up the next episode. Mechanisms visible: panic mechanics and liquidity spiral in March, then attention and FOMO machinery at scale within months. Engine lesson: cycle speed is itself regime dependent (compare Section 2.8). Calibration windows must include 2020 or every speed parameter in the engine will be biased slow, and percentile bands computed without 2020 will call ordinary stress unprecedented.

**2021.** The meme episode is the first great extreme in which coordination was explicit, public, and measurable in near real time. GameStop, a heavily shorted retailer (short interest above 100 percent of float in reported figures of the era, possible because lent shares are re counted, and itself a positioning convexity datum), rose from under 20 dollars in early January 2021 to an intraday high of 483 dollars on 28 January 2021, pre split prices, before broker restrictions on opening purchases, imposed under clearing collateral pressure, broke the move. The United States Securities and Exchange Commission staff report on the episode (2021) documents the roles of social media attention, retail order flow, short covering, and options market maker hedging (a gamma feedback loop in which call buying forced dealer buying of the underlying, positioning convexity in its purest modern form). The broader 2021 complex showed the full constellation simultaneously: record retail call buying, SPAC issuance at multiples of all previous history, IPO first day returns elevated, margin debt at records in nominal terms, and survey and flow extremes across families. The deflation of 2022 then demonstrated the reverse asymmetry: speculative, high sentiment beta segments (Section 2.6) fell far more than the broad index, exactly the Baker and Wurgler cross sectional prediction operating live. Mechanisms visible: explicit coordination replacing cascades, FOMO scoreboard dynamics (Section 1.8), dealer positioning convexity, issuance response, sentiment beta in the unwind. Engine lesson: in the modern market the fastest sentiment transmission runs through options positioning and social coordination, both measurable with free or held data (CBOE volume, exchange short interest on its publication lag, search and mention volume), and both capable of completing a full cycle inside a month. An engine sampled monthly would have missed the entire episode. Sampling frequency is a theory choice.

**Summary of the extremes.** Across six episodes spanning 92 years, the constants are leverage or mechanical feedback on the way down, issuance and flow extremes near tops, narrative epidemics as the transmission story, and the failure of any single gauge extreme to date the turn. The variables are speed (weeks in 2020 and 2021, years in 1929 and 2008 build up) and the lead series (broker loans then, options positioning now). A calibrated engine is therefore built as a panel of family gauges with coincidence and fragility logic on top, not as a single fear and greed dial. Single dial products exist because they are easy to display. This study regards them as a cautionary example.

# 4. The forecasting question: predicting what the crowd will think next

Everything before this section is about what sentiment is and what it has done. This section asks the harder question the engine actually poses: can a future state of crowd belief be forecast, by what route, with what evidence, and how would anyone know if the forecast was any good. The section is organised by a three way distinction that the engine must enforce in its own displays, because most sentiment products blur exactly these lines.

*Measurement* is a statement about now: the current percentile of a gauge, the current positioning, the current narrative prevalence. Measurement can be excellent or terrible, but it is not a forecast and must never be scored as one or displayed in forecast language.

*Extrapolation* is a statement about soon that assumes persistence or mechanical continuation: flows will continue because mandates and habits continue them, a narrative will keep spreading because its contagion rate currently exceeds its recovery rate, forced selling will continue while constraints keep binding. Extrapolation is a real forecast, but a conditional and usually short horizon one, and its skill comes from mechanics, not from insight into belief change.

*Genuine prediction* is a statement about later that requires forecasting a change in belief itself: the crowd will become fearful, the narrative will saturate and turn, positioning will unwind. This is the claim class with the thinnest evidence in the entire literature and the class where intellectual honesty is most often abandoned. The engine may issue such predictions, but only into the live ledger, only with base rates attached, and only under the scoring discipline of Section 4.5.

## 4.1 Positioning convexity: forecasting the forced, not the feelings

The single most forecastable element in crowd dynamics is not what people will think. It is what some holders will be forced to do at specified prices, because forcing functions are written down in margin agreements, risk rules, option contracts, and redemption terms.

**Dealer gamma and options positioning.** When customers buy large amounts of short dated call options, the dealers who sold them hedge by buying the underlying as it rises and selling as it falls, a positive feedback hedge flow whose size is computable from the open interest distribution (the gamma exposure concept, developed in practitioner work and formalised in research including Ni, Pan, and Poteshman (2008) on hedging demand and Barbon and Buraschi (2021) on dealer gamma and intraday momentum and reversal patterns). In the opposite configuration, dealer hedging is stabilising. The 2021 meme episode (Section 3.10) is the canonical case of the destabilising configuration at single name scale. The forecasting content is genuinely predictive in the narrow sense: given the positioning map, the sign and rough size of hedge flow conditional on a price move can be stated in advance, before the move. That is a forecast of flow conditional on price, not a forecast of price, and the engine must label it exactly that way. Data reality: full open interest by strike is published by exchanges and is available through the options data the desk already holds via its broker and TastyTrade read only routes for current snapshots; historical archives of full chains are the scarcest free resource in this program and are the binding constraint on backtesting this family. Propositions in this family are therefore weighted toward the live ledger.

**Leverage and risk rule convexity.** Volatility targeting, risk parity, value at risk constrained, and leveraged and inverse fund rebalancing flows share a structure: a rise in realised volatility or a fall in price mechanically implies selling of a computable approximate size, and a fall in volatility implies buying. Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2009) supply the theory (Section 1.9); the daily rebalancing arithmetic of leveraged exchange traded funds is documented in Cheng and Madhavan (2009). Forecasts of these flows, conditional on the volatility path, are extrapolations with unusually strong foundations, because the agents have pre committed to the rule. The forecastable object is again flow and fragility (how much selling a further 5 percent decline would mechanically generate), not the decline itself. An engine that publishes a fragility map, here is the estimated forced flow per incremental down move given current leverage and volatility, is making a checkable, useful, honest forecast of the systems response function. That is the highest value forecasting product this study endorses.

**Short interest and crowding.** High short interest is two forecasts in a trench coat: informed bearish positioning (predicting declines, supported by evidence that heavily shorted stocks underperform on average, for example Desai, Ramesh, Thiagarajan, and Balachandran (2002)) and squeeze convexity (predicting violent upside if forced covering begins, supported by the 2021 episode and by the borrow cost literature). Which dominates depends on borrow cost, recall risk, float, and catalyst proximity. The engine treatment is to report both channels with their conditioning variables, never a net score. Short interest is published by exchanges twice monthly on a lag, free, and its staleness must be displayed; presenting twice monthly lagged data in real time clothing is a provenance violation.

## 4.2 Narrative velocity and saturation

If narratives spread epidemically (Section 2.4), their dynamics have a forecastable shape. In the simplest susceptible, infected, recovered logic, prevalence growth is proportional to current prevalence times the remaining susceptible share. Early growth is therefore exponential and easy to extrapolate; late growth decelerates as the susceptible pool (people who have not yet heard, or heard and not yet acted) is exhausted; and the price relevant variable is not prevalence itself but the flow of new adopters, because buying is done by new adopters, not by the already convinced. This yields the central narrative forecasting claim of this study, stated so it can die: **price pressure from a narrative peaks near the point of maximum growth in prevalence, which precedes peak prevalence, which precedes peak price whenever valuation overshoot requires continued new buying.** The dot com constellation fits this shape in search and media archives; the housing constellation fits it at lower frequency; meme episodes fit it at very high frequency, with mention growth peaking days before price in the best documented cases, though the published lead lag evidence here is thinner and more episode specific than the theory, and this claim is graded **plausible mechanism, moderate direct evidence, carried into Section 5 as a testable proposition rather than asserted as fact**.

Saturation has measurable signatures beyond raw counts: the marginal new content becomes derivative (the story is retold to audiences already holding it), the audience composition shifts from specialists to generalists (a narrative that reaches general news front pages and late night comedy has, by construction, little susceptible audience left in the investing public), search queries shift from discovery phrasing to confirmation and price checking phrasing, and counter narratives begin to achieve comparable velocity. None of these signatures is a number by itself. Together, pre registered as a saturation checklist, they convert narrative watching from journalism into a gradeable instrument. The freezing rule of Section 2.4 applies with full force: narrative definitions, source corpora, and the checklist are fixed before grading begins, and any later amendment starts a new, separately graded series. Amended definitions applied backward are backfill of method, which this program treats with the same suspicion as backfill of results.

## 4.3 Attention leading flows, options as belief aggregates, and prediction markets

**Attention leading flows.** Section 1.5 established that attention predicts retail buying. The forecasting version is a lead lag claim: search and mention volume today predicts order imbalance and flow over the following days, because the consideration set formed by attention is acted on with a lag as accounts are funded and decisions are taken. Da, Engelberg, and Gao (2011) find search volume predicts price pressure over the following two weeks, which implies the flow channel operates over days, not only contemporaneously. This is extrapolation grade forecasting: the belief (interest) is already measured; the forecast is that measured interest converts to flow at a historically estimable rate. It is among the best supported short horizon forecasts available to the engine, with the standing caveat that attention is directionless until tone or imbalance supplies the sign (Section 1.5), and with a modern caveat that platform data breaks (Section 3.8) can sever the series without warning.

**Options markets as belief aggregates.** An options chain is a priced distribution. Implied volatility skew, term structure, and the risk neutral densities recoverable from option prices (Breeden and Litzenberger, 1978) state what the market will pay for each future state, which is a belief aggregate filtered through risk aversion and hedging demand. Two disciplined uses follow. First, the gap between risk neutral and physical probabilities is itself information: when downside states are priced far above their historical base rates, either fear or hedging demand is extreme, and the variance and skew risk premium literature (Bollerslev, Tauchen, and Zhou, 2009; Carr and Wu, 2009) shows these premia carry return information at monthly horizons. Second, changes in the aggregate distribution forecast changes in positioning better than they forecast changes in price: a rapid steepening of skew with rising open interest in downside strikes is a measured statement that belief, or at least insurance demand, has already moved, and the forecastable part is the unwind or persistence of that positioning, not the crash it insures against. The standing error to avoid is reading implied probabilities as forecasts of events. They are prices of events, systematically biased by the premium, and an engine that displays them as probabilities without the premium caveat is misinforming its reader.

**Prediction markets.** Where a prediction market exists on a relevant event (elections, policy decisions, macro releases on event platforms), its prices are the best calibrated belief aggregates available anywhere in this study. Wolfers and Zitzewitz (2004), "Prediction Markets," *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 18(2): 107-126, and Berg, Nelson, and Rietz (2008) on the Iowa Electronic Markets document long run calibration that outperforms polls and most pundit aggregation, with known pathologies: favourite longshot bias at extreme prices, thin market manipulation attempts that decay quickly, and horizon effects. For the engine, prediction markets are an input, not a competitor: on overlapping questions (will policy ease, will a contested corporate event complete), a liquid prediction market price should anchor the belief state, and any engine view that departs from it should be required to name its edge. Where no liquid market exists, which is most sentiment questions, the engine is on its own and must say so. Provenance: major event platforms publish prices freely in display form; programmatic history varies by platform and must be captured contemporaneously by the engine if it is to be graded later, another series that exists only if the ledger discipline starts now.

**Forecasting the forecasters.** The Keynes structure (Section 2.1) implies a final route: forecast revisions of beliefs, not beliefs. Survey expectations are revised toward recent returns (Section 1.7); analyst expectations are revised toward recent earnings and price action with well documented sluggishness; positioning is revised toward recent performance through flows (Section 3.4). A model of the revision process, which is mechanical and estimated on long backfilled histories, can therefore forecast next month survey level or next quarter flow concentration better than it can forecast returns. The engine should grade itself on belief forecasts (did bearishness rise as predicted) separately from return forecasts (did prices fall). Belief forecasting is the engines home turf, is more winnable than return forecasting, and is independently useful to a discretionary reader deciding how crowded the room she is entering has become. Conflating the two scoreboards is how sentiment programs delude themselves: they claim belief insight and quietly grade on returns, or claim return insight and quietly grade on nothing.


## 4.4 Contrary indicator or momentum input: the conditions, stated as a decision rule

The literature resolves the apparent contradiction (sentiment predicts continuation in some studies, reversal in others) into four conditioning variables. An engine reading should be classified by these variables before any directional language is attached.

**Horizon.** At horizons of days to a few months, measured sentiment and the positioning it creates tend to continue: flows persist, feedback buying operates, underreaction dominates (Sections 1.7, 2.7, 3.4). At horizons of one to three years, extreme sentiment tends to reverse: extrapolation has carried beliefs beyond any plausible fundamental, issuance has responded, and mean reversion of the sentiment factor dominates (Sections 2.5, 3.1, 3.5). The crossover zone is regime dependent and is where most practical errors occur. Any engine output that does not print its horizon next to its direction is incomplete by design.

**Crowding and capacity.** Sentiment is a momentum input while the believers still have capacity to act: cash to deploy, borrow available, mandates unfilled, susceptible audience unconverted (Section 4.2). It becomes a contrary input as capacity exhausts: leverage at percentile extremes, cash balances at lows in survey data, narrative prevalence saturated, issuance flooding the demand. Capacity variables are therefore not confirming decorations on a sentiment reading; they are the switch that determines its sign. This is why the extremes panel of Section 3.10 insists on coincidence across families: the contrary case is made by exhaustion evidence, not by enthusiasm evidence alone.

**Forcing.** While forced flows dominate (margin, risk rules, redemptions, dealer hedging in the destabilising configuration), sentiment direction continues regardless of valuation or survey levels, and contrary positioning is run over (Sections 1.9, 4.1). Contrary readings acquire force when forcing is spent: forced sellers exhausted, volatility falling from extremes so risk rules stop demanding sales, hedging configurations flipped to stabilising. The practical sequence at bottoms in the historical record is forcing, exhaustion, then reversal, and the middle term is the only one an engine can hope to detect in real time, through flow and volatility signatures rather than through price hope.

**Who holds it and how hard it is to arbitrage.** The same aggregate sentiment level implies continuation in the broad index (deep arbitrage, cash flow anchors) and reversal risk concentrated in the high sentiment beta tail (Section 2.6). Directional language must therefore always carry a scope: index, segment, or named tail. A bearish sentiment call on speculative segments can be right in a rising index year, as 2022 demonstrated in reverse, and an engine graded only on the index will mislearn its own best skill.

Stated as one rule for the terminal page: **sentiment continues until capacity or forcing says otherwise, and reverses when exhaustion across at least two independent families coincides with the forcing flow abating, at a horizon of months, in the high sentiment beta segment first.** Every clause of that sentence is a place where live data can prove it wrong, which is the point; it enters Section 5 as propositions, not as a slogan.

## 4.5 Scoring discipline: how the engine will know if it knows anything

A forecasting claim is only as good as its grading, and grading is where sentiment research, academic and commercial alike, most often cheats. The engine adopts the following rules, which are stated here so that any future relaxation is visible as a relaxation.

First, pre registration. Every forecast states, at issue: the variable, the instrument or segment, the horizon, the threshold or distribution claimed, and the base rate from backfilled history. Forecasts are timestamped into the live ledger before the horizon begins. The ledger is never backfilled (provenance rules). Amendments create new forecasts; they do not edit old ones.

Second, proper scoring. Directional claims are graded with Brier scores against the base rate forecaster who always predicts the historical frequency; distributional claims, where the engine issues them, with continuous ranked probability scores or log scores. A forecaster who cannot beat the base rate forecaster on a proper score over a pre stated sample does not have skill on that question, whatever its hit rate on selected episodes. Calibration is reported alongside discrimination: an engine that says 80 percent and is right half the time is worse, for a decision maker, than one that says 60 percent and is right 60 percent of the time.

Third, episode honesty. Financial time series contain far fewer independent observations than their day counts suggest. Grades are reported with the number of independent episodes (non overlapping horizons, distinct regimes) as well as the number of observations, and any claim resting on fewer than ten independent episodes is labelled preliminary in display. Section 3 is full of famous results resting on seven or fewer true extremes; the engine will not repeat that sin in its own name.

Fourth, separation of scoreboards. Belief forecasts (survey levels, flow concentration, narrative prevalence, positioning) and return forecasts are graded on separate boards (Section 4.3). Fragility forecasts (forced flow conditional on a move) are graded on the accuracy of the conditional map when moves occur, not on whether moves occurred.

Fifth, kill conditions in advance. Every proposition in Section 5 carries the observation that would retire it. A proposition that survives is one that has faced its kill condition repeatedly and not met it. A proposition that is never allowed to die is marketing.

# 5. Testable propositions

Each proposition below is falsifiable as written. Fields: the claim; the horizon; the data, restricted to free and public sources or keys the desk already holds; the grading board (live ledger, graded only forward from engine launch, or history backtest, graded on backfilled inputs and labelled a backtest and a RECONSTRUCTION wherever reported); and the kill condition, the observation that retires the proposition. Backtest propositions may be run on history immediately for calibration. Passing a backtest earns a proposition the right to enter the live ledger. It earns nothing else, and no backtest result may ever be reported in language that implies a live forecast was made.

**P1. Survey extrapolation, belief board.** Next month AAII bullish share and Michigan based expected return proxies will be better predicted by trailing three month market returns than by any fundamental release in the same window, in the sense that a trailing return only model achieves lower out of sample error than a releases only model. Horizon: monthly, rolling. Data: AAII archive (free, from 1987), Michigan Surveys of Consumers (free), index prices. Board: history backtest for calibration, then live ledger. Kill: trailing return only model loses to the releases only model on pre registered error over 36 consecutive live months.

**P2. Survey contrary content, return board.** When the AAII bull bear spread enters its top or bottom decile against full history from 1987, subsequent 12 month broad index returns will be lower after top decile readings than after bottom decile readings, by a margin exceeding the unconditional 12 month mean difference of zero. Horizon: 12 months, overlapping observations episode adjusted. Data: as P1. Board: history backtest (episodes are few; the backtest must report the independent episode count, expected to be under ten, and label the result preliminary), then live ledger. Kill: the sign reverses in the live ledger over the first five independent live episodes, or the backtest spread is fully explained by trailing returns alone in a pre registered control.

**P3. Baker and Wurgler cross section.** In months following top tercile readings of the Baker and Wurgler sentiment index, a high sentiment beta basket (small, young, high volatility, unprofitable, non dividend paying) will underperform a low sentiment beta basket over the next six months, and the reverse after bottom tercile readings. Horizon: six months. Data: Wurgler published index (free), CRSP based characteristic baskets as published in public factor libraries (Ken French data library, free). Board: history backtest from 1965 (RECONSTRUCTION for early decades, per Section 3.2), then live ledger. Kill: the conditional spread is zero or wrong signed over any rolling ten year backtest window after 1990, or over the first six independent live episodes.

**P4. Aggregate timing humility.** No sentiment composite the engine builds will achieve a monthly out of sample R squared for broad index returns above 2 percent sustained over any five year live window. This is a proposition about the engine itself, entered deliberately as a guardrail. Horizon: five year windows. Data: engine ledger. Board: live ledger only; history cannot test a claim about this engine. Kill: a pre registered composite sustains above 2 percent monthly out of sample R squared over a full five year live window with no construction changes inside the window, in which case the guardrail is retired and the composite is promoted with its full record displayed.

**P5. Put and call extremes predict variance, not direction.** Equity put and call ratio readings above their trailing three year 95th percentile will be followed by 20 trading day realised volatility above its unconditional median in at least 60 percent of episodes, while their directional implication for 20 day index returns will be within noise of zero once trailing five day returns are controlled. Horizon: 20 trading days. Data: CBOE daily statistics (free). Board: history backtest with regime flags at the 2020 and 2022 structure breaks (Section 3.3), then live ledger. Kill: directional content survives the trailing return control with stable sign across both the pre 2020 and post 2022 regimes, in which case the proposition is replaced by the directional version that earned it.

**P6. Signed option volume, single names.** Among liquid optionable names, buyer initiated put heavy volume (top quintile put to call by signed volume where classifiable through held broker data) predicts negative abnormal returns over the following five sessions, in the information direction of Pan and Poteshman (2006), not the contrary direction. Horizon: five sessions. Data: options volume through Alpaca and TastyTrade read only routes already held. Board: live ledger primarily; historical signed volume archives are not freely available at the required quality, and any partial history test must be labelled with its coverage. Kill: the live spread is zero or contrary signed over the first 200 qualifying name weeks.

**P7. Attention converts to flow.** Abnormal search and mention volume for a ticker (top decile against its own trailing year) predicts positive retail order imbalance over the following five sessions, and, where retail imbalance cannot be observed directly, predicts volume and volatility elevation over the same window. Horizon: five sessions. Data: search trend proxies and platform mention counts from free sources, prices and volume from Alpaca. Board: history backtest where search history honestly reaches (mid 2000s onward for search trends), then live ledger, with platform break flags per Section 3.8. Kill: attention spikes show no forward flow, volume, or volatility content beyond the spike day itself over 100 live episodes.

**P8. Attention pressure reverses.** Names in P7 that also show extreme herding signatures (many small trades, leaderboard or trending list appearance where observable) will show negative average returns over the following 20 sessions, in the direction of Barber, Huang, Odean, and Schwarz (2022). Horizon: 20 sessions. Data: as P7 plus trade size proxies from held feeds. Board: live ledger; the original platform dataset is no longer published, so no honest full history backtest exists and none will be simulated. Kill: the 20 session average is zero or positive over the first 50 qualifying live episodes.

**P9. Dumb money, modern form.** Category level flow extremes (top decile net inflows as a share of category assets, ICI summary data and free exchange traded fund flow aggregates) predict underperformance of the category relative to the broad index over the following 12 months, at a size smaller than the Frazzini and Lamont (2008) in sample estimates. Horizon: 12 months. Data: ICI (free summaries), issuer published fund flows where free. Board: history backtest from the 1990s, then live ledger. Kill: flow extreme categories match or outperform over the backtest post 2010 window and the first four independent live episodes combined, in which case flows revert to pure measurement status in the engine.

**P10. Issuance response.** Hot issuance windows (IPO count and average first day return, and equity share in new issues, each in top quartile against backfilled history) will be followed by below median subsequent 12 month returns for the newly issued cohort against matched seasoned firms, and by rising issuance volume for at least two further quarters as supply chases demand. Horizon: 12 months for returns, two quarters for supply persistence. Data: Ritter IPO data (free), Federal Reserve and SIFMA issuance summaries where free. Board: history backtest to 1980 for IPOs and to 1928 for equity share (early values are RECONSTRUCTIONS from published aggregates), then live ledger. Kill: the issued cohort matches its benchmarks over two consecutive hot windows in backtest or live record.

**P11. Media tone pressure and reversal.** A finance dictionary pessimism score (Loughran and McDonald word lists, free) computed on a frozen corpus of market columns and front page financial coverage will show the Tetlock (2007) shape: extreme pessimism days predict negative next day index returns and positive cumulative returns over days two to five, with the effect at least twice as large in NBER recession months as in expansion months, per Garcia (2013). Horizon: one and five days. Data: freely accessible news archives and feeds; the corpus definition is frozen at engine build and any corpus change starts a new series. Board: history backtest on the frozen corpus where archives honestly reach, then live ledger. Kill: the recession interaction is absent or reversed in both backtest and the first live recession, whenever one occurs; the proposition remains ungraded on that clause until a live recession exists, and must display that status rather than borrow the backtest grade.

**P12. Narrative growth peaks before price.** For named, pre registered narratives tracked by frozen phrase sets in search and news corpora, the growth rate of narrative prevalence will peak before prevalence peaks, and in episodes where the narrative is attached to a tradable asset or segment, the associated price peak will occur after the prevalence growth peak in at least 60 percent of episodes. Horizon: episode based, days to quarters by narrative class, pre registered per narrative. Data: search trends, free news counts, prices. Board: history backtest on archived narrative episodes (housing 2005 to 2007, dot com 1998 to 2000 in available corpora, labelled RECONSTRUCTIONS of prevalence), then live ledger for all future narratives. Kill: growth peaks coincide with or follow price peaks in the majority of the first ten graded episodes across boards.

**P13. Saturation checklist.** Episodes in which at least three of the pre registered saturation signatures of Section 4.2 are present (derivative marginal content, generalist audience migration, confirmation phrasing shift in search, counter narrative velocity parity) will be followed by declining narrative prevalence within one narrative generation (pre registered per class) in at least 65 percent of episodes. Horizon: one narrative generation. Data: as P12. Board: live ledger only for the checklist in its frozen form; historical checklist application is a RECONSTRUCTION and is reported as exploratory. Kill: hit rate below 50 percent over the first 12 live episodes.

**P14. Positioning convexity map.** The engine fragility map (estimated forced flow per incremental 5 percent index decline, built from leveraged fund assets, margin debt normalised by market value, volatility targeting exposure proxies, and dealer gamma configuration where computable) will rank realised episodes correctly: declines that occurred with a high fragility reading will show larger volume, larger correlation compression toward one, and larger volatility response per point of decline than declines with a low fragility reading. Horizon: episode based. Data: FINRA margin statistics (free, monthly), fund asset data from issuer publications, options open interest via held routes, prices. Board: history backtest in coarse monthly form (fragility inputs are monthly for much of history, and the backtest must not pretend to daily precision it does not have), then live ledger at full frequency. Kill: fragility readings show no association with decline mechanics across the backtest episodes of 2000 to 2002, 2008, 2020, and 2022 and the first three live decline episodes.

**P15. Dealer gamma configuration.** On days when estimated aggregate dealer gamma for the index is in its most negative quintile (dealers hedging in the destabilising direction), intraday price moves will show greater continuation (positive autocorrelation of 30 minute returns) than on most positive quintile days. Horizon: intraday, graded monthly in aggregate. Data: options open interest and prices via held TastyTrade read only and broker routes, captured contemporaneously; no free historical archive supports an honest backtest, so none is claimed. Board: live ledger only. Kill: the continuation difference is absent or reversed over the first 60 graded days in each configuration class.

**P16. Skew is a price, not a probability.** Displayed downside implied probabilities from the index options chain will exceed realised event frequencies over matched horizons (the premium bias of Section 4.3), by a margin that widens in top quintile VIX regimes. Horizon: 30 days rolling. Data: index options via held routes, realised outcomes from prices. Board: live ledger; historical chain archives are not freely held, so historical versions of this test run only on implied volatility summary series (VIX and SKEW, CBOE, free; SKEW from 1990 as published, with methodology notes) and are labelled partial. Kill: implied downside frequencies match realised frequencies within sampling error over the first 24 live months, in which case the premium caveat is removed from displays as empirically unjustified for this market in this era.

**P17. Prediction market anchor.** On questions where a liquid prediction market and the engine both hold views (policy and scheduled event outcomes relevant to positioning), the market price will be better calibrated (lower Brier score) than the engine view over rolling samples, and engine departures from the market price will not add accuracy. Horizon: per event. Data: published prediction market prices, captured contemporaneously into the ledger. Board: live ledger only; uncaptured historical market prices cannot be graded honestly after the fact and will not be reconstructed for grading. Kill: the engine view beats the market on Brier score over the first 40 jointly graded events, in which case the anchor rule is revisited with the record displayed.

**P18. Channel dependent asymmetry.** The speed asymmetry of Section 2.8 will hold conditionally: index declines accompanied by rising margin contraction and volatility rule selling signatures will reach a given drawdown depth faster than rises of the same depth, while social coordination episodes (P12 narratives with explicit coordination signatures) will rise faster than they fall in their first phase. Horizon: episode based. Data: prices, FINRA margin (monthly, flagged for frequency), narrative corpora. Board: history backtest on index episodes from 1928 (prices are the one series that honestly reaches; Dow daily history is published and free in standard research form), then live ledger. Kill: no speed difference by channel across the backtest episode set at conventional significance with episode counts reported, or the live record reverses the backtest.

**P19. Capitulation is not a date.** Volume and volatility capitulation signatures (joint top decile readings of index volume against its own history, VIX, and down day breadth) will identify zones, not bottoms: in at least half of historical and live episodes, the index will trade at least 5 percent below the first capitulation signature close within the following 60 sessions, as it did after October 2008. Horizon: 60 sessions. Data: prices, volume, CBOE VIX (free). Board: history backtest to 1990 on modern VIX and to 1928 on volume and breadth alone with the reduced gauge labelled, then live ledger. Kill: first signatures mark final lows within 5 percent in more than 70 percent of graded episodes, in which case the engine may, with the record shown, upgrade capitulation to a timing input. This proposition is written to protect the reader from the engine, and its kill condition is deliberately the hardest in the list to meet.

**P20. FOMO cross section.** Speculative episodes will concentrate as Section 1.8 predicts: assets combining no cash flow anchor, visible scoreboard salience, and low friction retail access will show larger attention, flow, and new holder responses to a given peer gain shock than anchored, institutionally held assets, by an ordering measurable across episodes. Horizon: episode based. Data: attention and flow proxies as P7 and P9, asset characteristics from public filings and free reference data. Board: history backtest on the 2020 to 2021 episode complex and earlier manias where proxies honestly exist, then live ledger. Kill: the predicted ordering is absent in the majority of graded episodes, in which case the FOMO decomposition is retired to narrative status and removed from engine logic.

**P21. Disposition overhang.** A capital gains overhang proxy (share weighted distance of the holder base from estimated reference prices, built from price and volume history in the Grinblatt and Han (2005) form) will add predictive content for cross sectional momentum beyond price momentum alone in large backtests, and will add nothing reliable at index level. Horizon: one to six months cross sectional; index clause graded annually. Data: prices and volume (Alpaca, free public histories). Board: history backtest; this is a pure history proposition until it passes, and enters the live ledger only on passing. Kill: no incremental content beyond price momentum in two independent backtest decades, or incremental content appears at index level in backtest, either of which falsifies half the claim as written, and the proposition is graded as failed, not edited into a survivor.

**P22. Coincidence beats single gauges.** A pre registered coincidence rule (extremes in at least two of the four families: surveys, flows and leverage, issuance, options stress) will identify subsequent 12 month periods of below median returns for the high sentiment beta segment more reliably than the best single family gauge alone, in backtest and live record combined grading with boards reported separately. Horizon: 12 months. Data: all of the above. Board: history backtest to the start of the shortest family in the rule as configured (the rule must print that start date; configurations reaching to 1965 use surveys in newsletter form and are flagged), then live ledger. Kill: the coincidence rule fails to beat its best component on the pre registered metric in backtest, in which case the engine ships single family gauges with no composite, and the composite idea is recorded as killed by its own test.

## Closing note to the builder

If this volume leaves a builder with one habit, it should be suspicion of the single number. The crowd is real, its psychology is measurable, and some of its future is forecastable: the forced part mechanically, the attentive part by conversion, the narrative part by epidemic shape, and the belief part by the revision process. The return part is forecastable least, latest, and with the smallest sizes, and every famous case of the opposite claim, examined closely in Section 3, turned out to rest on a short sample, a revised series, a mined specification, or a fund that quietly closed. Build the panel. Backfill the inputs and label the reconstructions. Keep the ledger sacred. Grade beliefs and returns on separate boards. Let propositions die in public. An engine that does those things will know a few true things about what the crowd will think next, and will know that it knows them, which in this field is the rarer achievement.
