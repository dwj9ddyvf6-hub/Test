# Psychology and Sentiment in Markets: The Study

For the owner. Compiled 2026-10-30 from the two source studies in `research_notes/psychology-engine-2026-10-30/` (STUDY-THEORY.md and STUDY-MEASUREMENT.md).

Plain words throughout. No em dashes, by instruction.

## What this study is, and what it is not

This is a study of what market psychology actually does, what can honestly be measured about it, and what would count as predicting what the crowd will think next.

It is not a trading system. It does not size anything, it does not change any desk sleeve or the Tensor engine, and it does not claim that any effect named here is currently tradable after costs.

One asymmetry runs through everything below. Psychology explains a large part of why prices can depart from a narrow fundamental value. It explains far less, on its own, about timing. A reader who keeps that one sentence in mind will read the rest correctly.

## How the grades work

Every major claim carries a grade and a citation.

* **Robust:** found in multiple independent samples, countries or data types, with a mechanism that predicts where the effect should be stronger or weaker, and that pattern actually observed.
* **Moderate:** replicated, but size, sign or horizon depends materially on sample, construction or regime. Usable as a conditional input. Dangerous as a standalone rule.
* **Fragile:** published and plausible, but sensitive to specification, period or multiple testing corrections. Treat as a hypothesis, not a fact.
* **Debunked or failed in its strong form:** the headline claim did not survive direct replication, out of sample tests or live deployment. A weaker cousin may still be true and is named where it exists.
* **Unverifiable as stated:** framed so loosely that no observation could refute it. Excluded from the testable propositions.

Three background facts discipline every grade. First, multiple testing is endemic: Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016), *Review of Financial Studies* 29(1), catalogue more than 300 published factors and argue a new factor needs a t statistic above roughly 3 to be credible. Second, broad replication is sobering: Hou, Xue and Zhang (2020), *Review of Financial Studies* 33(5), find a large majority of 452 published anomalies fail under uniform stricter methods, and McLean and Pontiff (2016), *Journal of Finance* 71(1), find published predictors decay by roughly a quarter to a half after publication. Third, almost every sentiment proxy is correlated with past returns, so a variable that merely repackages momentum or reversal will look predictive until those are controlled, and then often collapse. Apply a standing haircut to any published effect size. Assume the live effect, if real, is materially smaller.

## Part 1: The mechanisms that matter

A bias label is not an explanation. Each mechanism below is stated the same way: the mechanism, the footprint it should leave in positioning, the market consequence that follows only if enough capital shares it at the same time, and the boundary where you should stop invoking it.

### 1. Prospect theory and loss aversion

Source: Kahneman and Tversky (1979), *Econometrica* 47(2); formalised for asset prices by Barberis, Huang and Santos (2001), *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 116(1). The often quoted loss aversion number near 2.25 comes from Tversky and Kahneman (1992), *Journal of Risk and Uncertainty* 5. It is a laboratory median, not a constant of human nature.

Four parts do the work. Reference dependence: outcomes are coded as gains and losses against a reference point such as purchase price, a recent high or a round number. Diminishing sensitivity: the first 5 percent matters more than the move from 40 to 45 percent, and in the loss domain the value function turns convex, so a holder deep in a loss prefers a gamble that might restore breakeven. Loss aversion proper: realising a loss closes a mental account at a painful value (Thaler 1985, *Marketing Science* 4(3)). Probability weighting: small probabilities are overweighted, which makes lottery-like payoffs and insurance-like payoffs attractive in the same person at the same time.

Footprint: reluctance to sell below the reference price even when the thesis is broken, averaging down and moving to higher volatility instruments after losses, realising gains early, and demand for skewed payoffs such as deep out of the money options out of proportion to expected return. Aggregated, myopic loss aversion can generate the equity premium puzzle (Benartzi and Thaler 1995, *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 110(1)), and probability weighting alone can price positively skewed stocks low in expected return terms (Barberis and Huang 2008, *American Economic Review* 98(5)).

Boundary: prospect theory predicts conditional behaviour given a reference point and a framing. It does not predict that a market must rise or fall on a given day. An engine that claims loss aversion is high today without saying whose reference point, formed when, has said nothing testable. Grade: robust as a description of individual choice, moderate as a direct market timing input. Market experience reduces some anomalies in field settings (List 2003, *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 118(1)), so do not assume laboratory sizes survive among professionals unchanged.

### 2. Overconfidence and trading frequency

Sources: Barber and Odean (2000), *Journal of Finance* 55(2); Barber and Odean (2001), *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 116(1); Odean (1999), *American Economic Review* 89(5); theory in Daniel, Hirshleifer and Subrahmanyam (1998), *Journal of Finance* 53(6).

Keep three forms separate. Overestimation: believing your expected return or skill is higher than it is. Overplacement: believing you rank higher than you do. Overprecision: believing your information is more precise than it is, shown as confidence intervals that are too narrow. Overprecision has the cleanest trading implication: trade larger, trade more often, diversify less, update less on contrary evidence. Self attribution then protects the error, because wins are credited to skill and losses to bad luck.

Evidence and size: in Barber and Odean (2000), 66,465 households from 1991 to 1996, the most actively trading quintile earned about 11.4 percent per year net against a market return of about 17.9 percent, a gap of roughly 6.5 percentage points per year, done largely by trading costs and poor trade timing. Odean (1999) found the stocks individual investors bought underperformed the stocks they sold over the following year by on the order of 3 percentage points before costs. Men traded about 45 percent more than women in the same sample and reduced net returns by about 0.94 percentage points per year more (Barber and Odean 2001). Taiwanese day trading records show the same direction at large scale (Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean 2009, *Review of Financial Studies* 22(2)).

Footprint: rising turnover without a matching rise in information events, larger position size and higher leverage after winning periods, and options activity concentrated in short dated contracts.

Boundary: turnover is not a pure confidence meter. It also rises with genuine news, lower transaction costs, app design and volatility itself. The zero commission era after 2019 mechanically raised retail turnover. Read turnover only after normalising for cost regime, volatility and news intensity. Grade: robust for the cost of excessive trading, moderate for turnover as a real time sentiment gauge.

### 3. The disposition effect

Sources: Shefrin and Statman (1985), *Journal of Finance* 40(3), who coined the term; Odean (1998), *Journal of Finance* 53(5); Grinblatt and Keloharju (2001), *Journal of Finance* 56(2); Kaustia (2010), *Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis* 45(3).

Mechanism: selling a winner closes a mental account in the pleasant gain domain. Holding a loser keeps the account open and preserves the gamble to breakeven, while selling would force the painful loss to be experienced as realised. Regret and pride add force on both sides.

Evidence: Odean (1998), 10,000 accounts from 1987 to 1993, investors realised gains at a rate roughly 50 percent higher than losses, about 0.148 for gains against 0.098 for losses. The effect weakened in December, when tax loss selling gives an offsetting rational motive, which is itself a good check that the pattern is not a mechanical artefact. The winners sold went on to outperform the losers retained by about 3.4 percentage points over the following year. The effect appears in many countries and in housing (Genesove and Mayer 2001, *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 116(4)).

Market consequence: Grinblatt and Han (2005), *Journal of Financial Economics* 78(2), model how aggregate unrealised capital gains predict future returns. When holders sit on losses, supply is withheld and bad news is incorporated slowly, which contributes to momentum. When holders sit on large gains, eager realisation caps advances. The darker implication is for crashes: the same population that rides moderate losses can flip to capitulation once a pain or margin threshold is crossed, producing abnormally low selling on the way down and abnormally high selling at the bottom.

Grade: robust at the aggregate level as behaviour, weak as a day to day index timing tool, because the distribution of unrealised gains across holders is slow moving and hard to observe without holdings data.

### 4. Herding and information cascades

Sources: Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch (1992), *Journal of Political Economy* 100(5); Banerjee (1992), *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 107(3); Scharfstein and Stein (1990), *American Economic Review* 80(3); Lakonishok, Shleifer and Vishny (1992), *Journal of Financial Economics* 32(1); Chiang and Zheng (2010), *Journal of Banking and Finance* 34(8).

Two mechanisms share one word and must not be confused. In an informational cascade, agents decide in sequence and see predecessors actions but not their private signals. After a few early actions agree, a later agent may rationally follow the crowd against his own signal. Once a cascade starts, private information stops entering the public pool, so the cascade rests on a thin base and is fragile: a small piece of public news can reverse it. No irrationality is required. In reputational herding, a manager who fails conventionally risks less career damage than one who fails unconventionally, which clusters professional holdings even when private views differ. A third pattern looks similar but is not psychology at all: systematic trend following produces correlated buying of what has risen because the rule says so.

Evidence is method dependent. Lakonishok, Shleifer and Vishny (1992) found only modest herding among pension managers by their trade based measure. Return dispersion methods find dispersion compressing in extreme markets in many countries, consistent with herding in stress, but dispersion can also compress when one macro factor dominates returns, which is exactly what happens in crises. That identification problem has never been fully solved and must be stated whenever a dispersion number is shown. Explicit coordination, as in forum organised buying, is not a cascade in the strict sense: participants communicate directly, formation is faster, a narrative is visible, and platform moderation and broker restrictions act as circuit breakers.

Grade: robust as a mechanism, fragile as a single number. Measure the footprints separately: dispersion, flow correlation and positioning correlation, and report disagreement among them.

### 5. Attention-driven buying

Sources: Barber and Odean (2008), *Review of Financial Studies* 21(2); Da, Engelberg and Gao (2011), *Journal of Finance* 66(5); Peng and Xiong (2006), *Journal of Financial Economics* 80(3); Barber, Huang, Odean and Schwarz (2022), *Journal of Finance* 77(6).

Mechanism: buying and selling are asymmetric tasks. A seller can only sell what he holds. A buyer choosing from thousands of stocks faces a search problem, and attention solves it by shrinking the choice set to whatever caught his eye: extreme moves, high volume, headlines, trending lists. Net buying by individuals therefore concentrates in attention grabbing stocks not because they were evaluated as best, but because they entered the consideration set at all. Institutions show the pattern far less.

Evidence: abnormal Google search volume for a ticker predicted higher prices over the following two weeks and partial reversal over the following year in the 2004 to 2008 sample of Da, Engelberg and Gao (2011), stronger for smaller retail held names. On Robinhood data, stocks with extreme herding by platform users earned negative average returns over the following month, on the order of 5 percent in the most extreme events as reported (Barber, Huang, Odean and Schwarz 2022).

The signature sequence, where the theory is working, is: attention spike, retail order imbalance to buy, short horizon price pressure, elevated volume, then decay and partial reversal over days to weeks.

Boundary, and it matters: attention is not sentiment. Attention measures salience, not direction. A stock can dominate attention because holders fear collapse. Direction needs a second variable: tone, order imbalance or options skew. Much failed sentiment engineering comes from treating mention volume as if it were bullishness. Grade: robust for attention predicting retail buying and short term pressure. Not supported for attention alone predicting direction beyond a few weeks.

### 6. Anchoring

Sources: Tversky and Kahneman (1974), *Science* 185(4157); George and Hwang (2004), *Journal of Finance* 59(5); Hirshleifer (2001), *Journal of Finance* 56(4).

Judgements start from a salient number and adjust too little. Markets supply salient numbers for free: purchase price, the 52 week high and low, round numbers, the all time high, an offering price, an analyst target. Nearness to the 52 week high predicts future returns in the direction of continuation in United States data, and in the original sample outperformed standard price momentum measures (George and Hwang 2004). Grade the fact and the story separately: fact moderate to robust in equity cross sections, story contested, because two opposite stories are both told about the same observation.

Anchors are among the most measurable psychological objects in a market, because the candidate anchors are public prices. Distance to the 52 week high, to the all time high, to a volume weighted cost proxy and to round levels can be computed from free price data with no inference about inner states. That measurability is a reason to prefer anchor based variables over mood based variables in an honest engine.

### 7. Recency, extrapolation and the narrative fallacy

Sources: Greenwood and Shleifer (2014), *Review of Financial Studies* 27(3); De Bondt and Thaler (1985), *Journal of Finance* 40(3); Jegadeesh and Titman (1993), *Journal of Finance* 48(1); Bordalo, Gennaioli and Shleifer (2018), *Journal of Finance* 73(1); Taleb (2007), *The Black Swan*, for the popular term.

Survey expectations extrapolate. Greenwood and Shleifer (2014) compare six survey measures of expected returns against the expectations implied by standard models and find survey expectations are strongly positively correlated with recent past returns and price levels, and negatively predict subsequent realised returns at the aggregate level. The crowd expects most after prices are already high, and subsequently earns least. That inverts the naive reading of bullish surveys: at roughly annual horizons, high stated expected returns are a contrary indicator in that dataset, because they encode extrapolation rather than information.

The narrative fallacy is the storytelling layer. Humans compress a price path into a causal story with heroes, villains and a moral, then trade the story. Stories require a protagonist, prefer intention over accident, resist base rates, and once formed they filter new evidence. A narrative also solves a coordination problem: it gives strangers the same reason to buy the same thing at the same time, in portable form. Narratives are transmission infrastructure, not decoration.

Horizon structure is essential. Prices tend to continue at horizons of months (momentum), while extreme past performance tends to reverse at multi year horizons. Survey expectations extrapolate at both horizons, which is why they can negatively predict at the annual horizon while price momentum positively predicts at the monthly horizon. Grade: extrapolation in surveys is robust. The leap to fading every bullish reading tomorrow is not supported. The negative predictability is at aggregate, roughly annual horizons, is modest in explanatory power, and comes with no timing precision.

### 8. FOMO and envy

The academic treatment is thinner here than the popular treatment, and this study says so plainly. Adjacent rigorous work: Hong, Kubik and Stein (2004), *Journal of Finance* 59(1), on social interaction and participation; Kaustia and Knupfer (2012), *Journal of Financial Economics* 104(2), on peer returns raising an individual entry probability in Finnish registry data; Frank (1985), *Choosing the Right Pond*, on relative wealth.

Decompose FOMO instead of treating it as a primitive emotion. First, regret over counterfactual wealth: the pain is not poverty, it is watching a salient peer group become rich through an action that was available to you. It scales with peer salience and similarity, not absolute returns. Second, social proof under uncertainty: when valuation is genuinely hard, observed buying by others is fallibly treated as information. Third, anticipatory regret asymmetry: missing a continued rise is imagined as more blameworthy than joining and losing alongside everyone. Losing with the crowd is narratable as bad luck. Missing out alone is narratable as a personal failure.

That decomposition predicts where FOMO bites hardest: assets with no anchor valuation, visible scoreboards, low friction entry, and a story that makes early participants look clever rather than lucky. It predicts weak FOMO for dull, hard to discuss, institutionally held assets regardless of returns. FOMO buying is late cycle by construction, because the peer gains that trigger it must first accumulate and become visible, and it is reflexive: buying raises prices, enlarges peer gains, and recruits the next cohort. It ends when the supply of new entrants is exhausted or when a sharp drawdown flips envy into relief at not participating, and the same social machinery that built the position can liquidate it quickly.

Grade: peer effects on participation are robust in registry data. The full FOMO loop in speculative episodes is moderate, resting substantially on case evidence. Measure its footprints: new holder growth, small trade share, search growth, cohort entry after peer gains. Do not claim to measure the feeling.

### 9. Panic and capitulation

Sources: Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2009), *Review of Financial Studies* 22(6); Coval and Stafford (2007), *Journal of Financial Economics* 86(2); Khandani and Lo (2011), *Journal of Financial Markets* 14(1); Shleifer and Vishny (2011), *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 25(1); Kindleberger (1978), *Manias, Panics, and Crashes*.

Panic is not simply a large amount of fear. It is a change in the structure of selling, from discretionary to forced and from price sensitive to price insensitive. An initial decline tightens constraints: margin calls, value at risk limits, de-leveraging rules, redemptions, collateral haircuts. Constrained holders must sell regardless of their view of value, and they sell what is liquid, not only what fell. Their selling lowers prices and raises measured volatility, which tightens the same constraints again. Brunnermeier and Pedersen name the two loops: the loss spiral and the margin spiral. Liquidity withdraws at the same moment, so price impact per unit of selling rises precisely when selling volume rises. That convexity is the mechanical heart of a crash, and it requires no irrationality at the moment of the crash itself. The irrationality, if any, was installed earlier, in the leverage and crowding that made the system fragile.

Capitulation is the terminal phase in flow data: the last discretionary holders cross a pain threshold and sell into the forced flow, producing climactic volume. The folk claim that climactic volume marks the bottom contains a kernel of mechanism: once forced and capitulating sellers are exhausted, the marginal seller disappears. It is overstated as a timing rule. Volume climaxes also occur mid decline, forced selling can persist for weeks when redemptions are staggered, and in 2008 new forced sellers were manufactured by the decline itself.

Footprints that help tell forced from discretionary selling: selling concentrated in liquid widely held names rather than the names with the worst news (the fire sale signature), commonality in selling across funds with shared holdings, spreads and depth deteriorating faster than volume alone would explain, correlations spiking toward one, and margin contraction where observable.

Grade: robust as an account of amplification. Not a bottom prediction device. Use panic variables to describe fragility and widen uncertainty bands. Never convert a capitulation reading into a confident buy call.

### 10. How individual psychology becomes a system

**Keynes and higher order beliefs.** Keynes (1936), *The General Theory*, Chapter 12. Contestants in his beauty contest must pick not the faces they find prettiest, nor the faces they think others will find prettiest, but the faces others will think others will think, and so on. Professional investment under career pressure operates at the third degree and beyond: anticipating what average opinion expects average opinion to be. Formal work: Morris and Shin (2002), *American Economic Review* 92(5); Allen, Morris and Shin (2006), *Review of Financial Studies* 19(3). Three consequences follow. Public signals are overweighted, because a public signal moves your belief about everyone else as well as your own belief, so a widely broadcast sentiment reading can move prices partly because it is widely broadcast. Prices can be sticky in the wrong place and jumpy at thresholds. And the forecast target changes: a crowd that is bullish is one object; a crowd that is bullish, believes the crowd will become more bullish, and holds leveraged positions that force buying on strength is a different object entirely.

**Reflexivity.** Soros (1987), *The Alchemy of Finance*; Soros (2008), *The New Paradigm for Financial Markets*. In its philosophical form reflexivity is unverifiable as stated, because any outcome can be redescribed as reflexive after the fact. In its mechanical form it reduces to specific feedback channels, each measurable with a sign and a lag: price to collateral to credit to demand to price; price to flows to mandated buying to price; price to volatility to risk model limits to forced selling to price. Those channels are real and are formalised better by others, including Geanakoplos (2010), *NBER Macroeconomics Annual* 24, on the leverage cycle. Do not build a reflexivity index. Build channel monitors, and label the regime reflexive only where several channels point the same way.

**Minsky.** Minsky (1986), *Stabilizing an Unstable Economy*; Minsky (1992), Levy Economics Institute Working Paper No. 74. Stability is destabilising. Borrowers migrate from hedge finance (cash flow covers interest and principal), to speculative finance (covers interest, must roll principal), to Ponzi finance (covers neither, depends on appreciation or new borrowing). Stability validates risk taking, compresses premia and lending standards, and shifts the population toward the fragile end, not because anyone decides to destabilise the system but because each step is individually rewarded. Kindleberger five stages (displacement, boom, euphoria, profit taking, panic) are the narrative calendar laid over that balance sheet migration. The strongest support is long run credit data: Schularick and Taylor (2012), *American Economic Review* 102(2), using 140 years for 14 advanced economies, find credit growth is a powerful predictor of financial crises, and Jorda, Schularick and Taylor (2015), *Journal of Monetary Economics* 76, find bubbles funded by credit are associated with deeper recessions and slower recoveries than equity bubbles without leverage. The damage variable is leverage attached to the bubble, not overpricing alone. The dot com collapse destroyed large paper wealth with a mild recession. The housing collapse produced a systemic crisis because the overpricing sat on leveraged balance sheets. Minsky role in an engine: a fragility prior at the slowest frequency, not a timing input.

**Shiller and narratives.** Shiller (2017), *American Economic Review* 107(4); Shiller (2019), *Narrative Economics*; Shiller (1984), *Brookings Papers on Economic Activity* 1984(2). Narratives are contagious stories that change what actions feel appropriate. A narrative has a contagion rate and a recovery rate. When contagion exceeds recovery it goes epidemic, and its economic effects scale with prevalence, not truth. Narratives usually travel as constellations: clusters of mutually reinforcing stories that share a moral, which lower the social cost of acting and raise the social cost of abstaining. Timing runs both ways: stories cause price moves, and price moves cause stories, because a large move demands a story and one is supplied within hours. Any regression of price on narrative tone that ignores that simultaneity overstates the predictive content of tone. Keep this separate from the socionomics program associated with Prechter, which infers its central variable from the phenomenon it is meant to explain and is graded unverifiable as stated to fragile. Shiller weaker claim is defensible: specific identifiable stories spread measurably and affect specific decisions. Measure named narratives, not mood in the abstract.

**Baker and Wurgler and sentiment as a priced factor.** Baker and Wurgler (2006), *Journal of Finance* 61(4); Baker and Wurgler (2007), *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 21(2); Baker, Wurgler and Yuan (2012), *Journal of Financial Economics* 104(2). Sentiment here is a propensity to speculate that expresses itself most where valuation is most subjective and arbitrage is most limited. The index is the first principal component of six proxies, each first orthogonalised to macroeconomic conditions: the closed end fund discount, NYSE turnover, the number of IPOs, average first day IPO returns, the equity share in new issues, and the dividend premium. The central result is cross sectional and conditional, and is routinely misquoted as a market timing result. When sentiment is low, subsequent returns are relatively high for small, young, high volatility, unprofitable, non dividend paying, extreme growth and distressed stocks. When sentiment is high, those same categories earn relatively low subsequent returns. Large, old, profitable, dividend paying stocks are comparatively insulated. A replication and extension by Leong, Li, Li, Peng and Xu (2025), *Economic Inquiry*, confirms the pattern in the original 1963 to 2002 sample and in a new 2002 to 2023 United States sample, finds instability in some specifications, and finds failure in China. Grade: moderate to robust in the United States, conditional internationally. Huang, Jiang, Tu and Zhou (2015), *Review of Financial Studies* 28(3), build an aligned index from the same family and report monthly in sample and out of sample R squared around 1.7 percent and 1.2 percent, against near zero for the original index at the aggregate level. Even the improved aggregate predictor explains roughly one percent of monthly return variance. Economically interesting and practically humbling, and both halves must survive into any display.

**Limits to arbitrage and sentiment beta.** Shleifer and Vishny (1997), *Journal of Finance* 52(1); Barberis and Thaler (2003), in *Handbook of the Economics of Finance*; Pontiff (2006), *Journal of Accounting and Economics* 42(1-2). Arbitrage has three frictions, each sufficient alone: fundamental risk, noise trader risk, and agency and funding risk. Most arbitrage capital is other people money, managed under evaluation windows shorter than the life of a mispricing. When mispricing widens, arbitrageurs lose money in the short run, investors withdraw, and liquidation is forced at the worst moment. Performance based arbitrage is therefore least supplied exactly when expected returns to arbitrage are highest. Stocks differ in how much sentiment can move them because they differ in arbitrage cost: idiosyncratic volatility, short sale constraints and borrow cost, low float, low institutional ownership, absence of earnings anchors, and story intensity. The same market level sentiment extreme implies a large expected effect in a small unprofitable high volatility name and a small effect in a mega cap payer. Applying one sentiment dial to every name equally is a theory error, not a simplification.

**Noise trader risk.** De Long, Shleifer, Summers and Waldmann (1990a), *Journal of Political Economy* 98(4); (1990b), *Journal of Finance* 45(2); Black (1986), *Journal of Finance* 41(3). Noise traders trade on beliefs not warranted by fundamental information, and their sentiment is unpredictable. Unpredictability converts their presence from a profit opportunity into a source of risk: an arbitrageur betting against them bears the risk that sentiment becomes more extreme before it corrects, so he limits his position and prices can deviate from fundamentals in equilibrium. The companion paper adds the destabilising rational speculator: if feedback traders buy after rises, a rational speculator who receives good news buys more than fundamental value, anticipating feedback buying tomorrow and intending to sell into it. Riding can be return maximising for a speculator whose horizon is shorter than the bubble life, and the collective result is a larger bubble and a worse break. An extreme therefore does not forecast an immediate reversal. It forecasts elevated variance of the future path, fatter tails in both directions in the near term, and elevated reversal risk at longer horizons. Grading an engine only on directional hit rate would discard the part of the theory most likely to be true.

**Does fear move fast and greed move slow?** The proverb is conditional, not universal. Robust part: large down moves are on average sharper and more volatility generating than large up moves of similar size (Black 1976; Christie 1982; Bekaert and Wu 2000, *Review of Financial Studies* 13(1)). Also true in the other direction: speculative excess in issuance, first day IPO returns and turnover typically builds over quarters, post crisis distrust can persist for years, and in modern episodes greed has been the fast variable, as coordinated retail episodes in January 2021 produced vertical moves in days. Verdict: fear is fast when leverage and forced selling are present, because transmission is mechanical. Fear is slow when it takes the form of distrust and abstention, because abstention has no forcing mechanism. Greed is slow when it must be funded by credit formation and issuance pipelines. Greed is fast when transmitted by social coordination into liquid low friction instruments. Measure the transmission channel present in the episode and let the speed follow from the channel. Do not hard code the proverb.

## Part 2: The empirical record, honestly graded

### Surveys

AAII has polled members weekly since 1987. Investors Intelligence has classified newsletter writers since 1963. Michigan measures household economic sentiment with roots in the 1950s. Yale under Shiller publishes investor confidence and crash confidence series from the late 1980s and 1990s depending on the series.

Core finding: stated beliefs extrapolate the recent past and negatively predict aggregate returns at horizons around a year (Greenwood and Shleifer 2014, above). Explanatory power is a few percent of the variance of subsequent annual returns at most. Brown and Cliff (2004), *Journal of Empirical Finance* 11(1), and (2005), *Journal of Business* 78(2), find sentiment levels strongly related to contemporaneous and past returns, some contrary predictive content at long horizons, and weak near term power once past returns are accounted for. Fisher and Statman (2000), *Financial Analysts Journal* 56(2), find AAII and newsletter aggregates negatively related to subsequent S and P 500 returns in their samples, with the newsletter aggregate stronger. The standing problem: the number of independent extreme episodes in any survey history is in the low dozens at most, so confidence intervals around any extreme zone rule are wide, and rules fitted to famous extremes (October 2008, March 2009, December 2018) risk describing those episodes rather than stating a law.

Grades: survey extrapolation robust. Survey contrary prediction at annual horizons moderate. Weekly AAII extremes as a standalone short term timing signal fragile. Zhou (2018), reviewing to December 2016, reports the Baker and Wurgler index explains about 0.3 percent of monthly market return variance, its equally weighted version 0.006 in R squared terms, and a partial least squares combination 0.019. Real, small, and routinely oversold.

### Put and call ratios and options volume

The strongest options result is not about the aggregate ratio. Pan and Poteshman (2006), *Review of Financial Studies* 19(3), use signed buyer initiated option volume at the individual stock level and find stocks with high put to call ratios from buyer initiated volume underperform over the following week, with a long short spread on the order of 40 basis points per week in their headline specification, concentrated in volume classified as information motivated. Note the sign and the level: at single name level, heavy put buying predicts further declines in that name in the short run, an information pattern, not a contrary bounce. The aggregate contrary story and the single name information story point in opposite directions at short horizons. An engine that merges them into one dial will be wrong in one of its two uses at all times.

For the aggregate index ratio the evidence is practitioner research, exchange studies and newsletter backtests rather than settled peer reviewed consensus. Reported shape: extreme high equity put and call readings cluster around short term lows and are followed by above average returns over the next one to four weeks on average, with enormous dispersion, frequent mid decline extremes that fail, and performance that degrades when thresholds fitted on earlier decades are applied unchanged after the 2020 break (retail call buying permanently raised call volume) and after the daily expiry expansion. See also Simon and Wiggins (2001) and Bandopadhyaya and Jones (2008), both finding some predictive content with regime sensitivity and small magnitudes.

Confounds: put volume mixes speculation, hedging of long portfolios, and structured product and dealer flows whose sentiment sign is ambiguous or reversed. Index put buying by an institution hedging a large book is insurance purchased precisely because the buyer intends to stay long.

Grades: signed single name option volume in the information direction moderate. Aggregate put and call extremes as a contrary indicator over one to four weeks fragile to moderate, usable only as a percentile extreme against its own recent regime. Any fixed rule of the form ratio above 1.0 means buy is debunked as a universal rule by the structural change in the series itself.

### Fund flows and performance chasing

Return chasing is among the most replicated facts in empirical finance. Sirri and Tufano (1998), *Journal of Finance* 53(5), document the convex flow to performance relation: top performing funds receive disproportionate inflows, poor performers suffer muted outflows. Friesen and Sapp (2007), *Journal of Banking and Finance* 31(9), estimate timing decisions cost equity fund investors on the order of 1.5 percent per year relative to buy and hold in their sample. Industry DALBAR analyses report larger gaps in the same direction, with contested methods. The direction is not in serious dispute. The exact annual cost is sample and method dependent and should never be quoted as a constant.

The predictive version is Frazzini and Lamont (2008), *Journal of Financial Economics* 88(2): stocks heavily held by funds experiencing inflows subsequently underperform, with a headline three year value weighted spread between extreme flow quintiles of about minus 0.85 percent per month in the original 1980 to 2003 paper. An independent automated replication matches the in sample spread closely (about minus 0.67 percent per month) and then finds the effect essentially vanishes out of sample in 2004 to 2010 (about minus 0.04 percent per month). A result can be genuinely present in its discovery sample, genuinely replicable by code, and still fail to persist. Grades: performance chasing as behaviour robust. The dumb money cross sectional effect moderate in sample, fragile as a forward expectation at its published size.

What survives the haircut: flow extremes at category level remain a clean measure of where extrapolative demand is concentrated, and flow driven price pressure in underlying holdings is real and mechanical while it lasts (Coval and Stafford 2007; Lou 2012). Ask flows three questions: where is demand concentrated, how much forced trading does it imply, and has the concentration reached historical percentile extremes. Asking only whether prices will reverse gets a fragile answer.

### Issuance and IPOs: the supply side

Firms are better sentiment traders than households. Ritter (1991), *Journal of Finance* 46(1), finds United States IPOs from 1975 to 1984 underperform matched firms by economically large margins over three years, on the order of 29 percentage points cumulative in the headline comparison, concentrated in the heaviest issuance years. Loughran and Ritter (1995), *Journal of Finance* 50(1), extend the result to seasoned offerings. Measurement debates shrink the size, weakest in large firms and value weighted portfolios, but the direction has survived four decades and international samples. Grade: robust in direction, moderate in size.

Baker and Wurgler (2000), *Journal of Finance* 55(5), show the equity share in total new issues negatively predicts aggregate market returns at annual horizons in data back to 1928. First day IPO returns averaged roughly 7 percent in the 1980s, roughly 15 percent in the early 1990s in commonly reported Ritter tabulations, and roughly 65 percent in 1999 to 2000, before falling back. Grade for equity share and IPO heat as slow contrary state variables at annual horizons: moderate. As timing tools for quarters: fragile. Pipelines take months, and hot windows can stay hot far longer than a contrary trader can stay solvent.

### Consumer sentiment

Carroll, Fuhrer and Wilcox (1994), *American Economic Review* 84(5), find sentiment helps forecast consumption growth beyond standard variables. Howrey (2001) finds the Michigan index helps predict recession probability and growth. Barsky and Sims (2012), *American Economic Review* 102(4), decompose confidence innovations and conclude a large share reflects news about future fundamentals rather than pure animal spirits. Much of what consumer sentiment knows is real information about jobs, incomes and inflation arriving through households before official statistics. Lemmon and Portniaguina (2006), *Review of Financial Studies* 19(4), find both the macroeconomic component and a residual sentiment component negatively predict subsequent returns for small stocks and stocks with low institutional ownership, the hard to arbitrage segment. Qiu and Welch (2006) find changes in the Michigan index related to small stock excess returns in their specifications.

Grades: consumer sentiment as macro information moderate to robust. As a cross sectional proxy for small and retail held stocks moderate. As an aggregate stock timing signal fragile, because a high level can mean strong household fundamentals and extrapolative optimism at once, and the two channels fight.

### Media tone

Tetlock (2007), *Journal of Finance* 62(3), builds a pessimism factor from the Wall Street Journal Abreast of the Market column from 1984 to 1999 and finds high pessimism predicts downward pressure on the Dow the following day, followed by reversion over the following days, and extreme pessimism in either direction predicts elevated volume. Magnitudes are small, on the order of a few basis points per day for a one standard deviation move. Tetlock, Saar Tsechansky and Macskassy (2008), *Journal of Finance* 63(3), find the fraction of negative words in firm specific news predicts low earnings and low returns. Loughran and McDonald (2011), *Journal of Finance* 66(1), show general purpose negative word lists badly misclassify financial text and supply the finance specific dictionary that became the field standard. Garcia (2013), *Journal of Finance* 68(3), extends the newspaper approach from 1905 to 2005 and finds return predictability of tone concentrated in recessions, with a one standard deviation pessimism shock associated with a move on the order of 12 basis points in the next day Dow return in recession periods, and much weaker content in expansions.

Grades: moderate to robust for the existence and shape of the effect, fragile for any claim of economically large daily timing profits after costs, contested on whether tone causes sentiment or merely records it. The honest answer is both, in proportions that vary by episode. Tone should be expected to matter most exactly when trading on it is most dangerous.

### Social media mood: the famous study, the fund, and the aftermath

Bollen, Mao and Zeng (2011), *Journal of Computational Science* 2(1), analyse about 9.8 million tweets from 2008 and report that a Calm mood dimension Granger causes Dow changes at lags of several days, with a neural network including mood predicting daily Dow direction with 87.6 percent accuracy in their test window. A London fund, Derwent Capital Markets, launched on the strength of the approach in 2011, raised reported tens of millions, and closed after about a month of trading in 2012. No audited multi year live record demonstrating anything like 87.6 percent directional accuracy ever appeared.

Lachanski and Pav (2017), *Econ Journal Watch* 14(3), reconstruct alternative Calm series, including for November and December 2008 data the original authors collected but did not use, and find the reconstructed measures do not Granger cause the Dow in that held out window. They document specification fragility across lexicon choices, aggregation and lag selection, in a very short 2008 sample containing the crisis, an election and Thanksgiving. Grade: aggregate social mood predicting the index at multi day horizons with high accuracy is debunked in its strong form.

What survives is firm level and smaller. Bartov, Faurel and Mohanram (2018), *The Accounting Review* 93(3), find aggregate opinion in firm specific tweets before earnings predicts earnings and announcement returns. Sprenger, Tumasjan, Sandner and Welpe (2014), *European Financial Management* 20(5), find message volume, sentiment and agreement related to returns, volume and volatility at stock level with small magnitudes. Cookson and Niessner (2020) document that disagreement on social platforms is associated with trading activity and has asset pricing content at firm level. Platform access is a provenance fact: social data access has tightened repeatedly since 2023 and series break without notice, so any gauge built on a platform feed must carry a break flag and a death plan.

### A short honour and dishonour roll

Sunshine and weather: Hirshleifer and Shumway (2003), *Journal of Finance* 58(3), report morning sunshine in the city of a main exchange associated with higher daily returns across 26 exchanges from 1982 to 1997. Later scrutiny raises multiple testing, data handling and out of sample decay concerns. If present, a few basis points at most and untradable after costs. Grade: fragile. A teaching example of how a plausible mechanism plus many cities plus many weather variables can produce a beautiful false positive.

Sports sentiment: Edmans, Garcia and Norli (2007), *Journal of Finance* 62(4), find national team losses in major tournaments followed by negative next day returns in the losing country market on the order of minus 38 basis points in their headline estimate, with no symmetric gain after wins. Grade: moderate to fragile. Real as evidence that mood shocks can move prices, not usable as an engine input for a United States equity book.

The Super Bowl indicator and calendar folklore: data mined coincidences with no mechanism. Grade: debunked as prediction.

Closed end fund discounts: Lee, Shleifer and Thaler (1991), *Journal of Finance* 46(1), argue discounts move with small investor sentiment. Later work complicates the pure sentiment reading with fees, liquidity and tax explanations for parts of the average discount, while comovement and issuance timing remain hard to explain without a sentiment like common factor. Grade: moderate as a barometer, contested as a complete theory of the discount level.

VIX: a price of insurance, not a sentiment survey. It embeds a variance risk premium, so its level mixes expected volatility with the price of insurance (Bollerslev, Tauchen and Zhou 2009). Whaley (2000) popularised the fear gauge label. High VIX levels are followed on average by above average equity returns over subsequent months in United States history, with the small number of independent episodes problem: deep spikes are roughly 1998, 2008 to 2009, 2011, 2015, 2018, 2020 and 2022, a sample of about seven, not seventy. Grade: robust as a measure of the price of insurance and of stress, fragile as a precise bottom timing rule.

Margin debt: rises into the 2000, 2007 and 2021 peaks and contracts sharply in the breaks, a Minsky consistent footprint. As a level it trends with market capitalisation and must be normalised before any percentile language is honest. Grade: moderate as fragility, fragile as timing.

### Sentiment at the great extremes

1929. The Dow peaked on 3 September 1929 at 381.17 and fell 12.8 percent on 28 October and 11.7 percent on 29 October. The honest record is indirect: brokers loans had grown to roughly 8.5 billion dollars by October 1929, investment trusts traded at large premiums to net asset value, and newspaper archives show the new era narrative constellation in full epidemic form. White (1990), *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 4(2), remains the balanced account. Mechanisms visible: Minsky migration funded by broker loans, extrapolation, narrative epidemic, then forced liquidation. Lesson: the leverage and premium series are the transportable gauges. There is no AAII survey for 1929 (it begins in 1987), no published VIX (the index begins in 1990 in an earlier construction), and no modern put and call volume. Pretending otherwise would be fabrication with footnotes.

1987. On 19 October 1987 the Dow fell 22.6 percent in one session, still its largest one day percentage decline. The Brady Commission report (1988) assigns a central amplifying role to portfolio insurance, a rules based strategy that sold index futures mechanically as prices fell, interacting with illiquidity and futures and cash dislocation. Shiller (1987) survey evidence collected immediately after found investors cited the decline itself and anxiety about further decline, rather than any specific news, as their reason for selling. Lesson: the most dangerous seller is not the frightened human but the rule that must sell.

2000. The Nasdaq Composite peaked on 10 March 2000 at 5048.62. The Shiller cyclically adjusted price to earnings ratio peaked at about 44 in December 1999, the highest in its history (Shiller online data, revised as earnings revise). Average first day IPO returns reached roughly 65 percent in 1999 to 2000. Equity fund inflows set records in early 2000, peaking almost exactly with prices. Margin debt peaked in March 2000. The Nasdaq then fell about 78 percent peak to trough by October 2002, while the economy had only a mild recession. Lesson: when valuation, issuance, flows and leverage extremes coincide, the joint extreme carries information no single series extreme carries. Coincidence counting across independent families is a design principle, not a decoration.

2008. Two phases a single dial would merge disastrously. In the build from 2003 to 2007 the operative sentiment was complacency: spreads compressed, covenant quality deteriorated, housing narrative prevalence was epidemic, measured volatility was low while fragility accumulated. In the break, mechanics were forced: the VIX closed at 80.86 on 20 November 2008, AAII bearishness reached the mid 50 percent range at its crisis extremes, and fire sale comovement dominated name specific news. The market bottomed on 9 March 2009, months after the worst readings. The extreme zone was entered in October 2008 and the market fell for roughly five more months, losing roughly a further quarter of its value after its first VIX extreme. Lesson: extremes mark regimes, not dates.

2020. From its 19 February 2020 closing high the S and P 500 fell about 34 percent to its 23 March 2020 close, the fastest decline of that depth from a peak in its history. The VIX closed at 82.69 on 16 March 2020, exceeding the 2008 closing record under the same modern methodology. Baker, Bloom, Davis and Terry (2020) document that no previous infectious disease outbreak, including 1918 influenza, produced a remotely comparable United States market reaction. Lesson: cycle speed is regime dependent. Calibration windows must include 2020 or every speed parameter will be biased slow, and bands computed without 2020 will call ordinary stress unprecedented.

2021. GameStop, with short interest above 100 percent of float in reported figures of the era, rose from under 20 dollars in early January 2021 to an intraday high of 483 dollars on 28 January 2021, pre split prices, before broker restrictions on opening purchases, imposed under clearing collateral pressure, broke the move. The SEC staff report on the episode (2021) documents social media attention, retail order flow, short covering and options market maker hedging, a gamma feedback loop in which call buying forced dealer buying of the underlying. The broader 2021 complex showed record retail call buying, SPAC issuance at multiples of all prior history, elevated IPO first day returns and nominal margin records at once. The deflation of 2022 then showed speculative high sentiment beta segments falling far more than the broad index, the Baker and Wurgler cross sectional prediction operating live. Lesson: the fastest modern transmission runs through options positioning and social coordination, both capable of completing a full cycle inside a month. An engine sampled monthly would have missed the entire episode. Sampling frequency is a theory choice.

Summary across six episodes spanning 92 years: the constants are leverage or mechanical feedback on the way down, issuance and flow extremes near tops, narrative epidemics as the transmission story, and the failure of any single gauge extreme to date the turn. The variables are speed and the lead series. That is why a calibrated engine is a panel of family gauges with coincidence and fragility logic on top, not a single fear and greed dial.

### Grade table, in one place

* Performance chasing in flows: robust. Flow based return prediction at published sizes: fragile forward, moderate in sample.
* Prospect theory mechanisms and the disposition effect: robust as behaviour, weak as standalone index timing.
* Attention predicting retail buying and short term pressure: robust. Attention alone predicting direction: not supported.
* Survey extrapolation: robust. Survey contrary prediction at annual horizons: moderate. Weekly survey extreme timing: fragile.
* Baker and Wurgler cross section: moderate to robust, United States, conditional. Best aggregate sentiment timing: about 1 percent of monthly return variance. Treat larger claims as unproven.
* Issuance and IPO long run underperformance: robust in direction. Equity share as slow contrary state: moderate.
* Consumer sentiment for macro information: moderate to robust. For the small stock cross section: moderate. For aggregate timing: fragile.
* Media tone pressure and reversal at daily horizons: moderate to robust in shape, small in size, recession amplified. Causal direction: contested, both ways.
* Firm specific social media information: moderate. Aggregate Twitter mood prediction in its strong form: debunked.
* Weather: fragile. Sports loss effect: moderate to fragile. Super Bowl indicator: debunked.
* VIX as stress price: robust. VIX thresholds as bottom dates: fragile.
* Noise trader risk, limits to arbitrage, Minsky credit fragility and panic amplification: robust as mechanisms. None of them is a timing device.
