# Section 4: Waves and Fibonacci: The Echo Method

## What this section teaches

This section teaches one complete working method: how to locate yourself inside a move with Elliott Wave, how to price where that move is likely to pause or end with Fibonacci, and how to turn that location into a chart a reader can act on. The method is the Echo Analysis method as preserved in three local sources that agree with each other. The first is the Echo and Max playbook reverse engineered from 134 X chart posts, at `~/workspace/research/chart-methodology/PLAYBOOK_echo_max_2026-10-12.md`. The second is the full teaching run in Echo's Kit newsletter, six issues from September 7 to October 18, 2026, analysed in `~/workspace/.scratch-x-charts/ECHO-NEWSLETTER-ANALYSIS-20261018.md`, with the primary texts in `newsletter-emails/text/` alongside it. The third is the Echo Algorithm, at `~/workspace/.scratch-x-charts/echo-algorithm/ECHO-ALGORITHM.md`, which turns the same newsletter rules into a deterministic nine step procedure with no invented constants. Where this section gives a number, the number is one Echo published. Where it gives a drawing rule, the rule is one his charts observe.

The local stock analysis skill carries the same skeleton into Midna's own reads: name the cycle position before any indicator, build levels as zones with confluence, draw at most two trendlines on confirmed major pivots, count divergence only at extremes, and close every read with both scenarios and a conditional verdict line. The Best Setups chart law in the census carries the same grammar further: buy zone, invalidation, labelled targets, the named pattern itself, a dashed expected path into reserved future space, and volume, RSI and MACD panes. This section teaches the wave and Fibonacci core that sits underneath all of those surfaces.

Learn the order and keep it. Elliott supplies the map: which swing to measure and where you are in the cycle. Fibonacci supplies the prices: where a correction tends to end and where the next impulse leg tends to go. Moving averages, prior structure and trendlines supply confluence: whether a Fibonacci price is also a place the market has reason to respect. RSI divergence and volume supply timing evidence: whether the move into the zone is exhausting or still pressing. The reaction supplies the trigger: you never buy a level, you buy what price does at the level. Every claim in the method carries its own kill line, stated in advance as a price and a close.

By the end of this section you should be able to take a clean daily or weekly chart and execute the whole sequence without skipping: find the meaningful swings, decide impulse or correction, test the count against the three rules, draw the correct Fibonacci tool on the correct anchors, build the zone from confluence, wait for and judge the reaction, place the invalidation and the target ladder, and write the templated call: ticker, verdict, zone, target.

## 1. The one question, and the posture that goes with it

Echo's charts answer one question: where are we in the cycle, and what single level decides it? That is the shared skeleton line in the Playbook. Everything on the chart serves that question. If you cannot answer it in one sentence, you do not have a count yet. You have decoration.

Work in this posture:

1. Cycle position first. Before RSI, before volume, before any pattern name, say whether price is in an impulse leg, a correction leg, or a terminal pattern, and which wave inside it. The Playbook adoption notes make this the first line of every Midna read for a reason: the same Fibonacci level means opposite things in wave 2 and in wave B.
2. Patience in the middle. When price sits mid structure, between zones, the Echo call is corrective, not buy time, or simply patience. Mid structure is not a setup. The method earns its keep by refusing to invent one there.
3. Conditional language only. Every call is if and then with the level named in advance: losing a named price confirms the move to the named buy zone; flipping a named average is the low risk buy; price must hold here or much lower follows. A call without its condition is not in this grammar.
4. Both scenarios on one chart. The primary count and the alternate count live together. In the Playbook corpus the primary is drawn in white and the alternate in orange or red, with the alternate's terminus visible on the same chart, often a deep flush box. The chart carries its own invalidation instead of hiding it.
5. Sparse drawings. One to two trendlines maximum, anchored on major swing extremes spanning the whole pattern, months not wiggles. If a pattern needs five lines, there is no pattern. This is a Playbook rule and an Echo Algorithm rule at once: structure pivots come from a coarse view of the swings, and a fine wiggle must never be allowed to impersonate a wave.

Hold those five and the technical steps below stay honest. Drop them and wave counting becomes relabelling after the fact, which is precisely what the three rules and the checklist exist to prevent.

## 2. Step one: build the swing map

Do this before you count anything.

### 2.1 Choose timeframe in the right order

Draw the higher timeframe first. Weekly before daily, daily before the 4 hour. That order is verbatim in the Fibonacci newsletter issue of October 18 and in the Echo Algorithm fib checklist. The reason is practical: when timeframes disagree, the higher one wins. The newsletter example is exact: if the hourly 0.618 sits at one price and the weekly 0.618 sits 2 percent lower, price usually reaches for the weekly.

In Echo practice, counts are drawn on daily and weekly together. The weekly count governs, the daily times it. A daily wave 2 that contradicts a weekly correction is not a wave 2. Reconcile upward first, then time the entry downward.

### 2.2 Anchor on meaningful swings only, wick to wick

Use structure pivots: the major highs and lows that span the pattern. The Echo Algorithm implementation note is the clearest statement of the discipline: a coarse zigzag finds structure pivots, and a much finer pass is used only inside a leg to judge whether its substructure reads cleanly. By eye the translation is simple. Stand back until only the swings that changed direction for weeks remain. Those are your anchors. The small texture inside each leg is for the cleanliness check later, never for the main Fibonacci anchors.

Anchor wick to wick, always. Price traded at the wick, so the wick is the true high or low, not the candle body. That rule appears in both the Fibonacci newsletter and Step 1 of the Echo Algorithm. A body anchored Fibonacci shifts every level on the chart and quietly invalidates the whole construction.

Draw in the direction of the move. Uptrend: swing low to swing high. Downtrend: swing high to swing low. Reversed drawing is one of the common ways a correct level gets read at a wrong price.

One exception the newsletter names: stocks gap. Sometimes the swing extreme is a gap candle with no useful wick. Anchor to the extreme of that candle, then check whether price has respected the levels that follow. If it has not, redraw from the last candle that traded through the area normally. Respect is the audit. Levels price has never reacted to are a warning that the anchors are wrong.

### 2.3 Set the scale before you draw

On weekly and monthly charts, or on any move of several hundred percent, switch to log scale before you draw. The Fibonacci newsletter is direct: a linear Fibonacci on a multi decade or multi hundred percent move gives levels price ignores. On shorter timeframes the difference is negligible. The Echo Algorithm encodes the same caution: moves above 300 percent carry a log scale advised flag. Make the scale decision before the first Fibonacci, not after the levels fail to line up.

### 2.4 Mark the geography while the chart is still bare

Before any Fibonacci, mark only three kinds of geography:

1. The 50 period average and the 200 period average on the timeframe you are reading, and the 200 week average when you are on a long horizon. In Echo and Max geography, averages are not signals. The 50 average is the actionable pivot: reclaim is a trigger, loss is danger. The 200 average and 200 week average are the regime line and final backstop. Longer period means stronger level.
2. Prior pivot shelves: a prior swing high or low that already acted as support or resistance, ideally tested at least twice. The Echo Algorithm scores a prior level with two or more touches as a confluence element. One touch is a note. Two touches is a shelf.
3. At most two trendlines: a descending line across the two most recent countertrend highs of the corrective structure, or a rising line across the base's higher lows. Anchored on confirmed major pivots, spanning the pattern. A line price has already closed through is dead. Do not redraw it to keep the story alive.

That bare map is the structural half of every zone you will build later. Fibonacci without it is arithmetic. Fibonacci on top of it is a location.

## 3. Step two: decide impulse or correction

Ask the binary question first, in Echo's own framing from the impulse newsletter: is this move impulsive or corrective? Everything else follows from the answer. Five waves tells you the trend. Three waves tells you price is correcting. That one read decides which side of the market you want to be on and which Fibonacci tool you will reach for.

### 3.1 What a real impulse looks like before you label it

Do not start by placing numbers. Start by reading character. A real impulse, in the newsletter checklist:

1. Moves with conviction. You never doubt the direction.
2. Shows five waves: three pushes with the trend, two smaller pullbacks against it, and it reads without forcing.
3. Has momentum: wide candles closing near their highs in an advance, with clear acceleration in wave 3.
4. Never overlaps in its interior the way a correction does. Wave 4 stays clear of wave 1, so the move looks like a staircase.
5. Carries volume on the pushes. Legs with the trend trade more than the pullbacks.

A fake impulse shows the opposite texture:

1. Choppy. Candles overlap and price swings back and forth.
2. Moves in three waves. Two pushes and a counter move: A B C, not 1 2 3 4 5.
3. Can still be fast, but it is not controlled. Big wicks, sharp spikes, sharp reversals, no rhythm.
4. Has no volume follow through. The pushes carry no more volume than the pullbacks.

Memorise the aphorism that closes the comparison: if you have to squint to find five waves, you are not looking at an impulse. And the companion rule from the corrections teaching: speed is not structure. The worked example is FLNC. Its rally into wave B went parabolic and tagged the 0.786 Fibonacci at $28.63. Judged on speed alone it looked like a new impulse. Counted, it was three waves, spiking and overlapping, against a prior move into the larger wave 1 that had been controlled, with clean pushes and orderly pullbacks. Then it broke its trendline and wave C started down in five. A parabolic B wave can pay well. It starts nothing.

### 3.2 The personality of each wave

Once the texture says impulse is plausible, place yourself by personality before you finalise labels. Echo teaches the five personalities as a field guide, and they are diagnostic, not decorative.

Wave 1 is weak and unconvincing. Most traders still think the prior downtrend is intact and call it a relief rally. You will rarely spot it in real time, and the method tells you not to try. Section 7 of this section is devoted to that rule.

Wave 2 is a sharp, deep pullback that makes the downtrend feel like it is back. It shakes out the people who bought wave 1. It usually ends between the 0.618 and 0.786 retracement of wave 1.

Wave 3 is the strongest wave. Wide candles, rising volume, RSI pushing into overbought. The public notices and momentum traders pile in. It tends to reach the 1.618 extension of wave 1, and often goes further. Wave 3 is where the money is made, in Echo's phrase, and the whole method is organised to buy the end of wave 2 so that wave 3 is the leg you own.

Wave 4 is slow, choppy and frustrating. A sideways grind that gives back a small part of wave 3. Expect it to end between the 0.382 and 0.5 retracement of wave 3.

Wave 5 looks strong on the surface while momentum fades underneath. RSI diverges, volume runs lighter than in wave 3, and the crowd is at its most bullish. The end of wave 5 is the riskiest place on the chart to buy.

The fastest discriminator inside the impulse is momentum location. The highest RSI or MACD reading of the whole move usually prints in wave 3. So when you cannot tell wave 3 from wave 5, find where momentum peaked. Momentum still making new highs with price points to wave 3 still running. A new price high on lower momentum than an earlier push points to wave 5. The Echo Algorithm encodes exactly this: momentum peak inside wave 3 is part of the expected profile of a true impulse.

Extensions change the expectation without changing the rules. Most impulses have one wave that stretches much further than the other two, and in stocks it is nearly always wave 3. An extended wave 3 often reaches the 1.618 to 2.618 of wave 1, and zoomed in it splits into its own clear five waves. That subdivision is the trap: you can count five clean waves, call the top, and sell, when you were only counting waves 1 to 5 inside wave 3 and waves 4 and 5 of the bigger move were still to come. The defence is timeframe and momentum together. Check the higher timeframe before you call any top, and check whether the strongest RSI reading of the whole move sits inside your count. If it does, you are probably still in wave 3. After an extended wave 3, waves 1 and 5 tend toward equality, with wave 5 often travelling 0.618 to 1.0 times the length of wave 1, and wave 5 is more likely to fail. A truncated fifth, where wave 5 completes without making a new high beyond wave 3, usually follows an extended, exhausting wave 3, and the reversal after it tends to be fast and deep. It is rare. Know it exists so a failed new high does not get forced into a bullish recount.

### 3.3 Alternation: wave 2 tells you about wave 4

Wave 2's shape sets the expectation for wave 4: sharp against sideways. Once wave 2 has formed, wave 4 is half called already. That is a process law in the newsletter analysis and a computed field in the Echo Algorithm. If wave 2 was a sharp zigzag down, expect wave 4 to waste time sideways in a flat or triangle like grind. If wave 2 was sideways and time consuming, expect wave 4 to be the sharper, shorter correction. Use alternation to know what patience will be required, and to avoid demanding that wave 4 repeat wave 2's shape as proof the count is right.

## 4. Step three: enforce the three impulse rules

The three rules decide whether you are looking at an impulse at all. They are hard constraints, drawn in issue 5 of the newsletter and hard gated in Step 2 of the Echo Algorithm. Break one and the count is wrong. Recount. Do not stretch labels.

### Rule 1: Wave 2 never retraces all of wave 1

If price returns to the start of wave 1, there was no wave 1. In Fibonacci terms, the 1.0 retracement is invalidation, always. This is not a soft guideline about depth. It is the birth certificate of the count. Every Echo entry in this method carries its invalidation at this price in some form: below the start of wave 1 for the larger count, or below the start of the inner wave 1 in a nested count. A verdict that cannot name this price is not emitted. That is his law and the desk's law at once, as the Echo Algorithm states it.

Practical handling: mark the start of wave 1 on the chart the moment you propose the count, with a horizontal line that spans the read. In Echo's WYFI master chart that line sits at $10.49 across the whole bottom of the chart in red. It is not fine print. It is the loudest risk line on the page.

### Rule 2: Wave 3 is never the shortest

Wave 3 is never the shortest of waves 1, 3 and 5. In practice it is usually the longest. Measure the three motive legs in price distance, on the scale you set in section 2.3. If wave 3 is shorter than both wave 1 and wave 5, the impulse claim ends. There is no partial credit and no relabelling wave 3 as something else to save it. Recount the structure as a correction or as a different degree.

Supporting evidence, not a substitute for the rule: wave 3 should also be the strongest leg in character, with the most volume and the momentum peak inside it. A wave 3 that passes the length rule but carries the weakest volume and no momentum peak deserves a cleanliness downgrade and a wait for more evidence, per the newsletter checklist.

### Rule 3: Wave 4 never overlaps wave 1

The wave 4 low in an advance has to stay above the wave 1 high. The wave 4 extreme stays beyond the wave 1 top. Overlap breaks the staircase character that separates an impulse from a correction.

The sole exception Echo names is diagonals, which overlap by design. He deferred diagonals to their own future issue, and the Echo Algorithm v1 does not implement them: an overlap is recorded as a rejection. Follow that discipline in your own reads. Until you are deliberately working a diagonal lesson, treat Rule 3 as absolute. Invoking the exception to rescue an ordinary overlap is stretching labels by another name.

### The cleanliness score that sits beside the rules

Passing the three rules is necessary. It is not the whole judgement. The Echo Algorithm also sub counts each leg and reports it as a cleanliness score, not a gate, because sub counting noisy real data is a judgement call: legs of an impulse should themselves break into five sub pivots, corrective legs into three. By eye, ask the same question the newsletter checklist asks: does it read cleanly, or am I forcing it? Five not three? Pullbacks smaller and quieter than the pushes? A count that passes the three rules but reads forced, with overlapping texture and equal volume on pushes and pullbacks, is a WATCH, not a SETUP. Wait for more evidence. The market usually gives another opportunity. That closing line of the impulse checklist is part of the method, not politeness.

## 5. Step four: draw Fibonacci correctly: two tools, two prices

Most Fibonacci errors are tool errors, not level errors. The Fibonacci newsletter opens with that diagnosis: people pick a random high and low, read the target off the wrong tool, and draw on the wrong scale, then wonder why price ignores their levels. The levels are fine. The drawing is the problem.

There are two tools. Never substitute one for the other.

### 5.1 The retracement tool: two clicks, measures a pullback

The retracement takes two clicks: swing low then swing high in an advance, swing high then swing low in a decline. It measures how far price pulls back against a finished move. Use it for wave 2, for wave 4, and for the correction after a full five waves complete.

Levels that matter in this method: 0.236, 0.382, 0.5, 0.618, 0.786, 0.886, and 1.0. You will not use all of them on every chart. You will use the zone that belongs to the wave you are measuring, defined in section 6.

### 5.2 The trend based extension tool: three clicks, projects the next leg

The extension takes three clicks. It projects where the next wave is heading. Use it for every wave 3, wave 5 and wave C target.

For wave 3, click in this order: start of wave 1, end of wave 1, end of wave 2. The projection is measured from the end of wave 2.

For wave 5, click: start of wave 1, end of wave 3, end of wave 4. The newsletter gives the standard and extending bands for that projection in section 6.3 below, and adds a channel cross check: connect the end of wave 2 to the end of wave 4, copy that line, and place it across the top of wave 3. Wave 5 often ends at or near the copied line. When channel and Fibonacci land in the same area, pay attention. When price blows straight through the channel, wave 5 is probably extending.

For wave C, click: start of A, end of A, end of B, by direct analogy. The extension then projects C from the end of B.

### 5.3 The worked example that fixes the distinction permanently

The newsletter card example deserves memorisation because it prices the mistake.

Take wave 1 from 100 to 150, with wave 2 holding the 0.618 at 119.10.

Read the 1.618 off the retracement tool and you get 180.90. That tool measures from the start of wave 1.

Read the 1.618 off the trend based extension and you get 200. That tool measures from the end of wave 2.

Only the second price is the wave 3 target. Sell at 180.90 and you miss the last 19.10 points of wave 3. The Echo Algorithm encodes the two tools separately for exactly this reason and repeats the warning: the two tools' 1.618s are different prices, and the algorithm never substitutes one for the other. Neither should your hand.

Rule to say aloud before you quote any target: which tool made this price, and from which anchor? If you cannot answer in one breath, the target is not ready to print.

### 5.4 Drawing discipline checklist

Run this before you trust any level. It is the newsletter checklist in working order.

1. Right tool? Retracement for pullbacks, extension for targets.
2. Wick to wick anchors, on a meaningful swing?
3. Drawn in the direction of the move?
4. Log scale if the move is long term or several hundred percent?
5. Higher timeframe drawn first?
6. Anchors respected by price since? If levels line up with nothing price has done before, check the anchors before you blame the Fibonacci.

## 6. Step five: know the zones by heart

Levels are zones, not lines. That sentence appears in the Playbook, in the Fibonacci newsletter, and in the Echo Algorithm. A Fibonacci level is an area. Price often pokes through a level before it turns. You judge the zone by the close, covered fully in section 8. First, learn which zone belongs to which wave.

### 6.1 Wave 2: the primary buy zone

Wave 2 ends between 0.5 and 0.786, most often 0.618 to 0.786. The newsletter analysis quantifies Echo's practice: about 90 percent of his wave 2 buys sit in the 0.618 to 0.786 band. The Playbook states the same hunting ground in two depths: 0.5 to 0.618 for shallow corrections, and 0.786 to 0.887 for the deep, generational bottoms he hunts. Put together, the working table is:

| Depth of wave 2 retracement | Reading | Action posture |
|---|---|---|
| Shallower than 0.5 | Not yet a wave 2 depth in this method. Suspect a B wave or an unfinished correction. See the trap screen in section 9. | No wave 2 buy. Watch. |
| 0.5 to 0.618 | Shallow end of valid. Valid if confluence and reaction agree. | Starter only on a clear reaction, size after confirmation. |
| 0.618 to 0.786 | The sweet spot. Where most wave 2 buys live. | The zone to watch hardest. Build the full confluence stack here. |
| 0.886 to 0.887 | Can still hold, but marks a weak impulse. Low conviction by definition. Echo uses both 0.886 and 0.887 in different issues for the same shelf. Treat them as one band. | Cap conviction one grade. Smaller size, demand a stronger reaction. |
| 1.0 | Invalidation. Wave 2 has retraced all of wave 1. Rule 1 is broken. | Count dies. No averaging through it. Move to the alternate count. |

Two worked anchors fix the table in memory. INDI bounced from the 0.786 wave 2 and ran plus 119 percent, a clean sweet spot case cited in the newsletter analysis. CELH, covered fully in section 11, sat on the 0.887 at $26.50 for its inner wave 2: valid while it held, deeper than his usual zone, with its real invalidation lower at the start of wave 1, $22.60. Deep is not dead. Deep is discounted conviction with a wider distance to the kill line, and position size must respect that distance.

Stops follow the same logic. On a single entry, the newsletter impulse issue places the stop below the wave 2 low. If Fibonacci levels are bunched together and you scale in across them, the stop sits below the start of wave 1, where the count dies. Choose which stop you are using before you enter, and write it on the chart. A scaling plan with a single entry stop, or a single entry with a count death stop, mixes two risk grammars and misprices both.

### 6.2 Wave 4: the shallow buy zone

Wave 4 ends between 0.236 and 0.5, most often 0.382 to 0.5. The assignment range for mastery work is 0.382 to 0.5: that is the band to watch, to draw as a box, and to quote. The wider 0.236 to 0.5 range from the newsletter is the outer envelope. A wave 4 that retraces less than 0.236 has barely corrected; a wave 4 that pushes beyond 0.5 deserves a hard second look at Rule 3 and at whether you are in a larger correction instead.

| Depth of wave 4 retracement of wave 3 | Reading |
|---|---|
| 0.236 to 0.382 | Shallow end. Common in strong trends. Valid only if it also respects Rule 3 with room to spare and shows sideways, time consuming character. |
| 0.382 to 0.5 | The sweet spot. Most wave 4 buys live here. |
| Beyond 0.5 | Warning. Recheck overlap with wave 1, recheck degree, and consider the alternate that this is not a wave 4 at all. |

MOH on the 4 hour timeframe is the newsletter's clean wave 4 receipt: a 0.382 wave 4 bounce that ran plus 40 percent. CELH's larger wave 4 held the 0.382 as well. Shallow does not mean weak in wave 4. It means normal. Demanding wave 2 depth from a wave 4 is a category error that will make you miss the continuation entries this method is built to catch, and alternation from section 3.3 should already have warned you that wave 4 would look different from wave 2.

Wave 4 entries obey the same laws as wave 2 entries in every other respect: confluence minimum, buy the reaction, close decides, invalidation named. The invalidation logic differs only in its anchor: a wave 4 that closes into wave 1 territory has broken Rule 3, and the count that justified the entry no longer exists.

### 6.3 Extension targets: wave 3, wave 5, and wave C

Have the target marked before you enter. That is a direct instruction in the Fibonacci newsletter, and it is also the templated chart grammar in section 10: no call is complete without its target ladder printed.

Wave 3, measured with the trend based extension from start of 1, end of 1, end of 2:

1. 1.272 is the stall point. A moderate wave 3 can pause or stall there. Do not treat arrival at 1.272 as failure, and do not treat it as completion without a reaction that says so.
2. 1.618 is the primary target. This is the objective to print on the chart and to compute risk against.
3. 2.0 to 2.618 is extending. If price runs that far with momentum, wave 3 is extending, and you should also check you are counting the right degree, because extended thirds subdivide and invite the premature top call described in section 3.2. WYFI's subwave 3 ran to 2.618. That is the receipt for the top of the band.

Wave 5, measured with the extension from start of 1, end of 3, end of 4:

1. 0.618 to 1.0 is the standard band.
2. 1.141 to 1.414 is the extending band.
3. After an extended wave 3, expect waves 1 and 5 toward equality, so the lower part of the standard band deserves the most weight, and a failure to make a new high, the truncated fifth, is a live possibility to plan for rather than a surprise.

Wave C in a correction, measured with the extension from start of A, end of A, end of B:

1. 1.0 extension of A is the standard zigzag C target.
2. 1.618 is the extending C target.
3. In flats, B decides the family before C is projected. Regular flat: B ends close to but not past the start of A. Expanded flat: B travels past the start of A by 1.05 to 1.38 times A, and C tends toward about 1.618. Running flat: B travels past the start of A but C fails to reach the A low. Echo's note on a running flat read is blunt: recheck your count. TE in section 11 is the expanded flat worked live.

Triangle thrust, for completeness: when a triangle completes in wave 4 or wave B position, the thrust target is the widest part of the triangle, the A to B span, projected from the breakout. Triangles are five overlapping three leg swings contracting in range. Position matters: triangles live in wave 4 or B, never as a wave 2 in this teaching, and that positional check is itself a count check.

Post cycle corrections use the retracement tool across the whole 1 to 5 move. Echo's favourite confluence construction, detailed next, starts there: draw the retracement across the full impulse, mark 0.382, 0.5 and 0.618, then wait for A and B to form and project C into those shelves.

## 7. Never call wave 1: the patience rule that protects everything else

Most people lose money with Elliott Wave right here. They see a bounce off a low, label it wave 1, and buy it. Then it turns out to be a wave B. That paragraph opens the closing teaching of the impulse newsletter, and the rule that follows is absolute in Echo practice: never call wave 1 while it forms.

The procedure instead:

1. Let wave 1 complete. Five clean waves up, ticking every box in section 3.1, with the three rules intact.
2. Wait for wave 2 to pull back in three waves, ideally into the 0.618 to 0.786 zone.
3. Buy the reaction at support, not the level itself.

You give up wave 1 doing this. That is the price of knowing the impulse was real, and wave 3 is the bigger move anyway. The newsletter analysis phrases the same economics: most supposed new impulses on social feeds are wave B. The method would rather miss the first leg entirely than fund other people's B wave exits.

Why B waves are such an effective trap deserves its own paragraph, because you will meet them constantly. A B wave can be fast, steep, and emotionally convincing. FLNC's B was parabolic. TE's B travelled through the start of A to the 1.414 at $12.32. COIN's rally after its spring 2025 low ran through the top of its prior impulse and was later read as an expanded B. Speed, steepness, and even a new high do not convert three waves into five. Count the substructure, check the volume follow through, and check what preceded it. If a completed five wave impulse and a three wave correction do not precede your supposed wave 1, you are probably labelling the middle of a correction.

The nested form of the patience rule is Echo's best setup, the A star setup: a 1 2, 1 2. Read it from the top down. A larger wave 1 and wave 2 complete. Inside the larger wave 3, a smaller wave 1 and wave 2 complete as well. Two wave 1s and two wave 2s stacked on top of each other. The next move is wave 3 of wave 3, the strongest and fastest part of any impulse, because two degrees of motive pressure align at once. The entry is the end of the inner wave 2, with risk defined at the start of the inner wave 1, and the most powerful move on the chart still ahead. The Echo Algorithm reserves its A star grade for exactly this confirmed stack and nothing else: a completed larger 1 2 with an inner 1 2 complete and price at the inner zone. CELH in section 11 is the published open example. Do not award the label to a single 1 2, however pretty. The stack is the setup.

## 8. Step six: build confluence, then buy the reaction, not the level

A Fibonacci zone becomes a buy zone only through confluence, and becomes a trade only through a reaction. Those are two separate gates. Skipping either one is how levels get bought blind on the way through.

### 8.1 Confluence: two Fibonacci constructions plus one structural element

The minimum, stated identically across the newsletter analysis, the Fibonacci newsletter, and the Echo Algorithm, is: two Fibonacci constructions landing in the same zone, plus one structural element on top.

Fibonacci constructions means independent calculations, not two lines from the same drawing. Valid pairings include:

1. A retracement of the parent move and an extension of a sibling leg landing at the same price.
2. A retracement across the whole completed 1 to 5 move and a trend based extension projecting wave C into the same shelf. This is Echo's favourite construction after a full five waves, in his four steps: draw the whole move retracement and mark 0.382, 0.5 and 0.618; wait for A and B to form; draw the extension from start of A, end of A, end of B; where the 1.0 to 1.618 of C lands on one of those retracement levels, that is the zone.
3. A wave 2 retracement of the current degree and a retracement or extension of the next higher degree landing together. Higher timeframe Fibonacci counts double in spirit: it is both a second construction and the timeframe that wins ties.

The structural element is one of these, sitting inside or running through the same zone:

1. A moving average: the 50, the 200 daily, or the 200 weekly, with longer period weighted more. The Playbook example is CAVA: a buy zone from 0.5 at $67.47 to 0.618 at $61.74 sitting exactly on the 200 daily average. Without that average, the same Fibonacci band would have been a support test, not a buy zone, in Playbook language.
2. A trendline arriving at the band: one of your maximum two lines, through at least two prior pivots, reaching the zone at the time price reaches it.
3. Old support or resistance: a prior pivot shelf with at least two touches, or a horizontal shelf that already held a wave low inside the larger structure. The COIN case below carries a $140 to $160 shelf that had held the wave 4 low in September 2024.

Count the stack out loud and write it on the chart, one line per construction with its price. That provenance listing is how Echo's charts read and how the Echo Algorithm reports Step 4: a list, each entry with its price, not a vague claim of confluence. Scoring follows the stack:

1. Two Fibonacci constructions plus a structural element plus divergence plus a reaction is the highest conviction form short of an A star stack.
2. Zone plus reaction with a partial stack is working conviction.
3. Zone without reaction is WATCH, regardless of how beautiful the stack is.
4. A lone Fibonacci level with no second construction and no structural element is a watch level, not a zone. The Playbook says it plainly: zones without confluence are support tests, not buys. The local stock analysis skill carries the identical rule into Midna's reads.

A 0.886 deep wave 2 caps conviction one grade inside whatever the stack otherwise earns, because the impulse has already told you it is weak. Keep that cap mechanical. Do not negotiate it away because the chart looks oversold.

Zone tolerance in the Echo Algorithm is 1.5 percent: constructions within that distance of each other count as landing in the same zone. By eye, that is your fudge factor for calling a stack a stack, and also your discipline limit. If you need 4 percent to make the lines meet, they do not meet.

### 8.2 The reaction: what counts, in what order

The governing sentences deserve direct quotation in paraphrase because every word carries weight. The zone tells you where to watch. Price tells you when to act. Buy the reaction, not the level.

A zone becomes a SETUP only when price reacts at it, judged on closes. From Step 6 of the Echo Algorithm and the newsletter process laws together, a qualifying reaction is any of these, printed at the zone:

1. A bullish candle at the level: an engulfing candle or a hammer whose body and close do their work inside or directly off the zone. Bearish mirror at a resistance zone for the short or exit side.
2. A close back above the short averages, the 9 or 21 EMA in the algorithm's encoding, after touching the zone. In Playbook language the larger version of the same trigger is the 50 average reclaim: starters are allowed at the zone when confluences stack, full size goes on when the averages are reclaimed. Echo's own risk grammar states it in those two tiers: starter position acceptable at the zone retest, buys in size when the moving averages are reclaimed.
3. Volume returning on the bounce: bounce bar volume above the pullback's mean volume. The volume grammar behind it is consistent across all sources. Pullbacks should arrive on falling volume, sellers exhausting into the zone. A capitulation spike into the low counts for the setup. Bounces should arrive on returning volume. A candle shape with no volume return is a weaker reaction. Grade it down.

Supporting evidence that arms the zone before the reaction prints:

1. RSI divergence at the zone: price prints a lower low while RSI prints a higher low across the last two same direction pivots. Multi timeframe divergence outranks single timeframe. Divergence is an arming condition, never a standalone entry. The trigger remains the candle, the average reclaim, or the trendline break named in advance. One hard limit from the Echo Algorithm: if divergence breaks, meaning RSI makes the new low with price, the setup loses that evidence leg. Do not keep quoting a divergence that price and RSI have jointly cancelled.
2. A falling corrective trendline break. In CELH the trigger Echo wanted next was the break of the falling trendline drawn from the wave 1 high. When the zone, the stack, and the trendline all sit in one place, the close through the line plus the Fibonacci horizontal is the confirmation event, in Playbook trendline language.
3. Time symmetry that says the correction has had long enough. Section 9 covers the too quick trap. The positive form is simpler: a correction whose duration is proportionate to its impulse has earned the right to be judged complete when its other evidence agrees. A correction that is far too short has not, whatever its price level.

Position handling inside the reaction law is two tiered and should be written before entry. Tier one: a small starter at the zone is allowed when confluences stack, even before the full reaction confirms, because the zone itself was built to a minimum stack. Tier two: size goes on after confirmation, meaning the qualifying candle, the average reclaim, or the trendline close. If you take tier one, your stop still lives at the pre stated invalidation, so tier one size must be sized to that full distance, not to a hopeful tighter stop you have not earned yet.

### 8.3 Close, not wick, decides

Levels are zones, and the close decides. A wick through a level does not kill it. A close through the bottom of the zone does. That law repeats verbatim across the newsletter issues, the explainer cards, and Step 6 of the Echo Algorithm, and the coral zones card in the newsletter draws it literally: a candle wicking through the shaded sweet spot band and closing back inside it, captioned in plain words.

Apply it in both directions with the same strictness.

For survival: price may poke through the 0.618, through the 0.786, even to the bottom edge of your drawn box intraday. If the candle closes back inside the zone, the zone held. COIN wicked below its 0.618 into the bottom of its zone and then turned. That wick was the test, not the failure.

For death: a close below the zone's bottom, and in the absolute case a close at or through the 1.0 retracement, the start of wave 1, ends the count that the zone belonged to. Do not rescue a closing break by pointing at a wick recovery two days later and pretending the close never printed. The count died on the close. A later reclaim is a new structure to be counted fresh, possibly the alternate count taking over, which is exactly why the alternate was drawn in advance.

The same close rule governs trendlines and averages in this method. A close through the corrective trendline plus the Fibonacci horizontal confirms. An intraday poke through the 50 average that closes back below it has not reclaimed anything. Wick literacy is not pedantry here. It is the difference between COIN's plus 213 percent turn and a stopped out account that sold the exact low.

## 9. Step seven: run the trap screen and the time check

Before any post impulse continuation is trusted, run the WYFI screen. It comes from Echo's dissection of a failed bull flag on WYFI in newsletter issue 3, encoded as Step 7 of the Echo Algorithm with his thresholds. Run it against any apparent flag or shallow pullback that claims to be a wave 2 or wave 4 continuation. Each sign is a named detector.

1. TOO SHALLOW. The pullback retraced less than 0.5 of wave 1 where a wave 2 is claimed. WYFI fired at 0.382. A wave 2 depth problem is not cured by calling the shape a flag.
2. VOLUME WRONG. Pullback mean volume at or above impulse mean volume, or sell leg volume persistently above buy leg volume inside the pullback. Selling volume rising through a bull flag unmakes the flag.
3. NO THREE WAVES. The pullback does not segment into a three leg structure. A wave 2 or a wave A to C correction has a countable three wave texture. A single vertical drop or a five wave drop tells a different story.
4. TOO QUICK. Pullback duration less than 25 percent of the impulse duration. WYFI fired at 15 days against an 87 day impulse, about 17 percent. Time is evidence. A correction that is over too fast probably is not over.

Scoring: two or more signs means TRAP in the algorithm's vocabulary. The expected path is a B wave rejection, so watch the 0.5 to 0.786 retrace of the pullback itself for the rejection, and a C leg to follow. One sign is a warning to demand more from the reaction gate. Zero signs does not prove the flag. It clears the screen so the ordinary gates, rules, confluence, reaction, can judge it.

Add the two broader fake impulse checks from the newsletter that the screen does not cover. First, position: is your supposed new impulse actually sitting where a B wave lives, meaning after a completed A down and inside a larger correction that has not reached its own Fibonacci zone yet? Second, character under speed: parabolic, overlapping, three wave texture at speed is a B wave until proven otherwise, per FLNC and TE. Parabolic B waves pay well and start nothing, so trading one as a breakout with continuation targets misprices both the target and the stop.

## 10. Step eight: draw the templated chart and write the call

A method you cannot draw the same way twice is not yet a method. Echo's charts share one grammar across the X corpus of 240 charts confirmed in the study addendum, the 134 chart Playbook corpus, and the newsletter evidence charts. Learn the grammar as a template and use it whole. It has four layers: the canvas, the drawings, the labels, and the written call that mirrors them.

### 10.1 The canvas

Dark TradingView style canvas. Price pane carries the argument. Volume sits where it can falsify the call. RSI appears mainly at pattern extremes, where divergence is part of the evidence, not as permanent decoration. The right hand side of the canvas is reserved empty future space. That reservation is the signature of the whole grammar: the forecast lives in the void, pre labelled at its future prices, so the reader sees where the count expects price to go before price goes there. INDI's future 3 and 4, CELH's future waves in parentheses, TE's circled target wave, all sit in empty space on their published charts. Reserve the space on your chart before you draw the past. If the future will not fit, your chart window is wrong, not your forecast.

### 10.2 The drawings: what marks go on, and no others

1. Elliott labels at the pivots, in two degrees. Circled numerals for the primary degree, plain numerals and letters for the sub degree. Labels sit at the swing extremes they name, never floating mid leg. Two degrees maximum on one readable chart. A third degree belongs on a second chart at a different timeframe.
2. Fibonacci levels as short horizontal lines or as a shaded zone box, each level labelled with ratio and price together: 0.786 with its price in parentheses is the house style from the verified X exemplar onward. A level is never a bare line. It always carries its number, so the reader can audit the construction without trusting the drawer.
3. The buy zone as a rectangle spanning the sweet spot band, from the top of the zone to its bottom, extended into the future space until its expiry or its reaction. Boxes, not lines, because zones are areas. The Playbook is explicit: buy zones and target zones are drawn as rectangles, either a Fibonacci band or a measured target box.
4. The invalidation as a full width horizontal line at the start of wave 1, in the danger colour, labelled as the price where the count dies. WYFI's $10.49 line spanning the whole chart bottom is the standard. If your invalidation line is subtle, you have drawn your risk dishonestly.
5. At most two trendlines, cyan in Echo's evidence charts, anchored as section 2.4 prescribes. One of them is usually the corrective trendline whose break becomes a named trigger.
6. The 50 and 200 averages as geography curves, in Max's shared template green for the 50 and red for the 200, present when they are part of the confluence stack or the trigger, absent when they would only decorate.
7. A measured move arrow for the target, white in the primary count, with the percentage to target printed beside it, terminating at the extension line in the reserved space: 1.618 with its price, and the percent move next to it. Echo prints losses and expectations in the same style, including the minus 59.08 percent realised on WYFI and the plus 406.28 percent to the WYFI wave 3 target. The percent is part of the record, not marketing copy pasted on winners only.
8. The alternate count in the second colour, orange or red, with its own terminus box visible. Circles marking the exact evidence points, on the price pane and at the same horizontal position on the volume pane where volume is the evidence, as on RDW's retest and WYFI's climax volume in the newsletter images.
9. A dashed expected path from the present into the future pivots. Dashed matters. In the Best Setups chart law the expected path is drawn dashed into reserved future space, visually subordinate to traded price, so the future reads as a plan and never impersonates history. Echo's own explainer card for the 1 2, 1 2 draws the same convention: the expected path dashed from a circled A star entry, with the dashed floor labelled start of wave 1, where you are wrong.

If a mark is not on this list, justify it or delete it. Sparse is not minimalism for taste. Sparse is what makes the five second extraction in section 10.4 possible.

### 10.3 The written call: ticker, verdict, zone, target

Every chart ships with a written call in a fixed template. The template has four required fields in this order, plus two required companions. This is the templated grammar the playbook as a whole standardises, fusing Echo's conditional verdict line with the desk requirement that entry zone, invalidation, and target all print at their prices.

Field 1, Ticker and frame. Cashtag or ticker, timeframe, and cycle position in one breath. Example form: TICKER, weekly, larger wave 2 complete, inner wave 2 at its zone inside wave 3. The cycle position is not optional throat clearing. It is the claim the rest of the call stands on.

Field 2, Verdict. One conditional sentence, level named, patience explicit when patience is the call. House forms from the Playbook: losing a named price confirms the move to the named buy zone; flipping the 50 average at a named price is the low risk buy; price must hold the named zone or the named lower shelf follows. When there is no trade, the verdict says so in the same grammar: corrective, not buy time, or WATCH, zone identified, no reaction yet. The Echo Algorithm output vocabulary is the controlled list for this field: A star for the confirmed 1 2, 1 2 stack, SETUP for zone plus reaction, WATCH for zone without reaction, TRAP for two or more WYFI signs, NO TRADE when no zone survives the gates. Use the controlled word. Do not improvise synonyms that blur which gate was passed.

Field 3, Zone. The buy zone as a price range with its construction listed: top and bottom prices, the two Fibonacci constructions with their ratios and prices, and the structural element with its price. Add the reaction state in the same field: no reaction yet, starter taken at the zone, or confirmed by the named candle, average reclaim, or trendline close, with the trigger price. A zone quoted without its stack is an opinion. A zone quoted with its stack is an auditable location.

Field 4, Target. The target ladder with its tool named: stall watch at the 1.272 extension with its price, primary objective at the 1.618 extension with its price and the percent move to it, extending case at 2.0 to 2.618 with its price when the degree supports it. For wave 5 calls, the ladder uses the wave 5 bands from section 6.3 instead, and says so. Risk companions print immediately after the target, in the same block, never in a footnote: invalidation as a closing price, start of wave 1 with its price, and R computed to the stall point and to the primary objective. Echo prints percent to target; desk law prints R as well; both are carried, as the Echo Algorithm notes.

Companion 1, Alternate. One or two sentences: the alternate count, its trigger, and its terminus price. If price closes through invalidation, the alternate is the working count from that close onward, with its own zone already identified where the structure allows it. WYFI's published management is the model: three scenarios, each with its own action, printed before resolution.

Companion 2, Checklist result. Name any checklist row that failed or sits borderline, by name. Section 12 supplies the checklist. A call that prints only its passes is hiding its risk in prose. The failing row named is what lets a future review grade the call fairly: was the weakness known and priced, or discovered afterwards?

Write the call before you consider the trade live. If any field cannot be filled with a price, the call is not ready, and the correct verdict is WATCH or NO TRADE with the missing field named as the reason.

### 10.4 The five second test

Echo's grammar is built so a reader extracts the whole read in about five seconds: where we are in the count from the labels at the pivots, the two prices that matter now from the labelled Fibonacci lines, and the one destination from the arrow and percent at the top right of the future space. Test your finished chart against that extraction, in order. Cover the text. Can a fresh reader say the cycle position, the zone, and the target without hunting? If she has to hunt, the drawing failed, however correct the analysis underneath it is. Fix the drawing: enlarge the zone box label, move the target percent next to its line, delete the third trendline, clear the indicator that carries no evidence. Analysis that cannot be extracted will not be acted on correctly under pressure, including by its own author a week later.

## 12. The full execution sequence

Execute in this order on every new wave read. The sequence merges the Echo Algorithm's nine steps with the newsletter checklists and the drawing grammar above. Do not reorder the gates. Confluence before reaction, reaction before size, invalidation before target enthusiasm.

Step 1, Frame. Higher timeframe first. State timeframe, scale, and regime in one line: weekly governing, daily timing, log scale if the move demands it. Mark the 50 and 200 averages and the prior shelves on the bare chart.

Step 2, Swings. Identify structure pivots, wick to wick, spanning the pattern. Name the candidate swing you will measure. If no single swing dominates the chart, stop: there is no count to build yet.

Step 3, Structure call. Impulse or correction, with the texture evidence from section 3.1 in plain words: five or three, clean or forced, volume on pushes or not, momentum peak located. Classify a correction by the checklist order from the Echo Algorithm: A's substructure first, five points to zigzag family, three points to flat or triangle family; then B's retrace band; then contraction for triangles; then relabel to W X Y if a completed A B C keeps correcting instead of forcing C to be terminal.

Step 4, Rules gate. Test Rule 1, Rule 2, Rule 3 in writing, pass or fail each. Any failure ends the impulse claim. Record the cleanliness score beside the gate: substructure counts, overlap texture, volume relationship. Borderline cleanliness with passing rules routes to WATCH.

Step 5, Fibonacci construction. Draw the retracement on the wave being corrected and the extension on the wave being targeted, with the correct tools and anchors from section 5. Read the zone table for the wave in question from section 6. Write every price with its ratio.

Step 6, Confluence stack. Count independent constructions inside the zone tolerance and list them with prices. Require two Fibonacci constructions plus one structural element for a high probability zone. Apply the 0.886 depth cap where it belongs. Below minimum, the zone is a watch level. Say so.

Step 7, Evidence and trap screen. Record divergence state at the zone, volume grammar into the zone, time proportion against the impulse, alternation expectation, and the four WYFI signs with a count. Two or more trap signs route the whole read to TRAP handling regardless of the stack.

Step 8, Reaction gate. Has price reacted at the zone on a closing basis, by candle, average reclaim, or trendline close, with bounce volume above pullback mean? Yes routes to SETUP handling with the two tier size plan. No routes to WATCH with the exact trigger named and priced: the candle form awaited, the average and its price, or the trendline and its current price. A wick through the zone neither promotes nor kills the read. A close through the zone bottom kills the zone; a close through the 1.0 kills the count.

Step 9, Call and chart. Fill the four field template from section 10.3, draw the chart to the grammar in section 10.2, run the five second test, and grade conviction from the stack using the Echo Algorithm grades: A star only for the confirmed stack, high for two plus Fibonacci confluences with divergence and reaction, working conviction for zone plus reaction with a partial stack, WATCH for zone without reaction. A verdict that cannot name its invalidation is not emitted.

Calibration, in the mastery sense, is what you do with finished calls. Keep the count as drawn, with its zone, stack, trigger, invalidation and target ladder frozen at publication. When the call resolves, grade it against its own geometry first: did price reach the zone, did the reaction form that was named, did the close rule save or end it correctly, did the target ladder's stall point and primary objective behave as levels? Then grade the process separately from the outcome: a call that followed every gate and stopped out at its printed invalidation is a correct execution with a losing outcome, and the lesson it teaches is about the stack or the degree, not about discipline. A call that made money after skipping the reaction gate is a process failure with a winning outcome, and it is the more dangerous of the two records, because it trains the skip. Review in that order, geometry first, process second, outcome last, and let each review change exactly one habit at a time so you can tell what the change did. That is how a master checks and improves her own reads inside this method, using Echo's own receipts habit as the model: he embeds his earlier dated, priced posts, including the losses, inside the later teaching. Your archive should be able to embarrass you in the same useful way.

## 13. The checklist, printed whole

Tape this to the chart. It merges Echo's three published checklists, impulse, correction, and Fibonacci, into one page in execution order.

Impulse:

1. Five waves, not three?
2. Does it read cleanly, or am I forcing it?
3. Did wave 2 stay above the start of wave 1?
4. Is wave 3 longer than wave 1 or wave 5, and never the shortest of the three?
5. Did wave 4 stay above the wave 1 high?
6. Is wave 3 the strongest leg, with the most volume and the momentum peak inside it?
7. Are the pullbacks smaller and quieter than the pushes?
8. Did wave 2 end between 0.618 and 0.786, or if deeper, have I capped conviction for 0.886 and priced the stop to the start of wave 1?
9. Did wave 3 reach the 1.618 or beyond, with 1.272 respected as the stall point on the way?
10. Did wave 4 end between 0.382 and 0.5?
11. Has wave 1 actually finished, or am I calling it early?

Correction:

1. Is A five or three?
2. B sits in which band: 0.5 to 0.786 for a zigzag, near the start of A for a regular flat, 1.05 to 1.38 past it for an expanded flat, past it with C failing for a running flat and a recount warning?
3. C projects where, at 1.0 or 1.618 of A by the extension tool?
4. Is the shape contracting across five overlapping threes, which would make it a triangle, and is its position legal, wave 4 or B?
5. Is the position, wave 2 against wave 4, consistent with alternation?

Fibonacci:

1. Right tool? Retracement for pullbacks, trend based extension for targets, and can I name which tool made each price?
2. Wick to wick anchors, on a meaningful swing, drawn in the direction of the move?
3. Log scale for a long term move, higher timeframe drawn first?
4. Levels treated as zones, judged by the close?
5. At least two Fibonacci levels stacked in the same zone?
6. A moving average, trendline, or old support there too?
7. Am I waiting for the reaction, or buying the level blind?
8. Is the invalidation printed at the start of wave 1, and the target ladder printed before entry?

Any rule row failing ends the claim that row protects. Rows that merely sit borderline route the call to WATCH until more evidence prints. That routing is the method working, not the method failing.

## 14. Common execution faults and their corrections

Fault 1: Reading the target off the retracement tool. Correction: rebuild the target with the three click extension from the end of wave 2, and reprint the ladder. The 100 to 150 example in section 5.3 is the permanent reference: 180.90 is the wrong tool's price, 200 is the target.

Fault 2: Anchoring to bodies or to the nearest convenient wiggle. Correction: redraw wick to wick on structure pivots only, then audit by respect. Levels that line up with nothing price has done are anchor errors until proven otherwise.

Fault 3: Calling a zone on one Fibonacci line. Correction: add the second construction or downgrade the language to watch level. The word zone is earned by the stack.

Fault 4: Buying the first touch of the zone with full size. Correction: restore the two tiers. Starter at the zone only when the stack meets minimum. Size on the reaction: candle, average reclaim, or trendline close, with returning volume.

Fault 5: Selling the wick. Correction: wait for the close. A wick through the zone that closes back inside it is the zone holding under test, as COIN demonstrated at its 0.618.

Fault 6: Defending a count through its invalidation with a relabel invented after the close. Correction: the count died on the close through the start of wave 1. Promote the pre drawn alternate, count it fresh through every gate in section 12, and log the dead count as drawn. Relabelling has a legitimate place in this method: before resolution, as the published alternate, and after a kill, as a fresh count. Its illegitimate place is as a shield for a count that already broke its own rules.

Fault 7: Demanding the same depth from every wave. Correction: return to the zone tables. Wave 2 lives at 0.618 to 0.786. Wave 4 lives at 0.382 to 0.5. A wave 4 hunted at wave 2 depth will be hunted below wave 1 territory, where Rule 3 has already ended the story.

Fault 8: Drawing the past beautifully and the future not at all. Correction: reserve the right hand space first, place the future labels, the extension line, the percent arrow, and the dashed expected path, then check the five second test. The future is the product. The past is its evidence.

## Closing standard
